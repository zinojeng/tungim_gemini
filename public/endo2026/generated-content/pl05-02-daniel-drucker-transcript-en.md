He is being honored for his role as one of the pioneers in advancing next-generation treatments for diabetes and weight loss. He has contributed greatly over the years to the development of a new class of drugs, including Ozempic and Wegovy, based on his discoveries involving the gut hormones known as glucagon-like peptides, GLP-1 and GLP-2. These medications have been life-changing for people living with type 2 diabetes, obesity, and intestinal failure.

Dr. Drucker has served the Endocrine Society in various roles on numerous committees, most recently as an editorial board member for the *Journal of the Endocrine Society*, as editor-in-chief of *Endocrine Reviews*, and as a member of the Society’s Nominating Committee. Please welcome to the stage Dr. Daniel Drucker.

## Origins of GLP-1 research

**Daniel Drucker:** Good morning, everybody. It is great to be here, and let me extend my personal invitation for those of you coming to Toronto next year. If you need any help with any arrangements, just call me. I will help you.

Let us get going. These are my disclosures. I have worked with a number of companies over the years. Some of them support basic-science studies in my lab. As they get to know me and my personality, generally the number of companies becomes fewer over time, which is totally understandable.

How did I get started? As some of you know, I went to Massachusetts General Hospital in 1984 to do thyroid research. I was told I could not do that. Bill Chin was taking the thyroid project to Brigham and Women’s Hospital, and I got stuck with what was, at the time, this obscure project.

We showed that GLP-1 stimulates insulin secretion and cyclic AMP. It was glucose-dependent, and the effect occurred only with the short form of GLP-1. Yes, we thought that perhaps this might have some relevance to the treatment of type 2 diabetes. Today, we are much more excited about the clinical implications of these studies.

About 10 years later, in January 1996, Steve Bloom showed that GLP-1 reduces food intake in rats. Several months later, we similarly showed in mice that this was conserved biology. Notice that in GLP-1 receptor-knockout mice, we lost this effect completely.

The editors of *Nature* were not happy with us because the GLP-1 receptor-knockout mice did not eat more and were not obese. They rejected us and sent us to this obscure journal at the time, *Nature Medicine*. Today, we know that we do not see syndromic obesity in young children or adults with inactivating mutations in the GLP-1 receptor. The mice really were correct in terms of translational biology.

## Expanding clinical use of GLP-1 medicines

Today, I can fit these medicines on one slide. Every year going forward, we will have difficulty fitting the medicines on one slide unless we make them very, very small, because we will probably see two to three new medicines approved every year. There is a tremendous amount of innovation and, hence, an opportunity for members of the Endocrine Society to determine how we should use these medicines widely to improve human health.

This slide summarizes what we have learned over decades from preclinical studies, and it never ceases to amaze me. If you look at the arrows directionally highlighting the biology, they are all favorable for reducing cardiometabolic disease. It would not take more than one arrow, right? If I flipped the blood-pressure arrow and made it indicate increased blood pressure, I would not be giving the plenary lecture here today because these medicines would be harmful to people.

Even more amazingly, as someone who runs a mouse lab and is extremely skeptical of all our mouse data in terms of translational impact, when we look at human phase 3 clinical trials for many of the same conditions that we studied in mice, we see positive benefits from these medicines. That never ceases to amaze me, and we are going to see more and more indications studied, which we will discuss going forward.

Around 2018 and 2019, we started to get the early results from the semaglutide program. Remember, liraglutide was approved for weight loss in 2014, but there was no celebrity culture or amazing public awareness of liraglutide. When these data started to emerge, we really saw a surge in attention.

Then, sadly, we had COVID-19 and did not see one another for about a year, until the vaccines became available. You then started seeing friends and family members after a hiatus, and some of them looked pretty good. Was this an opportunity to double down on lifestyle during the COVID-19 pandemic, or was there a little help from semaglutide? It was probably a little bit of both, depending on the individual.

A few years later, we got tirzepatide, which is even more effective. We now have GLP-1 and GIP together in one peptide, and it is about 25% more effective for glucose control or weight loss in people. Some people think it is also a little better tolerated. GIP has an antiemetic action, which is conserved in humans as well. There is a great deal of exciting innovation with clinical impact.

## Pediatric diabetes and obesity

Many of you are pediatricians and are aware of how difficult it is to treat young people with type 2 diabetes and those living with obesity. We have seen many studies struggle to achieve even a 0.5-percentage-point reduction in HbA1c over time, and weight loss is very difficult.

If you look at the semaglutide data from STEP TEENS, we see very robust results in this patient population. Equally amazing to me are the SURPASS-PEDS data with tirzepatide in this population, showing double-digit weight loss. If you look at HbA1c control, we are basically normalizing glucose control—something that we have been unable to do with previous regimens in this patient population.

## Gastrointestinal symptoms and the mechanism of weight loss

How does this work? I hear many people say, “Of course these medicines work because they make you feel sick all the time. You are walking around with an airplane airsickness bag, and no wonder you do not eat and you lose weight.” That can happen rarely, early after the initiation of therapy or during dose escalation.

Skilled clinicians will tell you that they rarely encounter patients with gastrointestinal side effects because they start at doses even lower than those required in clinical trials. They escalate much more slowly and do not increase the dose until patients are symptom-free.

If you look at the clinical-trial programs with either semaglutide or tirzepatide, what is plotted here is the presence or absence at any time over a year and a half of NVD: nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea. Even among people who never once reported nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea—and think of this in real-world terms: how many of you could honestly say that, over the last year and a half, you never once had a little nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea? All the restaurants you went to were amazing? All your uncles’ barbecues were fantastic?—about 90% of the weight loss is achieved in people who never reported a gastrointestinal side effect. Visceral illness is not what drives the weight loss.

## Discontinuation and weight regain

Another point of discussion is what happens when people discontinue these medicines. We know that many people discontinue them after one year. Adherence is not great, and this is a major challenge and opportunity for the field.

People say, “You gain back all the weight. You gain back even more weight. Instead of becoming an endocrinologist, your children become orthopedic surgeons, and your life just unravels.” What the SURMOUNT-4 trial actually teaches us is that people who received tirzepatide for 36 weeks and then stopped had no drug for a year. Of those individuals, 46% still kept off at least 10% of their weight, and 25% still kept off at least 15% of their weight.

Yes, most people slowly but surely gain weight over time after cessation, but there is tremendous interindividual heterogeneity, both in the initial weight loss and in weight regain. Some people do not lose any weight with these medicines, and we do not understand why. Some people regain a great deal of weight quickly, whereas others do not. This is a tremendously important research opportunity for the community.

One might say that, surely, in the era of genomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and clinical assessments, we should be able to develop predictive scores that distinguish responders from nonresponders. Hopefully this will happen, but so far we have not been successful.

Here is a fairly large study of almost 11,000 people across multiple biobanks, examining weight loss after either metabolic surgery or GLP-1 medicines. Genetics does not help us predict the extent of weight loss or weight regain. It may identify a few people with rare mutations associated with perhaps a 1% difference in weight loss, but so far we have not been able to figure this out.

## Cardiovascular benefits beyond weight loss

Why are we so interested in GLP-1 medicines beyond glucose control and weight loss? They seem to do many other beneficial things. We have been very interested in understanding whether these are indirect benefits reflecting glucose control or weight loss, or whether there is something else about the “magic” of GLP-1 that produces these benefits.

Here is a typical mouse study that we did in our lab about 16 years ago with Mansoor Husain in Toronto. If we give liraglutide to a mouse with a myocardial infarction, it has better survival and preservation of cardiac function, whether or not the mouse has diabetes and whether or not the mouse loses weight, because we conduct the experiment with pair-feeding.

This was in 2009, when we did not have any cardiovascular outcome trials. Then, in 2016, we got the LEADER trial with liraglutide, showing reductions in heart attacks, strokes, and death.

We also got an interesting trial with albiglutide, which was marketed by GSK. It was a long-acting albumin–GLP-1 fusion protein and was withdrawn from the market because it was commercially unable to compete with liraglutide or dulaglutide. However, GSK continued the cardiovascular outcome study, Harmony Outcomes. Here was a medicine that, over two years, produced less than 1 kg of weight loss, yet there was a 22% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events, driven by a reduction in myocardial infarction. This really started to teach us that glucose control and weight loss were not driving these cardiovascular benefits.

You can conduct a post hoc analysis of virtually any cardiovascular outcome trial in this field. Here is a post hoc analysis of SUSTAIN-6 with semaglutide in people with type 2 diabetes. It does not matter whether you lose weight; you still receive the cardiovascular benefits.

That is type 2 diabetes. We see the same thing in people living with overweight or obesity and cardiovascular disease in the SELECT trial. Notice how quickly these curves diverge, showing a cardiovascular benefit from semaglutide well before there is any weight loss. We therefore have to appreciate that some benefits in some disorders are truly independent of weight loss.

We are also grateful that we now have similarly robust cardioprotection with tirzepatide in the SURPASS-CVOT trial.

Another striking phase 3 trial was STRIDE. These are people with peripheral artery disease who have claudication—pain when they walk. After a year of semaglutide, they experience a benefit, with greater walking distance and less pain.

How does this happen? We have extensive animal data suggesting a reduction in atherosclerosis, so we try to understand this in the lab. Most aortic endothelial cells are GLP-1 receptor-positive. We can eliminate those receptors using mouse genetics, yet we still reduce atherosclerosis in mice despite virtual ablation of most GLP-1 receptors in the mouse aorta. We are trying to extend this work and understand whether rare populations of immune cells or vascular smooth muscle cells are involved. This work is ongoing.

## Kidney protection and blood pressure

We have also seen striking protection of the kidney in people with type 2 diabetes. The FLOW trial with semaglutide was stopped early because of clear protection in the semaglutide arm. The question is: how does this happen?

There are not many GLP-1 receptors in the kidney. Some vascular smooth muscle cells in afferent arterioles are GLP-1 receptor-positive, but most of the glomerulus, epithelia, and tubular cells do not express the receptor.

What do these vascular smooth muscle GLP-1 receptors do? They are not responsible for all the effects of GLP-1 medicines in the kidney. We have knocked them out in mice. We lose the reduction in blood pressure when we knock out this vascular smooth muscle GLP-1 receptor, but we do not really lose the other effects—the diuresis, natriuresis, and some of the kidney protection. It therefore remains a mystery how the kidney benefit occurs, and I think much of it is also independent of weight loss.

Is the blood-pressure effect independent of weight loss? We know that the reduction in blood pressure will obviously be greater in people who lose weight, but look again at some of the clinical data. Here is tirzepatide in the SURMOUNT-1 trial. Look at the first time we measure blood pressure, a couple of weeks after starting tirzepatide, when there has been no meaningful weight loss. We are just starting at the lowest dose, and barely a couple of weeks later we already see a 4 mm Hg drop in blood pressure.

Yes, weight loss is beneficial for almost all the diseases that we treat, but when we look carefully, there is often a weight-loss-independent component.

## Metabolic liver disease

What about metabolic liver disease? From the ESSENCE trial, we know that semaglutide is now approved in the United States for this purpose. It was tested at 2.4 mg once weekly in people with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, or MASH.

It is very logical to state that weight loss is tremendously beneficial for the liver. It clears fat from the liver, reduces inflammation, and, in turn, reduces fibrosis. Is the entire benefit therefore reflective of weight loss?

We have examined this very carefully. In mice, we can prevent weight loss by either pair-feeding or knocking out GLP-1 receptors in the brain. When we knock out GLP-1 receptors in the brain and treat the mice with semaglutide, we still obtain virtually all the benefits in the mouse liver, even though there is no weight loss.

We can also conduct the experiment another way. We can allow the mouse to lose 15% or 20% of its body weight, but if we knock out GLP-1 receptors in rare populations in the liver, we lose the metabolic benefits in the liver despite tremendous weight loss.

This is because a population of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells expressing the GLP-1 receptor is tremendously important. These endothelial cells send signals to virtually every other cell type in the liver to reduce inflammation, reduce fat synthesis, and improve liver health.

My skeptical translational response to our own data is: “Sure, Dan, that is very nice for your mice. Congratulations. Is it relevant to humans treated with these medicines?” We have several glimpses from post hoc analyses of the ESSENCE trial. Whether you look at reductions in inflammation or fibrosis, it does not matter whether or not the patients lose weight. You can see the same pattern for liver stiffness, liver enzymes, and many other important parameters.

We are starting to learn that, for some conditions in which we use these medicines, mechanical unloading and weight loss are tremendously important and drive the benefit. For others—particularly heart, liver, and kidney disease, as well as some additional conditions—weight loss is probably not the driver of the benefit.

## Osteoarthritis, sleep apnea, and airway disease

Obviously, if you have knee or hip osteoarthritis—and knee osteoarthritis was studied in the STEP 9 trial—losing weight will logically be tremendously helpful.

What we do not yet have is a post hoc analysis from that trial, or from the trial with the remarkable investigational medicine retatrutide, in which people are losing a tremendous amount of weight and experiencing major clinical benefits for osteoarthritis. Is there a weight-loss-independent component to the benefit? In mice, you can see weight-loss-independent reductions in inflammation in experimental arthritis, but we do not yet have the post hoc analyses from these clinical trials.

The benefit in obstructive sleep apnea, which is now an approved indication for tirzepatide, is logically probably a consequence of weight loss. We have no evidence of weight-loss-independent benefits in obstructive sleep apnea. However, trials of semaglutide and tirzepatide are underway in people with allergic airway disease to determine whether inflammation might be reduced in a weight-loss-independent manner.

## New formulations and next-generation medicines

In just the last six months, new medicines and new doses have been introduced, particularly in the United States. We now have higher doses of semaglutide that achieve what we might call tirzepatide-like weight loss.

The debate then becomes: why would you choose one rather than the other? It depends on the individual in front of you and that person’s goals. If the only goal is maximal weight loss, tirzepatide is an amazing drug. By virtue of its earlier start in the field, however, semaglutide has much more outcomes data.

If the person in front of you is living with liver disease, kidney disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and other comorbidities, this does not mean tirzepatide would not be an amazing drug. We simply have better and more extensive outcomes data for semaglutide at present.

We also now have this new higher-dose oral formulation of Wegovy. It is 25 mg of the same semaglutide with which we are familiar, with a slightly different formulation from that originally used for the 7-mg and 14-mg doses in type 2 diabetes. One pill a day—as all your NBA superstars advertising it on television every day will tell you—produces about the same weight loss as injectable semaglutide 2.4 mg.

We like to have options in the clinic. Some people prefer injections, whereas others prefer oral tablets. The caveat with oral semaglutide is the need to avoid eating and drinking for at least 30 minutes after taking the tablet.

The primary differentiation so far for orforglipron, a new medicine developed by Chugai and in partnership with Eli Lilly, is that it is a small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist. It is also approved in the United States for weight loss and has no food or drink restrictions. You can take it without regard to whether you want to have your coffee, for example. It is also highly effective and, I think, will be a very promising additional medicine in our toolbox.

## GIP receptor agonism and antagonism

Tirzepatide activates both the GIP receptor and the GLP-1 receptor. However, Amgen is developing an investigational medicine, maridebart cafraglutide, or MariTide, that uses an antibody to block the GIP receptor while activating the GLP-1 receptor. One might logically ask: what is going on? How can one remarkable medicine activate the receptor while another—whose phase 2 data look quite good—blocks it?

Remember that human genetics examining variation around the GIP receptor shows that loss of GIP receptor signaling in humans is associated with favorable cardiometabolic traits, including reduced BMI and cardiovascular disease.

We also know that when we give GIP alone to either animals or humans—and we have human data—animals and humans lose weight with GIP receptor agonism alone. It therefore makes sense that, when you add GIP receptor agonism to GLP-1 receptor agonism in a molecule such as tirzepatide, you obtain an effective medicine.

It turns out, however, that if you block GIP receptors in some neuronal populations, you sensitize the actions of GLP-1. We now have very good pharmacological and genetic evidence that loss of GIP receptor signaling potentiates responses to both endogenous and exogenous GLP-1 receptor agonism.

We therefore have this conundrum: GIP receptor agonism by itself is beneficial, but blocking the GIP receptor acts as a GLP-1 receptor-signaling sensitizer, not only in animals but also in humans. I think this explains the genetics, because loss-of-function human GIP receptor alleles will likely sensitize our bodies to endogenous GLP-1.

## Glucagon–GLP-1 coagonists

We also have a number of glucagon–GLP-1 receptor coagonists. This was phase 2 data for survodutide, originally developed by Zealand and now in partnership with Boehringer Ingelheim. They have just released some phase 3 data.

I think the glucagon medicines will be particularly useful in people with metabolic liver disease. Remember, the GLP-1 receptor is not expressed in hepatocytes, whereas the glucagon receptor is. Glucagon receptor signaling is very powerful in reducing lipid synthesis and promoting fat oxidation.

When you combine that with the actions of GLP-1, we will see what the phase 3 data show for retatrutide, survodutide, and pemvidutide. I think this is where we will elevate the glucagon–GLP-1 medicines in the future.

## Substance-use disorders

We are also entering a fascinating new era in substance-use disorders, motivated in part by reports from patients telling us, “I am not interested in smoking. I am not interested in alcohol. I do not use as much cannabis,” and so forth.

We have some promising data for alcohol and much less promising data for smoking. Will the effect size in a randomized controlled trial be sufficient to merit approval? If the evidence is only anecdotal and 5%, 10%, or 15% of people in a trial respond, that is not very robust. But if 30%, 40%, or 50% respond, remember that we do not have very effective therapies for many of these substance-use disorders.

Eli Lilly, for example, is developing once-monthly medicines. You could conceive of an era in which these individuals—often difficult-to-treat patients with multiple competing health issues—might benefit from long-acting GLP-1 medicines. If the data support this, I think that will be extremely useful going forward.

## Pregnancy and the need for clinical trials

Another area in which we need more data is pregnancy. First, we need to discuss this with women of reproductive age. You might be anovulatory now, but be careful when using these medicines because you may become pregnant. Current guidance suggests stopping the medicines before pregnancy—not because we have evidence of teratogenicity or malformations, but because there is an absence or dearth of evidence.

What happens when you stop the medicines before pregnancy? If you are a woman with a background history of obesity and hypertension, stopping the medicines results in weight gain, increased blood pressure, and increased glucose, creating a much higher-risk pregnancy.

This era cries out for clinical trials from our colleagues in obstetrics and maternal–fetal medicine. My personal belief is that, if we had clinical-trial data, they would show that these medicines are not only safe but probably reduce the risk of maternal and fetal complications. We need to take this on as a community, work with our Research Ethics Boards, and obtain permission to conduct these trials.

## Healthy weight loss and sarcopenic obesity

Another fascinating area, at least to me, is healthy weight loss and sarcopenic obesity. We have about a dozen medicines being repurposed, many involving the TGF-β superfamily, whether through blocking myostatin, targeting activin, using growth hormone-releasing hormone or selective androgen receptor modulators, or targeting apelin. There are many approaches, and the question is: how will we judge their success?

Lean mass by itself is not a clinical outcome. We do not have many people presenting after their weight-loss journey and saying, “My DXA scan shows a 21% reduction in lean mass, but I am exercising more, feeling healthier, and feeling better.”

Similarly, even if there were benefits in grip strength, stair climbing, and six-minute walk time, would that be sufficient to represent a meaningful clinical benefit that merits approval and reimbursement? Probably not.

How would you design a trial? How would you select the patient population? Which outcomes would be clinically meaningful to patients and save money for the healthcare system? We do not know. These discussions are ongoing within the community and regulatory agencies.

## Cancer research

Cancer is another area attracting considerable attention. For those of you who followed the American Society of Clinical Oncology meetings, there were about 20 abstracts showing that people living with cancer who were treated with these medicines in real-world retrospective registry studies did better, with fewer recurrences and less metastatic disease.

Many trials are now being initiated in people either at high risk of developing cancer or living with cancer, examining progression from minimal residual disease or clinical disease progression. This is an exciting area.

Again, is the effect driven by weight loss, or does it reflect changes in the tumor microenvironment? This is a very interesting and clinically relevant topic for further investigation.

## Future directions and closing remarks

Today, we have two primary medicines that activate both the GLP-1 receptor and the GIP receptor, but we will have dozens of new medicines that alter many other receptors. Will they add value in particular organs? Will they subtract value? Hopefully not. How will we choose among them, and in what sequence will we use them?

These are tremendous research opportunities for the young people in the audience as we move forward in this new age of GLP-1 medicines.

That is what I wanted to share with you today. I also want to remind everyone that the Endocrine Society has a tremendous amount of activity underway in this field. In September, you will see new clinical practice guidelines and new research documents. A substantial amount of activity within the Endocrine Society is focused on this area, and I look forward to much more activity in the future.

Finally, these are the people in my lab who do the work. Thank you so much for inviting me here today.
