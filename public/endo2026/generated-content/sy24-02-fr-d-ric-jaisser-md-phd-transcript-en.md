Moderator: The next presentation will be given by Dr. Frédéric Jaisser. Frédéric is director of an INSERM unit in Paris at Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital and will present a talk on the role of mineralocorticoid receptors and therapeutic opportunities in nephrocardiology. Thank you, Frédéric.

Frédéric Jaisser, MD, PhD: I would first like to warmly thank the organizing committee for this invitation. It is an occasion to meet friends, but also to meet people whom I am not used to meeting because I am more of a nephrocardiologist than an endocrinologist. But it is great to be here.

The focus of the talk is to discuss the potential role of the mineralocorticoid receptor and therapeutic opportunities provided by antagonizing its activity in nephrocardiology. Why nephrocardiology? I have some disclosures here.

We commonly say that our patients in nephrology die from the heart and not from the kidney. That is a bit of a statement, but it is intended to show that the cardiovascular impact of renal dysfunction and renal failure is very important. This is shown, for example, on this panel. As the level of renal failure and renal dysfunction increases, cardiovascular events—atherosclerotic or not—increase and survival decreases. This is really a major problem in our patients.

Why is there this connection between the heart and kidney? It is because there are common pathways that are modulated, activated, and deleterious in the heart, vessels, and kidney. Of course, some are very specific. Cardiomyocyte arrhythmia is specific to the heart; renal dysfunction, renal failure, and production of uremic toxins come from the kidney. But there are interactions between these organs. One possible therapeutic approach is to identify common mechanisms underlying dysfunction of the three organs, as well as the crosstalk between them.

That is why we focused on one of these possible mechanisms: aldosterone and the mineralocorticoid receptor. You know this very well, but I have this slide just to say that aldosterone is a good thing. It is a physiological hormone needed to ensure sodium, potassium, and water homeostasis. But if the system is overactivated—and we can discuss at length what that means exactly, whether it is an increase in aldosterone production, an increase in mineralocorticoid receptor expression, or hyperactivation of the system—then in many conditions, in what I call a “too much” environment, inappropriate or excessive activation can occur. In those cases, the system can be targeted by blocking the MR.

Why is it interesting to look at this system in nephrocardiology? First, if you look at the center panel, the MR is expressed in many cell types involved in the function of the three organs I am discussing: vessels, heart, and kidney. There is some cell-specific expression—for example, in podocytes in the kidney and cardiomyocytes in the heart. We heard about endothelial cells and smooth-muscle cells in the vessels. But there are also immune cells that express the MR, whatever the ligand that activates it. Macrophages and T cells, for example, can express the MR. In cardiac and renal disease, there is this combination of activated cells in which the MR can also be activated.

Another point, which was mentioned in the previous presentation, is that aldosterone production increases in chronic kidney disease. The worse the renal function, the higher the observed aldosterone level, as shown here in this recent paper.

The other part of the slide, on the right, is interesting because it focuses not only on the ligand but also on expression of the MR. Several of us have emphasized over the past few years that the MR can be upregulated under disease conditions. This example is interesting because it is a single-cell analysis. It was not performed by us, but I went into the data and showed that the MR can be overexpressed in immune cells and some other cell types in human kidneys from patients with diabetic nephropathy.

This means that we have cells that express the MR, an increase in the ligand, and probably and possibly an increase in the receptor. In this condition, everything is there to suggest that aldosterone and the MR—or the MR with or without aldosterone—could be a very good therapeutic target.

Today I would like to discuss what we know about this role, including a target we identified in the laboratory that could be interesting as a biomarker or as a new target in the pathway. I would also like to discuss some perspectives and show you unpublished data in which we focused on valvular disease, which is important in patients with CKD. This is quite new in the laboratory. I also have a few slides on aldosterone synthase inhibitors. They do not target the MR directly, but in the context of ENDO I think we can raise the question.

For many years, my laboratory has focused on the role of the MR and the potential benefits of blocking it within this triangle linking the kidney, heart, and vasculature. Over the years, using mainly animal models but also translational studies, we have identified several parameters that are modified when the MR is activated and that can be improved under disease conditions with an MR antagonist.

This is particularly relevant in view of the amazing results obtained over approximately the past five years in chronic kidney disease, especially diabetic CKD but also nondiabetic kidney disease. For a long time, the nephrology community was very sad because we had the powerful success of angiotensin receptor blockers and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, but then for 20 years nothing was really effective in reducing the progression of renal dysfunction and the associated cardiovascular consequences.

We had to wait 20 years, and now we have many new tools, especially the MR antagonist finerenone, which we just heard about, as well as sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists. Finerenone is a new nonsteroidal MR antagonist, marketed as Kerendia.

These are amazing data published a few years ago from a very large clinical-trial program using finerenone. It was tested in two parallel clinical trials: FIDELIO-DKD, which was oriented more toward patients with renal dysfunction, and FIGARO-DKD, which was oriented more toward patients with cardiovascular dysfunction, although the studies shared common parameters. FIDELITY was the pooled analysis of the two trials. What is important here is that, overall, there was a major reduction in cardiovascular events in these patients with diabetes and chronic kidney disease, as well as a reduction in progression of renal dysfunction. This was extraordinary because, as I said, we had not had anything very effective during the preceding 20 years.

The question now is: how does it work? We have some elements because many people have worked on this, but we are trying to determine whether we can identify patients who would be more likely to benefit from MR antagonism, SGLT2 inhibition, or perhaps both.

We have been working on this for several years, in parallel with the clinical trials and in collaboration with Bayer. For that purpose, we developed a model in our laboratory using rats. I should say that the rat is much better than the mouse—especially the C57BL/6 mouse, which is not good in our hands, at least for assessing the crosstalk between kidney and heart.

We developed a 5/6 nephrectomy model. The model itself is not new. We showed, for example, that it produces cardiac fibrosis and that this cardiac fibrosis can be prevented by finerenone, whether finerenone is given at the beginning of renal failure or later, when renal failure is already established.

Together with its beneficial effect on cardiac fibrosis, finerenone also affects cardiac parameters, especially diastolic parameters. One problem is heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, or HFpEF, which is present in patients with chronic kidney disease and is also present in this rat model. Finerenone also has an effect on cardiac perfusion, probably through vascular effects. Cardiac perfusion is decreased in CKD rats, and this is improved by finerenone.

This work is published, but the question, as I said, is what the underlying mechanisms could be and whether there are new mechanisms that we can explore. I like this figure, which comes from a review that is not mine. It shows common and distinct mechanisms in the heart and kidney that are related to MR activation.

We focused on one target, NGAL. NGAL is my favorite gene because we have worked on it for almost 20 years. NGAL is neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, also called lipocalin-2 or LCN2. We identified this target about 20 years ago, which makes me a bit old. Those were the old days when transcriptomic arrays contained approximately 1,000 genes—nothing like single-cell sequencing today. Nevertheless, we identified a gene that was modulated by cardiac MR and started to analyze its possible role.

We asked whether it was regulated by aldosterone under all conditions and in all cell types. To assess the gene's role, we developed pharmacological antagonists, and we also developed knockout mice and, as you will see, a knockout rat. This began with a fishing experiment in 2007, before the iPhone—so to young people it looks like prehistory, but it is true. We then worked on it for 20 years and continue to obtain very interesting results.

The latest result was published this year in *Hypertension*, and it relates to the context I am discussing here. As I showed you, the CKD model in rats is particularly effective for the question we are asking. We developed an NGAL knockout rat and applied the same 5/6 nephrectomy model. We showed that the cardiac fibrosis induced by CKD, which I showed you before, is fully prevented in the NGAL knockout rat. Diastolic dysfunction and cardiac perfusion are also improved, although not completely. NGAL is therefore one of the MR target genes that appears to be very important in cardiorenal crosstalk.

I will not go into the details of the mechanism of action because it involves several papers and a great deal of signaling analysis. But it relates to the role of NGAL in inflammation, macrophage polarization, induction of fibrosis, and extracellular-matrix remodeling. We have dissected these mechanisms in many papers.

I would now like to discuss something new that is related to aortic stenosis. Why? Because one of the cardiovascular problems in patients with CKD is aortic stenosis, which is present in 10% to 15% of patients. We asked whether aldosterone and the MR could be involved, because aortic stenosis involves extracellular-matrix remodeling and calcification, and we know from the literature and from our own work that aldosterone–MR signaling can modulate both extracellular-matrix remodeling and vascular calcification.

The valve is very specialized. The leaflet is composed mainly of two cell types. One is endothelial cells, which are somewhat different from classical vascular endothelial cells. The others are interstitial cells, which are prone to becoming fibroblast-like cells and developing extracellular matrix. Proteoglycans and collagen lie between them and give the valve its plasticity.

When aortic stenosis or valve remodeling occurs, there is inflammation and activation of these interstitial cells, which are called VICs—valvular interstitial cells. Activation of these cells leads to remodeling and production of extracellular matrix, including proteoglycans and collagens, as well as osteogenesis and calcification. At the end of the day, this produces aortic stenosis.

We asked whether aldosterone and the MR could be involved. We again developed a model based on the 5/6 nephrectomy CKD model. We used Sprague–Dawley rats and pushed the system a little by giving a high-phosphate diet and vitamin D to increase calcification. When you look at the aortas of these animals at sacrifice, it is amazing—they are like bone.

These animals develop clear remodeling of the valve at the hinge, where the leaflet attaches to the cardiac tissue, and also within the leaflet. Several calcification markers are induced, including BMP4, SOX9, and many others. When we treat the animals with eplerenone, we prevent or blunt this calcification. Everything else is the same: blood pressure is unchanged, and renal dysfunction is also not modified.

The question is: how does it work? I had a very talented postdoctoral researcher from Spain working on the signaling pathways. She was able to prepare primary cultures of VICs from these rats and analyze signaling in those cells.

I will not go into the details. You have the colors, if you like colorful diagrams, but the important point is that aldosterone at 10⁻⁸ mol/L induced inflammation, VIC activation, fibrosis, and osteogenesis. We measured a large panel of genes and secreted proteins, and everything was blocked by finerenone in the culture medium. Thus, the effects were aldosterone-dependent and MR-dependent.

The amazing thing to me—because I was really not sure it would work—was what happened when I asked the postdoctoral researcher to test whether pooled serum from CKD rats would do the same thing. It did. We measured aldosterone in the serum and found that it was increased, which fits somewhat with what happens in patients.

When we add CKD serum, we obtain exactly the same pattern as with aldosterone itself: increased inflammation, VIC activation, fibrosis, and osteogenesis. Everything is blocked when finerenone is added ex vivo to these cultured cells. This means that the MR is engaged. I do not know whether that is due only to aldosterone, and we are now working on non-aldosterone-mediated MR engagement and activation, especially by phosphate. I think that is very interesting.

The next step was to determine whether NGAL was involved in this cascade. I told you that, for us, NGAL is very important in MR signaling. We used the same NGAL knockout rat that I presented before, with the same experimental approach, and found that NGAL deletion blunted calcification and fibrosis induced in the valve in this animal model.

In summary, with respect to CKD-related valvular disease and MR activation, we showed—part of this work was published earlier this year in the *Journal of the American Heart Association*, while another part involving the NGAL story was submitted—that aldosterone and MR activation increase in CKD rats and induce aortic-valve remodeling.
