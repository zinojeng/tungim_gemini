## Human pluripotent stem-cell and organoid platform

I was trained as a chemist and stem cell biologist. So in my lab, we are mainly interested in two questions. One is how to build these functional human cells or organoids, and then once we can build it, how to use it for disease modeling and drug screening. And the cell type we use is called human pluripotent stem cells. To this audience, I don't think they need a lot of introduction. There are mainly two types of human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs). One is called human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), which is isolated from inner cell mass. And back to 2006, Dr. Shinya Yamanaka found that using four transcription factors, we can reprogram skin fibroblasts back into pluripotency stage.

We call it induced pluripotent stem cells, or iPSCs. And currently at Cornell, we have an IRB protocol. If you donate 5 mL of your blood, we'll do a paired whole genome sequencing and iPS cell production. When CRISPR technology was developed, it was immediately applied to human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) using now a CRISPR gene editing or prime base editing. We can precisely knock out a gene, knock out a regulatory region, or even knock in a single-nucleotide polymorphism to create these isogenic cells and then differentiate into the disease-relevant cell type and to study the biological function of these genes or genetic variants.

So in the early stage of my career, we really focused on this question, is how to convert these pluripotent stem cells into the cell type we are interested in. And after two decades' work, we developed our own protocol. We also adapt other people's protocol. Currently, we have a platform. We can make around 15 different types of cells or organoids from human pluripotent stem cells.

## Center for Genomic Health and screening platform

Several years ago, we built a center at Cornell. We call the Center for Genomic Health, CGH. The CGH is really centralized by this organoid model, centered on what we call an Organoid Village platform. When we call it a village, we have iPS cells from both sexes, both different ethnic backgrounds, and also different disease backgrounds, and we convert these iPS cells to different kinds of organoids. And this pipeline was set up for collaboration. So at the first branch, we have a large bioinformatics pipeline of single-cell spatial short reads, long reads to work with pathologists and surgeons to analyze samples and generate hypotheses.

In the second part, we work with epidemiologists to use stem cell model to validate any genes or genetic variants identified from GWAS. In the third pipeline, we work with chemical biologists and biochemists to convert all these useful tools they develop into the organoid platform. And in today's talk, I focus on the last part, which is our drug screening effort. When I was trained as a chemist, so I love drug screening, and this is a drug screening pipeline at CGH.
