### Introduction by Carol A. Lange

**Dr. Carol A. Lange:** Congratulations to all of our Laureate Award winners. And now we'll turn to this morning's plenary topic, unraveling hormonal complexity. Advances in genomics are transforming our understanding of hormonal regulation across tissues.

This morning, two renowned experts will share how this work is advancing our understanding of hormones, and it's my great pleasure to welcome to the stage our first speaker, Holly Ingraham. Holly is the Herzstein Endowed Professor in the Department of Cellular and Molecular Pharmacology at the University of California, San Francisco. Her research focuses on sex differences in hormone-responsive nodes in the brain and peripheral tissues that regulate metabolic, skeletal, and gut physiology in females. Through question-driven basic science, Dr. Ingraham seeks to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying adaptive responses throughout the female lifespan to understand how hormonal fluctuations affect women's health.

Ingraham's innovative and multidimensional work has illuminated basic molecular mechanisms that impact female physiology, going beyond the reproductive system. During her postdoctoral work in the Rosenfeld lab at UCSD, she was the driving force behind identifying Pit-1, one of the first tissue-specific regulators and a founding member of the POU transcription factor family. At UCSF, Dr. Ingraham demonstrated that the nuclear receptor SF-1 is a key developmental factor in establishing gonadal sex differences by regulating the peptide hormone anti-Müllerian hormone, or AMH. Using biochemical and structural studies, her lab went on to identify phospholipids as ligands for SF-1 and further demonstrated the in vivo impact of SUMOylation of these receptors in endocrine organs and metabolic tissues.

Dr. Ingraham's more recent work has defined molecular pathways that control female physiology.

Her group provided a mechanistic understanding of the preovulatory activity spike, underscoring the impact of estrogen in countering metabolic decline. Dr. Ingraham has chaired and served on numerous NIH review panels and scientific advisory boards. She was actually a chair of one of the study sections I was on, and she was a great leader in that role.

Her longstanding dedication to understanding basic female physiology has led to numerous distinguished lectureships and public forums on women's health. She is also the founder of a biotech venture focused on alleviating age-related frailty and skeletal decline. Dr. Ingraham is an elected fellow of the American Association for the Advancement of Science and a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences.

She earned her PhD from Revelle College at the University of California, San Diego. Please join me in welcoming Dr. Holly Ingraham.

And I think this could, you know, what we're finding could be relevant to other pelvic issues that really plague women. ### The estrogen-responsive L-cell–EC-cell cascade

So it all starts with estrogen acting in those L cells, not the GLP-1 cells, but really mostly the PYY-expressing L cells in the colon, because that's where ERα is. You get PYY released, and estrogen also upregulates Olfr78, which is then going to respond to things like acetate. Acetate, of course, is at millimolar levels when you take a high fructose, high—basically you have a diet that's high in those FODMAPs. The bacteria are going to convert all of that to these short-chain fatty acids, such as acetate.

They get very, very high in the distal colon, and that then sets up this whole system to be sensitized to a sugary diet. And then through PYY, you get an increase in serotonin output and increase in visceral sensitivity. So, and of course, you know, this—I've simplified the story. I'm sure there's going to be other components to this that we still have to find out.

But I think what this really shows is how estrogen increases sensitivity to a high-sugar diet, especially in the female gut. And the other thing is that it really identifies two new targets that you could go after for irritable bowel syndrome that were not known before. So we'll see how that goes.

### PYY as a nociceptive signal and a possible pregnancy adaptation

Now, I wanted to also highlight one thing, especially for you endocrinologists who think about hormones: this really places PYY as a nociceptive signal in the gut rather than a satiety factor. And, you know, this is where AI doesn't work. If you were to do a literature search before our paper, which I did, and you looked for PYY as a signal for visceral sensitivity or gut pain, you would come up with Roger Cone's paper on food aversion, and then you'd find these two or three other papers from Japan that had only been cited about nine times, outlining this pathway between PYY and visceral sensitivity. If you looked for PYY in food intake, you'd find over 900 citations.

So really, you would just think PYY has to be important for food intake. And in all of our studies with manipulating estrogen, we didn't find anything related to food intake. And so this is just their little graph that they put out in 2012. They did this probably—they probably spent 100 or 200,000.

Our study, maybe a million, because it was much fancier. But they really got the whole system right with just a simple gut preparation and pharmacology. So my advice to younger people is don't forget to read. Find out the foundations of science that is going to make your science better.
