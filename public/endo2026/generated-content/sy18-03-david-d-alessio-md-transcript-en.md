# Next-Generation Injectable Incretin-Based Therapies: Dual and Triple Agonists

## Presentation

**Moderator:** We had two great talks. Our last speaker is a prominent leading physician-scientist. Dr. D'Alessio is chief of endocrinology at Duke. Thank you.

**David D'Alessio, MD:** What do I have to do to make this—I'll start. Start? Yeah, good. Well, thanks very much for the introduction and for the invitation to speak. Thanks for coming. I've given the last talk of the day in the past. It's usually me and the other two speakers, so I commend everybody for hanging in on this one.

I'm going to talk about the injectable GLP-1s and where they might be going. There's a lot of exciting stuff going on. What I'm going to do is walk you through quickly how we got from zero to where we are now, and then, rather than show you a list of all the activity—and there's plenty—I'm going to show you a couple of avenues of research that are ongoing. Let's see and give an example of how that is, just so you see what the possibilities are.

It wasn't supposed to be like this. When GLP-1 got discovered, it was supposed to be an insulin secretagogue. You put it on cells that make insulin, and they secrete insulin. This was the incretin that we had been looking for for a long time. The receptor gets cloned from beta cells, and this was where we were moving: a diabetes drug.

This figure is from the study that got everybody on board. This was a niche area until Michael Nauck did this study in which he gave diabetic subjects an infusion of GLP-1, and their blood sugars went from 220 to 90 in four hours. So he normalized blood sugar with native GLP-1. Then people said, “Oh, that's not just a gut hormone. This is really going to be something.” I think that really launched the effort to put GLP-1 into the clinic.

Exendin-4 gets discovered. People figure out the receptor, figure out that you can't give the native peptide, and we get the first generation of drugs probably 15 years after GLP-1 is discovered, which was pretty rapid progress at that time. Exendin-4 and its ilk in the first wave gave you a reduction in A1c of about 1% and 2 to 4 kilograms of weight loss. One percent was what it took to get a diabetes drug on the market, and 2 to 4 kilograms seems kind of paltry right now. But at the time, we were treating diabetic patients with insulin, sulfonylureas, and TZDs, and they all came back mad because you'd say, “Your A1c is great,” and they'd say, “I gained 8 pounds.” To be able to tell them you had a neutral drug, or maybe one that would cause a little weight loss, was really cool.

What changed everything was semaglutide. It's a very interesting story of how you get from the first generation to the second generation. We don't have time to do that story today, but regardless, it's the extended pharmacokinetics that comes from some very clever medicinal chemistry and the fact that they were pretty aggressive in the dosing. All of a sudden, you had a drug that was giving you 15% weight loss if you didn't have diabetes and 12% if you had diabetes. This was what people had been looking for for a long time. Everything else on the market couldn't get placebo-controlled weight loss much beyond 5%, and people found it more frustrating than anything. But semaglutide really changed the game.

What it also did was get the neuroscientists, who heretofore had been spending all their time on leptin, to start looking at GLP-1 receptors in the brain. It turns out there are some, particularly in the hypothalamus and the hindbrain. These receptors are located in what are called circumventricular organs, areas in the brain where the blood–brain barrier is fenestrated and circulating products can interact with neurons or parts of neurons.

You see here, in this cutaway of rodent brains—over the cut through the top—that if you give fluorescently labeled liraglutide, in hindbrain and forebrain you can see right here in the median eminence, right above the pituitary gland, that there's a lot of liraglutide. This is a circumventricular organ. Then this place in the back, the area postrema, is just loaded with GLP-1 receptors.

Lots of work has gone into whether GLP-based drugs are transported across the blood–brain barrier, whether there are receptor systems, et cetera. I think the consensus, or the general belief, is they don't get into the brain. They do all their business here in the circumventricular organs. They're like that crazy neighbor who jumps on the porch and yells through the window but never really comes into the house, right? I think this is a feature of the system. It allows you to target a complex organ, the brain, with molecules and have them act in a really specific way. There are GLP-1 receptors all through the brain, but it's these ones in these key metabolic areas that are mediating the obesity effect. It was nice to have the neuroscientists on board and start to explain how this worked.

Once you have semaglutide, you can start to use it as an obesity drug. In the background, people are playing around with these multi-receptor agonists. You can go back into the 1990s and find molecules that talk to two G protein–coupled receptors at the same time. You can find native hormones: oxyntomodulin binds the glucagon receptor and the GLP-1 receptor. But specific drugs took a while.

Richard DiMarchi, who's a brilliant chemist who worked at Lilly—he's the guy who invented insulin lispro and started the insulin revolution—was trying to make a soluble glucagon. He was moving the amino acids around, trying to make it easier for patients to handle so he could use it to treat hypoglycemia. What he found instead was a molecule that hit the glucagon and the GLP-1 receptor. He was working with Matthias Tschöp, who's a great physiologist, and they showed that this drug caused weight loss and improved glucose tolerance.

This brings this notion of iterative chemical refinement, which is really just flipping amino acids in and out and seeing what happens. They started making glucagon–GLP-1 agonists, GIP–GLP-1 agonists, et cetera. None of those compounds made it to the clinic, but Lilly was playing around with the same idea. They were reading the papers and saying, “This seems cool.” So they took GIP and, again, changed the sequence, added a little bit here and a little bit there, and got a molecule that is pretty good at the GLP-1 receptor and 100% at the GIP receptor. They called it tirzepatide. There's a good story about that that I can't remember right now.

Tirzepatide works on both receptors. You can show it in mice: when you knock out one receptor, it still works; you knock out the other, it still works. We showed it in human islets. If you block the GLP-1 receptor, tirzepatide still stimulated insulin secretion. It looked like a true dual agonist, and it was a hellacious weight-loss drug.

In people without diabetes, in a comparison against semaglutide—which did very well, as it always does—tirzepatide was a little more potent. People were getting 20% weight loss on average, which again is a substantial change over 10 years of drug development.

Now, this is weight loss in a trial that was just finished, SURPASS-CVOT. This was a trial to test the effects of tirzepatide on MACE, on cardiovascular events. The trick here is that you've got to follow them for a long time. This is a four-year trial, the longest trial of tirzepatide, and it was done with an active comparator, dulaglutide. The notion was that the standard of care if you have heart disease—these were people with type 2 diabetes in their 60s, with 15 years of diabetes and demonstrated atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. These were the patients we see in clinic. These are the ones primary care refers to us to consider a GLP-1 receptor agonist.

You can see here that the dulaglutide group lost about 5 pounds and the tirzepatide group lost about 12 pounds. What was interesting was that more than half of the patients lost more than 10%, and this was maintained pretty well over three years. That is, as long as they stayed in the trial and took the drug, there was really good lasting weight loss, albeit at a plateau.

Here are the waterfall plots, as Sean just alluded to. On the top is A1c for tirzepatide and dulaglutide, and down here is weight for tirzepatide and dulaglutide. Some people got the short straw and didn't lose any weight, or maybe gained. Waterfall plots for gastric bypass look just like this. Can you imagine paying for a bypass and not losing any weight? That would make you think about karma.

But the point that was made is this enormous variability in the clinical response. Some of that is adherence and other conditions and behavioral things, but some of this is baked in. In the normal population, if you measure insulin secretion in response to GLP-1, so you get a beta-cell sensitivity to GLP-1, you can show that that's reproducible. Some people are always high responders, some people are always low responders, and it varies about sixfold in people with normal glucose tolerance. Baked into the receptor sequences, the signaling sequences, et cetera, are variants such that we all have a signature.

We know this as endocrinologists, right? TSH, parathyroid hormone receptor, ACTH receptor—all these have different sensitivities. So it's not surprising that it varies with GLP-based drugs.

If dual agonists are good, why don't we try triple agonists? We all know there's a triple agonist clanging around out there and making a lot of noise because it's really potent. Why would you add glucagon agonism to GIP and GLP-1?

The notion is that glucagon is always supposed to have been a counterregulatory hormone that really just makes glucose go up. In fact, I think that's wrong. I think glucagon's major effect is as a prandial hormone. Glucagon goes down if you give intravenous glucose, but if you give a meal—and most people eat meals that contain protein—it goes up about fivefold. Every time we eat, we get insulin and glucagon in the portal vein. It's a good insulin secretagogue through both the glucagon and GLP-1 receptors. It seems, in the liver, at least in some mouse studies, to increase insulin sensitivity. It's important in protein metabolism, and it probably increases energy expenditure. So it's not a crazy thing to add glucagon agonism.

Retatrutide—again, the press releases always come with a gasp and an exclamation point—is a potent drug that lowers body weight. Survodutide is another drug that's a dual agonist. It's just GLP-1 and glucagon. One of the arguments its developers have is that there are no GLP-1 receptors on hepatocytes, but there are tons of glucagon receptors. In fact, the glucagon receptor is the most common GPCR on a hepatocyte. It allows you to have a pharmacologic entrance into the liver, potentially to increase lipid metabolism and have effects on MASLD. That has been the push with survodutide.

Retatrutide presented last week at the ADA and had this phase 3 trial in people with diabetes in which, at the top dose, there was a 2% drop in A1c and a 16.8% drop in body weight. Sixteen percent is not what they reported in people without diabetes, because weight loss with all of these drugs is about 25% less in people with diabetes. But that's a big increase over what other drugs do in diabetes.

Then the survodutide data were reported. They really emphasized effects on the liver in terms of fat composition and fibrosis, both of which responded nicely to survodutide. Over the course of that roughly 70-week study, they had about 12% weight loss. Both of these agents with glucagon agonism are good for weight loss. They certainly don't cause hyperglycemia in people without diabetes, and they lowered A1c, or contributed to lowering A1c, in people with type 2 diabetes.

Pharmacokinetics, I said, was really important to semaglutide and the emergence of these drugs for weight loss. Can we do other things with pharmacokinetics that might help? There are a couple of pharmacologic tricks behind some new agents that increase residence time in the circulation and may lead to once-monthly drugs.

The first is berobenatide, a compound with an extended linker that stretches out the space between albumin and the molecule. The trick to getting semaglutide into the circulation is to put a linker on it, hook it to a fatty acid, have the fatty acid bind to albumin, and then it lasts for a long time. The drug can reach an equilibrium in which it comes off at a gradual pace that allows it to hit the receptor. This drug's linker is long enough that albumin won't interfere with peptide binding and receptor activation. It's like a drive-through: you can float by on the albumin, touch and activate the receptors as you go by, rather than having to come off the albumin and be absorbed into the cell.

The kinetics look like it might be monthly. These are very early data, with about 12% weight loss over a short period. The question—and the argument—is whether a longer residence time will improve adherence and whether the flatter steady-state profile will reduce side effects.

MBX is a company that Richard DiMarchi is involved with now. They have a related prodrug platform in which a cap masks the peptide while it binds albumin; the cap is removed gradually, releasing active peptide. It also looks as though it could support monthly dosing. It's a platform they are using with several drugs, including parathyroid hormone. They have MBX 4291, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, and MBX 5764, which combines GLP-1, GIP, glucagon, and dual amylin/calcitonin receptor agonism. These molecules that last a month could be very interesting. In shorter-acting drugs, peak-to-trough changes were associated with side effects, and it may be that a steadier dose will have fewer side effects.

This is the last thing. It came out in *Nature* two or three months ago, and this is a quintuple agonist. This is, again, Richard DiMarchi and Matthias Tschöp, with Timo Müller. They took a triagonist that hits the PPAR-alpha, PPAR-gamma, and PPAR-delta receptors and hooked it to a GLP-1/GIP co-agonist, a tirzepatide-like molecule. The notion is that if they can deliver the PPAR triagonist to key cells, they can give smaller doses and not get the classic PPAR side effects.

This is all preclinical data. What they showed was binding and activity in the brain and pancreas, without as much PPAR activity in the usual off-target tissues where there are no GLP-1 or GIP receptors. They got a number of dramatic effects. The weight loss in their little chubby mice was sort of astonishing. They got a lot of PPAR activity, which turns out to be really important in the brain and involved in energy metabolism. The point is that this group has been using incretin receptors to target other molecules to key areas in metabolism.

This next slide is a list from a 2024 paper and is probably already out of date. It looks a little bit like a lizard, doesn't it, with a head here and then the spine? I was thinking this was a tip of the hat to exendin and the Gila monster. But it just shows you all the things that are going on. This is a vibrant field, and everything keeps turning up—at least in terms of clinical effects and physiologic effects—as a winner. Everything you try looks really good. There are dual agonists, triagonists, and multiple GLP-1/glucagon agonists. There's a lot of emphasis on whether we can use these drugs to really get at MASH. There will be a lot of activity, and I hope competition.

Jaime did a beautiful job with the concerns about body composition. As far as I can tell from STEP and all the data I've looked at, it looks like about 20% to 30% of the weight lost is lean mass and 70% to 80% is fat mass on average with these drugs. That's the same pattern you get with restrictive diets and with bariatric surgery. The problem is that these are all mean data. There are obviously going to be people at either tail, and it brings to light how little we know about the regulation of body composition. I think we really need to fill in those gaps.

Before we go forward, I would tell you that in SURPASS-CVOT, the patient-reported outcomes in a population of sick people with vascular disease showed that more weight loss was better. They got functionally better with tirzepatide and with more weight loss. Again, as Jaime said, it's critical to have meaningful functional data to make the kinds of judgments that people are implying with their views of the clinical trials.

I want to finish with this. There's going to be a lot of activity in this field, with many papers coming forth and things to watch for. I realized, as I was watching all the talks at the ADA last week, that I reflexively assigned a number: “Oh, yeah, that doesn't impress me,” or, “That does impress me.” So what should we watch for as the data emerge?

If you know one GLP-1 receptor agonist, you know one GLP-1 receptor agonist. Even though we call them an agonist, a dual agonist, or a triagonist, what's becoming clear is that the way the molecules interact with the receptors differs from one to another. How much each molecule activates a receptor differs, and even how it signals differs. Some of them signal through G proteins, as we think. Some signal better through beta-arrestin. Semaglutide, for example, needs beta-arrestin. Some get internalized and degraded. For some, internalization is when they generate all their signal.

You don't want to lump all these together and say that they all work through a receptor in the same way. It's going to be more complicated. What it means is that each compound that comes down the road has to be evaluated de novo.

Be skeptical of attribution with these multi-receptor agonists: “Oh, that must be the glucagon effect,” or, “That must be the GIP effect.” We have no way to sort that out in vivo. Because these molecules are so long-acting, they're really hard to study by giving a molecule and then blocking it, because it takes five weeks to get to steady state. The molecules are what they are, and you have to be really careful about what you think is going on.

The instruments we use in trials to measure adverse effects are crude and imprecise. That's why it's really hard to differentiate. All the papers say, “This has the same side-effect profile as you'd expect for a GLP-1.” That's because the tests we do aren't good enough to really sort that out. If people start arguing that their trial shows a difference in side effects, you have to be a little skeptical.

Beware of cross-trial comparisons, of course. The population in SURPASS-CVOT was very different from all the tirzepatide populations before that, which had been healthier groups of people. You really do have to be careful looking at one study and saying, “Well, this one's 21%, and that's better than 19% in that one,” because it just doesn't hold.

Finally, regardless of the evidence that comes out in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, in the clinic we're all faced with the vagaries and vicissitudes of practice. In America, what that means is that you're really at the mercy of the formularies and the middlemen who decide who can get prior authorization and who can't. You may have set somebody up for tirzepatide, and they draw the short straw and get long-acting exenatide or something.

The patients also play a role in this. They come in and their neighbor tells them something, or they do research on the internet, and they come in with a strong bias about this or that. That gets in the way, too. We're really hampered in being able to use these drugs to their best effect because of access, for one reason or another—whether it's patient choice or, usually, cost.

I think being able to channel the beautiful effects we have in clinical trials into an actual clinical impact on public health isn't happening. We're not bending the curve, but it's an access problem. It's a problem with being able to treat the many people who need these agents. Thanks very much.

## Questions and Answers

**Moderator:** Thank you so much. We will open it up for some questions. It looks like everybody had that 6:15 dinner. I'll hold you here, David.

**Julio Ayala, Vanderbilt University:** Nice to see you. To your point about each one of these compounds being very unique and not knowing how they interact with receptors and so on, I wonder if you could comment on your thoughts about MariTide. If you combine GLP-1 receptor agonism with GIP receptor antagonism, you get effects very similar to agonizing both. What do you think might be happening?

**David D'Alessio:** We could go on for a long time. What I would say is that those two agents—say, just tirzepatide and MariTide—are acting on different systems, different populations of cells. I think MariTide requires the GIP system to be in concert with GLP-1 cells. With the dual agonist, I think you get some GIP cells that have independent effects. Whether the difference is that one is a huge molecule and maybe it doesn't get through all the fenestrations in the area postrema like the other one does, I don't know.

There are a couple of really good papers that have come out, one from Timo Müller's group and one from Reimann's group, that start to make that story pretty clear. We had a debate in the journal *Diabetes*, from Lilly and from Amgen, about this. It was really great. They put their scientists in, they wrote papers, and we didn't get any resolution. But it's a really interesting biologic question, and it speaks to both neuroscience and receptor pharmacology.

**Julio Ayala:** Thanks.

**Moderator:** I'm going to read a question from the app. In the SURPASS-CVOT trial, there was increased utilization of SGLT2 inhibitors during the actual trial. Was this assessed as a confounder?

**David D'Alessio:** Wow, somebody is doing some close reading. In SURPASS-CVOT, which we started recruiting for in 2020, penetration of SGLT2 inhibitors has only gone up. We predicted that it would go to 30%. In the dulaglutide group, it went to 40%. In the tirzepatide group, it didn't go up nearly as much. We are looking to see whether there was a difference and whether that might explain some of the cardiovascular findings.

What was really interesting is that in the tirzepatide group in SURPASS-CVOT, the amount of insulin people took went down and the use of other medicines dropped. People were on less medicine to achieve that low A1c, whereas the dulaglutide group increased their insulin use and increased their SGLT2 inhibitor use. Again, we have a paper under review that talks about the difference between a modest GLP-1 receptor agonist and a stronger one in terms of burden of care, number of other medicines needed, et cetera.

**David Moore, University of California, Berkeley:** I have a question that your comments at the end motivated me to ask. It's really from left field. In San Francisco, and I think in a lot of other cities in the United States—although maybe not so many around the world—people have already started taking retatrutide, right? Some of these peptides are widely available on the gray market or black market or whatever. I'm wondering what your comments are on the huge wave of peptide use and abuse outside medical guidance.

**David D'Alessio:** I think that may be a small wave. The big wave is that people are coming to my clinic with a much firmer idea of what they want in their medical care. It's not usually logical. It's not often what I want to do, but people have a much stronger— I feel like I have patients who see me as a route to prescriptions more than as an expert in disease and healing.

What happens with these compounded drugs is that it allows—actually, if you treat any bodybuilders, I get all the “T” guys in my clinic. If you treat bodybuilders, they've been doing this for years. They know all the chemistry they want to know, and they just find ways to get these drugs. They're the ones taking retatrutide after they bulk and then cut with “Reta,” or they call them “Chinese peptides,” because the peptides come from China.

My last comment is that, as you probably know, Secretary Kennedy is actually in favor of wide peptide access. I think this is really something that's coming. Nobody's putting the genie back in the bottle. Once the compounding thing got out there during the shortages, the demand became so pent up, and the availability will be widespread.

**David Moore:** Good point.

**Moderator:** Last question.

**Martin Graeber, Albert Einstein College of Medicine:** What about patients who have achieved substantial weight loss and are metabolically healthy? The pharmaceutical companies have no interest in showing how people should get off the drugs. What studies are being done to show how to deal with these people?

**David D'Alessio:** The first thing I would disagree with is that there are a lot of companies looking to do the off-ramp. They don't think there's going to be a hard stop, but they're looking at whether there are drugs or procedures that could help with weight maintenance. I think people see that as a really important adjunct. I don't know that anything beyond brainstorming is going on in that area.

The issue with these drugs is: what's the end game? What's the endpoint? Do you take them forever? It's really easy when I get a patient who has a BMI of 38 and needs a kidney transplant and they want him to be at 35. Oh, I can do that, right? Or I can get him to his spine surgery. I can help her through her fertility treatment. But for the usual person, it's hard to tell.

In diabetes, we have models for that. You treat the A1c and body weight with the other end-organ complications in mind, and you fit it in with their comorbidities. But in people treated for weight loss, we just don't have—I just don't know what the end game is yet. I know there needs to be an off-ramp because it seems pretty clear to me that these are dose-dependent, drug-requiring effects.

**Moderator:** Thank you so much. Thank you to all our speakers.
