## Trial background and adrenal-safety objective

Hello. Welcome, and thank you, everyone. I appreciate the opportunity.

The setup for this study was an ulcerative colitis trial using a novel device to insert next-generation hydrocortisone acetate (ngHCA) into the rectum to treat a patient population with flares. The comparison was placebo. I was consulted to establish a protocol to ensure adrenal safety for these patients, given the duration for which they would be taking hydrocortisone.

We also performed pharmacokinetic (PK) studies during the trial to assess response, administration of therapy, and compliance.

## Randomization and dual-dose cosyntropin testing

This is the trial design. We had a screening period in which participants underwent a cosyntropin stimulation test with the standard 250 µg dose to exclude primary adrenal insufficiency and ensure that we were not enrolling patients with secondary risk going into the trial.

They were randomized to one of three groups: twice-daily (BID) rectal ngHCA delivered with the device invented by the company; morning ngHCA with the evening dose as placebo; or full BID placebo dosing.

During the trial, PK testing was performed. There was sparse PK as well as intensive PK. I did not put those details on the slide to make the slide easier to review.

At the end of treatment, participants underwent the low-dose, 1 µg cosyntropin stimulation test. After the two-week washout, they underwent the test again at Visit 8. My responsibility was to assess Visit 8 in relation to Visit 7 and determine whether the patient had a safety signal concerning for secondary adrenal insufficiency. If so, the patient was directed to return for Visit 9, a dedicated safety visit with a repeat low-dose cosyntropin stimulation test.

If the data at that point continued to indicate or suggest secondary adrenal insufficiency, the patient was asked to see an external endocrinologist for follow-up after the safety visit was completed.

## Unexpected safety referrals in participants assigned to placebo

What was very interesting about this, and the reason we are presenting it, is that a significant number of patients who were referred to an endocrinologist after Safety Visit 9 turned out to have been assigned to placebo. They were randomized to placebo, and we could not understand how they developed secondary adrenal insufficiency if they were supposedly taking placebo.

We conducted a thorough review and corporate analysis of the products. We went back to the devices assigned to those patients, selected devices at random, analyzed the devices and the drug, sent them for external review, and audited the entire process for a possible randomization error, distribution error, or investigational-product administration error. We found nothing to that effect.

As a result, we decided to apply the basic endocrine principles in which we were all trained to identify patients who potentially—I call them “cheaters,” although we did not call them cheaters—had exposure to external or exogenous corticosteroids that were not part of the protocol.

I performed this analysis without knowing—and I want to make this very clear, because the FDA does not believe me—what the participants' outcomes were, which treatment arm they had received, or what their resolution was. I simply reviewed their cortisol values to assess the likelihood of potential exogenous corticosteroid exposure relative to basic biologic principles.

## Physiology-based interpretation principles

I will go through each of the biologic principles identified on the previous slide, using examples. I think they are easier to understand graphically than in writing.

### Principle 1: low-dose response relative to the screening maximum

In the first graph, the screening visit shows the high-dose cosyntropin stimulation test, with the baseline and stimulated cortisol values. One of my rules was that a low-dose cosyntropin test cannot produce a value higher than the original screening test. That is not biologically plausible.

### Principle 2: absent cortisol response at the end of treatment

The second principle was a lack of cortisol response to ACTH stimulation at the end of treatment, when the participant had completed the trial. This potentially indicates suppression and an absent cortisol response. Again, we should not be seeing secondary adrenal insufficiency in these patients.

### Principle 3: PK cortisol above the screening stimulated maximum

Principle 3 concerns the PK data. Using the screening ACTH stimulation test as the reference point, with the maximal stimulation produced by the 250 µg dose, a random cortisol value cannot be above the biologic maximum. In this example, it is clearly well above the biologic maximum, so this was also clearly a violation.

### Principle 4: low PK cortisol without a circadian pattern

The next example, on the far right as we move from left to right, is Principle 4. During the PK assessment, the participant comes in and has baseline cortisol measured. The investigational product is then administered under investigator observation, and PK values are drawn throughout the morning.

This pattern is also clearly disruptive: a person cannot have no cortisol throughout the day during a PK assessment. It is evidence of exogenous corticosteroid exposure suppressing endogenous cortisol production because there is no cortisol response in the expected circadian pattern, particularly when the assessment was performed first thing in the morning.

### Principle 5: baseline PK cortisol above the screening stimulated value

The last principle again involves one of the PK assessments, because, as I mentioned, two different types of PK testing were performed. Principle 5 concerns a baseline cortisol value at the PK draw that exceeded the screening stimulated cortisol value—that is, a random cortisol value that significantly exceeded the maximally biologically stimulated value possible based on the 250 µg stimulation-test dose.

Principles 3 and 5 therefore concern PK testing and comparison of baseline values with the maximum biologically plausible stimulated values.

## Implications for safety, efficacy, and trial integrity

The implication is that these principle-violating patterns may identify non-study corticosteroid exposure. Such exposure may confound safety interpretation and efficacy outcomes, which is clearly disruptive when assessing a new product and may make it difficult to define success.

In trials that involve corticosteroids or potentially corticosteroid-sensitive outcomes, we need to discuss how to structure hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis assessment and cortisol monitoring for both safety and trial-data integrity. If the study uses corticosteroids and participants also use exogenous corticosteroids, how do we distinguish between the two?

## Conclusions

In conclusion, cortisol values combined with a dual-dose ACTH stimulation strategy create an interpretable physiologic framework for non-endocrine corticosteroid therapeutic trials. Standard-dose testing can help define adrenal reserve; we have done this for decades with cosyntropin stimulation testing. Low-dose testing supports the detection of adrenal suppression from secondary causes or external sources such as exogenous corticosteroids. Serial patterns of cortisol values, potentially obtained during PK analysis, can reveal unexpected corticosteroid exposure.

I think this was a very clever way to track patients who potentially had intermittent exposures.

Finally, trials using corticosteroids, or trials in which corticosteroid exposure may affect outcomes—including non-oral formulations and repeated or prolonged use—should incorporate strategies to monitor HPA-axis safety and protect data integrity.
