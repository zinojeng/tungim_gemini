### Introduction

**Lisa Orloff:** Thank you. I have no disclosures.

More than 100,000 thyroidectomies are performed every year in the United States, and approximately 64,000 are performed for benign disease. Even thyroid lobectomy carries a postoperative hypothyroidism rate of about 30%. These figures help explain the interest in nonsurgical, thyroid-sparing therapy.

The available ablative modalities include ethanol ablation, laser ablation, radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation, and high-intensity focused ultrasound. I will focus primarily on RFA.

Ethanol is especially useful for cystic lesions. Laser, microwave, and high-intensity focused ultrasound all produce thermal injury by different mechanisms. RFA has become the most widely discussed thyroid thermal-ablation technique in North America because it permits continuous ultrasound guidance and controlled treatment of small tissue units.

### How radiofrequency ablation works

RFA uses a high-frequency alternating electrical current. Ionic agitation produces frictional heat, which causes coagulative necrosis within the target tissue. The ablated tissue then undergoes involution over time, reducing nodule volume.

The first publication on thyroid RFA came from Korea in 2006. The procedure spread through Asia and Europe before becoming available in North America. In the United States, the relevant devices have FDA clearance for soft-tissue ablation and are used for benign thyroid disease.

In 2022, an international multidisciplinary group published a consensus statement to standardize patient selection, technique, training, outcomes, and follow-up. That statement emphasizes that success depends on both appropriate patient selection and operator expertise.

### Indications and patient selection for benign nodules

The principal indications are symptomatic benign nonfunctioning nodules and autonomously functioning thyroid nodules. For a nonfunctioning nodule, we generally want two benign biopsies, although one may be sufficient for a nodule with a highly characteristic benign ultrasound appearance. Treatment is intended for symptoms or cosmetic concerns rather than for an incidental asymptomatic nodule.

Symptoms may include pressure, dysphagia, cough, or a visible or palpable neck mass. Cosmetic concern is a legitimate indication when it matters to the patient. The clinician should document that the symptoms are plausibly attributable to the target nodule and discuss alternatives, including observation and surgery.

A typical nonfunctioning target is at least 2 cm, but there is no absolute upper size limit. Larger nodules are more difficult to treat completely and may require more than one procedure. The nodule should be visible and accessible by ultrasound. We usually treat only one thyroid lobe at a time. Solid, mixed solid-cystic, and microcystic nodules may be treated. Predominantly cystic nodules may respond more simply to ethanol ablation.

The surrounding anatomy is critical. The recurrent laryngeal nerve lies in the posteromedial “danger triangle” or “danger arc.” Other structures at risk include the trachea, esophagus, carotid artery, internal jugular vein, vagus nerve, sympathetic trunk, skin, subcutaneous tissue, strap muscles, and anterior jugular veins. A treatment plan must account for all of them.

The vagus nerve usually lies within the carotid sheath between the carotid artery and internal jugular vein, but its position can vary. The sympathetic trunk is posterior to the carotid sheath. The recurrent laryngeal nerve is usually not directly visible, so the operator relies on landmarks and a conservative margin. Inferiorly extending nodules may descend below the clavicle or sternum and become difficult to visualize or reach safely.

### Expected outcomes and complications

Nodules shrink rapidly in the first one to three months. The international definition of technical success has generally been a volume reduction ratio (VRR) of at least 50% by six months. Many nodules continue to decrease, reaching reductions of up to about 90% by 12 months.

Voice change is the most common important complication, reflecting thermal injury to a laryngeal nerve. Other complications include pain, hematoma, skin burn, and the rare event of nodule rupture. Hypothyroidism is uncommon because untreated thyroid tissue is preserved, although underlying autoimmune thyroiditis or limited baseline thyroid reserve may increase risk.

During the procedure, the patient remains awake and able to speak. A change in voice, cough, or unexpected pain can provide an early warning. Stopping energy delivery promptly and using protective fluid may limit injury. The operator must balance complete treatment against safety; leaving a small untreated margin beside a nerve is preferable to causing permanent neurologic injury.

### Procedure and moving-shot technique

RFA is usually performed under local anesthesia in an office-based setting. Hydrodissection can be used to displace or protect sensitive structures. The trans-isthmic approach helps stabilize the electrode and keeps its active tip visible.

Local anesthesia also permits real-time neurologic monitoring through conversation. Sedation, if used, should not prevent the patient from reporting symptoms. Sterile technique, appropriate grounding, continuous ultrasound visualization, and careful control of the active tip are essential. The procedure can be time-consuming, particularly for a large or highly vascular nodule.

The moving-shot technique treats the nodule as multiple small conceptual units. The electrode is advanced into the distal portion of the nodule, energy is delivered until an echogenic zone appears, and the electrode is then withdrawn through adjacent tissue layer by layer, usually from inferior to superior. Because a nodule is three-dimensional and ultrasound displays two-dimensional planes, the operator must repeatedly reassess it in perpendicular views.

One example was a young woman with a 3-cm nodule that had grown over several years and had undergone repeated FNA because of growth, with repeatedly benign cytology. After treatment, the nodule measured 1.2 cm, with a 95% volume reduction. It no longer altered the thyroid contour and was no longer visible or palpable. This is a best-case result, but it illustrates the potential benefit without a cervical scar and with preservation of thyroid function.

Another example showed a prominent lobulated nodule that became more obvious when the patient swallowed. Pre- and post-treatment views demonstrated substantial contour improvement. The result preserved thyroid function and avoided a surgical scar. These visual examples also show why linear diameter alone is a poor measure of response: volume should be calculated in three dimensions.

### Autonomously functioning nodules

For an autonomously functioning thyroid nodule, the goal is biochemical as well as structural: to render the patient euthyroid. TSH normalization is achievable in up to about 80% of cases. Success is more likely for smaller nodules and when a greater volume reduction is achieved.

Larger or more vascular functional nodules may require more than one treatment. In a large toxic goiter, RFA can be combined with radioactive iodine, although that partly changes the rationale for choosing a local ablative treatment.

The biochemical endpoint does not always track perfectly with the anatomic endpoint. A nodule may shrink substantially without fully normalizing TSH, and a smaller nodule may normalize earlier. Patients need biochemical follow-up and may require ongoing antithyroid medication, repeat ablation, radioactive iodine, or surgery if hyperthyroidism persists.

### Regrowth

The greatest long-term limitation is regrowth. After a single procedure, up to 25% of nodules may show interval regrowth within two to four years, although they do not necessarily return to their original volume.

Regrowth usually begins at the margin. Complete peripheral treatment may be impossible where the nodule approaches the recurrent laryngeal nerve, trachea, esophagus, vagus nerve, or other sensitive structures. We therefore try to treat the nodule's vascularity and as much of the periphery as can be treated safely, while recognizing that anatomy may limit complete ablation.

Regrowth should be distinguished from temporary edema and from expected evolution of the ablation zone. Follow-up ultrasound evaluates volume, internal vascularity, and the viable peripheral rim. Renewed vascularity and progressive enlargement at the margin may indicate residual tissue that could benefit from a second procedure.

### Malignant disease: recurrent, metastatic, and primary tumors

There is no clear benefit from attempting to ablate large, aggressive, or locally advanced primary thyroid cancers; they are generally too invasive for this technique to provide meaningful control.

Interest is growing in papillary thyroid microcarcinoma, meaning papillary carcinoma 1 cm or smaller. This also raises a concern: ablative treatment could encourage additional detection and treatment of tumors that might never become clinically significant.

Percutaneous ethanol injection and RFA have also been used for recurrent or residual nodal disease after surgery for papillary thyroid carcinoma. They may provide local palliation for patients at high risk of morbidity from another operation, those who are unsuitable for surgery, or those who refuse it. However, there are no prospective randomized trials of ablation for metastatic thyroid cancer.

These procedures are local treatments. They do not replace systemic staging, assessment of disease biology, or treatment of disease elsewhere. In a previously operated central or lateral neck, scarring may alter anatomy and increase the risk of both reoperation and percutaneous treatment. Multidisciplinary review is therefore especially important.

The 2025 American Thyroid Association differentiated thyroid cancer guideline addresses these uses. Recommendation 52 discusses ethanol ablation and RFA as alternatives for local recurrent or residual disease in patients at high risk of complications from reoperation. Recommendation 76 addresses focal ablative treatment for solitary or oligometastatic radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, including selected liver, bone, and lung lesions. These are conditional recommendations based on low-certainty evidence.

### Papillary thyroid microcarcinoma

Several meta-analyses have pooled studies of thermal ablation for papillary thyroid microcarcinoma. Most data come from Asia, and some publications include overlapping patient cohorts. The studies are heterogeneous and retrospective and use different thermal modalities. Reported success rates are high—up to 100%, averaging about 91%—with one report extending to 10 years and several to five years. Complication rates have been low, but replication in other health-care settings is needed.

“Success” is not identical across studies. Some define it by complete disappearance, others by a negative biopsy of the ablation zone, absence of local progression, or lack of nodal disease. Different follow-up schedules, operators, and patient-selection rules make direct comparison difficult. High reported success should therefore be interpreted in the context of these differences.

Recommendation 11 of the 2025 ATA guideline states that ultrasound-guided percutaneous ablation may be considered as an alternative to active surveillance or resection for carefully selected patients with clinical T1aN0M0 papillary thyroid carcinoma.

The selection criteria resemble those for active surveillance. The diagnosis should be Bethesda VI papillary thyroid carcinoma. There are no comparable data for medullary thyroid carcinoma. The patient should be asymptomatic; symptoms raise concern for extrathyroidal extension. The tumor should be 1 cm or smaller, ideally unifocal, centrally located within the lobe, and not heavily calcified. Underlying autoimmunity or an elevated baseline TSH may be relative contraindications because of concern about future thyroid reserve.

High-quality neck ultrasound must show no suspicious lymph nodes and no evidence of distant disease. Multifocality, poorly visualized margins, aggressive cytology or molecular features, and inability to maintain long-term follow-up weigh against ablation. Patient preference matters, but preference does not overcome unsafe anatomy or inadequate diagnostic certainty.

Location is crucial. Undesirable sites include the posterior thyroid near the recurrent laryngeal nerve or esophagus. A subcapsular position is relatively unfavorable because malignant-tumor ablation must extend beyond the visible tumor margin. This contrasts with benign-nodule ablation, where we remain inside the nodule and accept incomplete peripheral treatment near vulnerable structures.

The relationship to the trachea matters. Broad, obtuse-angle contact is high risk and may be a contraindication. Near-right-angle contact is intermediate risk; a small acute-angle point of contact is lower risk, but the ability to clear the margin still requires careful judgment. Treating the safest 90% while leaving the highest-risk margin untreated does not accomplish the goal.

One example showed a subcapsular papillary microcarcinoma treated beyond the lesion itself. The required margin caused some thermal injury to the overlying strap muscle. The ablation zone was substantially larger than the original tumor and could continue to evolve over the first several months.

This example illustrates a different risk-benefit balance from benign ablation. For benign disease, the treatment may deliberately stop short of a vulnerable boundary. For cancer, a deliberately untreated margin may leave viable tumor. The operator must be confident that a complete oncologic margin can be achieved without unacceptable injury.

By 12 months, near-complete or complete disappearance should be seen for the treatment to be considered successful. The patient still requires close monitoring comparable to active surveillance. Patients do not escape surveillance by choosing ablation; some may later choose surgery because of surveillance fatigue or anxiety even without disease progression.

### Evidence limitations and shared decision-making

No randomized controlled trial has been completed. Two trials intended to compare RFA with surgery or active surveillance for papillary thyroid microcarcinoma were terminated before completion: NCT03808779 in China and NCT05132205 at Johns Hopkins. We therefore lack prospective randomized outcomes, and the available evidence is mainly retrospective and from Asia.

RFA also provides no surgical specimen. We cannot identify occult aggressive variants, lymphovascular invasion, or angioinvasion. Surgery yields final pathology that may upstage a patient and lead to additional treatment—for better or worse. This absence of pathologic staging remains an important limitation.

A core biopsy or FNA establishes the sampled diagnosis, but it cannot characterize the entire tumor in the way a surgical specimen can. A lesion believed to be conventional papillary microcarcinoma may harbor an unsampled higher-risk component. This limitation must be part of informed consent rather than treated as a purely theoretical concern.

Inappropriate candidates include patients with extrathyroidal extension, lymph-node or distant metastases, or known aggressive histologic variants. The significance of FDG avidity in this setting is not established. A patient unwilling to undergo surveillance comparable to active surveillance is also not an appropriate candidate.

Shared decision-making now involves a more complex menu. For a carefully selected low-risk unifocal papillary carcinoma, RFA may offer thyroid-sparing, minimally invasive treatment that is safe and effective in the available observational data. Disadvantages include the lack of randomized evidence, absence of pathologic staging, need for ongoing surveillance, and requirement for considerable technical expertise.

Papillary and medullary microcarcinomas can look similar on ultrasound, so medullary thyroid carcinoma must be satisfactorily excluded before ablation. At present, RFA can be considered a bridge between active surveillance and surgery—not freedom from monitoring.

### Panel Q&A

**Moderator:** Thank you, Dr. Orloff. We have eight minutes for questions. Dr. Sipos, if a thyroid nodule is FDG-avid, do ultrasound features affect its pretest probability of malignancy? Older literature suggests that FDG-avid nodules have a 35%–40% risk of malignancy, but that risk may not be the same for every nodule. Would you manage a low-suspicion FDG-avid nodule the same way as an intermediate-suspicion FDG-avid nodule?

**Jennifer A. Sipos:** That is a very good question, and we addressed it in the guideline. The main point is that studies remain limited. More data are expected from The Ohio State University. Sonographic stratification appears to inform malignancy risk in PET-positive nodules.

For low- and very-low-suspicion sonographic categories, PET positivity should not change the decision about FNA. For intermediate- and high-suspicion nodules that are PET-positive, we may use the lower end of the recommended size range for considering FNA.

The patient's overall clinical context matters. If the patient has a much more aggressive nonthyroid malignancy and evaluation of the thyroid finding would not change management or eligibility for a clinical trial, observation may be appropriate. Patients with incidentally identified PET-positive nodules have worse five-year mortality, so the thyroid incidentaloma may be less important than the primary cancer.

**Audience member:** Regarding RFA, what is the status of insurance and Medicare coverage?

**Lisa Orloff:** Medicare covers RFA. Because other insurers often look to Medicare, it has become harder to label the procedure experimental. A dedicated CPT code was released last year, which has helped recognition. In my experience, UnitedHealthcare has been the most difficult. Even when a procedure is authorized, the insurer may state that authorization is not a guarantee of payment. Coverage is becoming more accepted, but it still requires careful authorization.

Patients should therefore verify both professional and facility coverage in advance. A favorable authorization is helpful but does not eliminate the possibility of a later denial or patient cost-sharing.

**Audience member:** I wanted to ask about the taller-than-wide criterion. Sometimes a dimension of 2.01 versus 2.00 is called taller-than-wide even though it looks round. Should a ratio cutoff such as 1.2:1.0 be used?

**Jennifer A. Sipos:** That is a good point, and we discuss it in the guideline. Composition and echogenicity matter. Taller-than-wide is not predictive in a partially cystic nodule. In a solid nodule, echogenicity affects risk, and the feature is less robust in an isoechoic nodule. Size may also matter; taller-than-wide may be more predictive in a small nodule than in a large one.

A very small numerical difference may simply be measurement error. It should look taller-than-wide to the naked eye, and it must be measured in perpendicular planes. For these reasons it is probably the least robust suspicious feature. The literature is difficult because suspicious features tend to cluster and are rarely isolated cleanly.

The key is not to let a hundredth of a centimeter create false precision. The feature should be interpreted with the nodule's composition, echogenicity, size, and other suspicious findings rather than as an isolated binary measurement.

**Audience member:** Malignant tumors recruit intralesional and perilesional vessels. Do the new guidelines consider vascularity as a feature of malignancy?

**Jennifer A. Sipos:** Vascularity is another uncertain area. It may be more predictive when a follicular neoplasm is suspected and in the solid portion of a partially cystic nodule. We did not include it as a final decision rule because the evidence is less robust, although the guideline text discusses it. It may contribute to a decision about FNA, especially in a partially cystic nodule.

**Audience member:** For Dr. Fish: thyroid nodules are heterogeneous, with epithelial and stromal components. Gene clusters contributing to test specificity may be expressed in either component. Is specificity now the main limitation of molecular testing?

**Stephanie Fish:** That is a difficult and important question. Nodules are heterogeneous, and a needle samples only one portion, which must be kept in mind. At present, a negative or benign molecular result is strongly associated with a benign nodule, so the tests function well as rule-out tools.

We must still accept that some benign nodules will have indeterminate or positive results and that some patients with benign disease will undergo surgery. Performance has improved, but it is not perfect.

**Audience member:** Is the minimum size for RFA the same for a toxic adenoma as for a nonfunctioning adenoma?

**Lisa Orloff:** No. We generally use 2 cm or larger for a nonfunctioning nodule, but there is no minimum size for a toxic nodule. However, the larger the toxic nodule, the less effective RFA tends to be.
