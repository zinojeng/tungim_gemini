## When post-bariatric hypoglycemia is not post-bariatric hypoglycemia

**Brittany Weisbrot:** Good morning. My name is Brittany Weisbrot, and I will start with a question: How many of you who see patients—or really interact with anyone day to day—pay attention to the details? I will spend the next three minutes trying to convince you how important those details are.

Our story begins with a 45-year-old woman who had undergone biliopancreatic diversion with duodenal switch 15 years earlier. She presented to the endocrinology clinic with symptomatic hypoglycemia, including episodes of syncope at home. Her symptoms improved after eating. She also mentioned that they fluctuated according to the phase of her menstrual cycle.

We could not continue the evaluation in the inpatient setting, but we obtained a continuous glucose monitor (CGM). In post-bariatric hypoglycemia, we typically expect glucose to fall after meals. That was not the pattern in this patient. Her glucose was persistently low, and the CGM showed glucose in the low or very-low range approximately 70% of the time.

Something did not fit. She presented 15 years after bariatric surgery, whereas post-bariatric hypoglycemia usually presents in the first several postoperative years. She had also reported that symptoms worsened at different points in her menstrual cycle.

## Insulin autoantibodies and hyperestrogenism

Further testing showed positive insulin autoantibodies at 12.3 U/mL (reference, <0.4 U/mL) and a markedly elevated estradiol level of 866 pg/mL. Prior studies suggest a temporal relationship between insulin sensitivity and estrogen levels across the menstrual cycle.

Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging showed bilateral ovarian masses. The patient underwent bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and pathology showed benign ovarian lesions. After surgery, glucose normalized: CGM time in range increased to 97%. Insulin autoantibodies became undetectable, and estradiol fell to <15 pg/mL.

This case highlights an opportunity to explore further the relationships among estrogen, insulin sensitivity, and autoimmune modulation. Initial studies suggest possible associations, but the mechanism remains poorly understood. Paying attention to the atypical timing and cyclical symptoms helped us identify the likely process, and the patient entered sustained biochemical remission after surgery.

## Audience Q&A

**Audience member:** When you say “attention to the details,” did the patient volunteer the information, or were you actively asking questions?

**Brittany Weisbrot:** We were actively asking questions. At the initial visit, she noted that her hypoglycemic symptoms changed cyclically. During follow-up discussions, we explored this further and learned that she had a complex gynecologic history and was undergoing evaluation with her gynecologist. **Audience member:** Is this insulin autoimmune syndrome—Hirata disease? It is often triggered by a medication or another second signal; methimazole is one recognized trigger. Was there anything on her medication list that might have triggered the antibodies?

**Brittany Weisbrot:** There was nothing notable on the medication list, and we reviewed this specifically. She did not have a thyroid history. We hypothesized that the markedly elevated estradiol, together with the hormonally active ovarian masses, may have contributed to immune or autoimmune modulation and triggered the syndrome. This remains a hypothesis based on the clinical sequence rather than a proven mechanism.
