### Opening and G protein signaling

Good morning. I want to thank the organizers for letting me speak this morning. Although this is a session about PTH signaling, I am not actually going to talk very much about PTH signaling. We have already had very nice introductions from the previous speakers about G proteins and pseudohypoparathyroidism.

I want to put this slide up to reiterate that we are going to focus primarily on Gαs, but also a little on Gαq, which you will hear about later. Gαs is ubiquitously expressed and is involved in virtually any cellular process that leads to production of intracellular cyclic AMP. You see PTH and PTHrP at the top, where they belong, but I will focus on signaling by other hormones and neurotransmitters involved in weight regulation, diabetes, and related processes.

### Albright hereditary osteodystrophy and parent-of-origin effects

As already shown several times, Albright hereditary osteodystrophy, or AHO, is the canonical hormone-resistance syndrome and was the first such syndrome described, in the 1940s. The key takeaway is that the AHO phenotype, which is mainly skeletal and neurocognitive, can occur with either a maternally inherited form, pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1A, or a paternally inherited form, pseudopseudohypoparathyroidism.

Because of imprinting, several features are specific to PHP1A. These include multihormone resistance and, of particular interest this morning, early-onset obesity and insulin resistance.

This is a slide from one of our mouse papers, now almost three decades old, illustrating tissue-specific Gαs imprinting. At the top, when a mouse inherits a mutation on the maternal allele, there is almost total loss of Gαs expression. These are probably Western blots, although Northern blots showed the same result. The same mutation inherited from the father has little or no effect on Gαs expression.

In most other tissues, whether the mutation is inherited maternally or paternally, there is a similar 50% deficiency, consistent with a lack of Gαs imprinting in that tissue. The slide shows several tissues in which tissue-specific Gαs imprinting has been demonstrated in mice or inferred in humans. The phenotype we will focus on is obesity, which we believe derives fully or largely from imprinting in the central nervous system.

This was the first clinical study to show that obesity, like hormone resistance, has a parent-of-origin effect. It was the work of Emily Germain-Lee's group. Older textbooks described obesity as part of the general AHO syndrome, but this study showed that early-onset obesity is not really a feature of pseudopseudohypoparathyroidism.

That does not mean someone with pseudopseudohypoparathyroidism cannot eventually develop obesity, like much of the general population. Rather, obesity is not a salient feature of PPHP in the way it is in PHP1A.

### Global and CNS-specific Gαs mouse models

We and others made several models in which Gαs was disrupted globally. A paternal Gαs mutation produced a minimal metabolic phenotype. The same mutation on the maternal allele produced severe obesity, insulin-resistant diabetes, and hyperlipidemia.

Interestingly, the obesity was not primarily due to increased food intake, which is the cause in most genetic forms of obesity. Instead, we observed reduced sympathetic activity and energy expenditure.

To summarize: maternal Gαs mutations cause severe obesity, insulin-resistant diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Data I will not show demonstrate that reversal of Gαs imprinting reverses this metabolic phenotype. The phenotype presumably results from severe Gαs deficiency in one or more tissues through the combined effect of a mutation on the active maternal allele and tissue-specific silencing of the paternal allele.

We created a Gαs-specific conditional allele to produce tissue-specific knockouts. The one site in which we could replicate the parent-of-origin obesity phenotype was the central nervous system. We used Nestin-Cre to disrupt Gαs throughout the CNS. These were heterozygous mice; we knocked out either the maternal or paternal allele, not both alleles.

When the maternal allele was disrupted in the CNS, the mice developed severe obesity and insulin-resistant diabetes. This was associated primarily with reduced sympathetic activity and energy expenditure, reduced diet-induced thermogenesis, cold intolerance, impaired brown adipose tissue activation, and lower heart rate and blood pressure. Food intake, at least initially, was normal, and body length was normal.

### Central melanocortin system

I want to briefly introduce the central melanocortin system. POMC neurons are located in the hindbrain and in the arcuate nucleus. They act downstream of leptin and project to the paraventricular nucleus, or PVN, as well as to many other hypothalamic and extrahypothalamic regions. In simple terms, they tell the body to stop eating and burn more calories.

These actions are mediated primarily by the melanocortin 4 receptor, MC4R, a classic Gαs–cyclic AMP-coupled receptor. MC4R mutations in mice and humans are the most common cause of monogenic early-onset severe obesity. They have been associated with increased linear growth, hyperphagia, reduced energy expenditure, insulin resistance, and hypercholesterolemia.

Brad Lowell and colleagues took MC4R-null mice and selectively reactivated MC4R in SIM1 neurons using Sim1-Cre. They could essentially reverse the effect on food intake but not the effect on energy expenditure. They concluded that the major melanocortin action on food intake occurs in the PVN, whereas the action on energy expenditure occurs at sites outside the PVN. Many subsequent studies have supported this conclusion.

When we administered the MC4R agonist melanotan II, or MTII, to mice with CNS Gαs disruption, the drug's effect on food intake was largely unaffected. Its ability to stimulate energy expenditure, however, was impaired in mice with the maternally inherited mutation. This was not seen in mice with a paternal mutation.

### Human PHP1A data

These findings could be relevant to patients with pseudohypoparathyroidism. A 1999 study measured fasting plasma norepinephrine as a readout of sympathetic activity in lean controls, children with nonsyndromic obesity, and children with PHP1A. Children with obesity generally had lower norepinephrine, but the PHP1A group had an additional reduction of approximately 50%, suggesting reduced sympathetic activity.

A study from Ashley Shoemaker's group showed lower energy expenditure in children with PHP. One caveat is that the analysis used fat-free mass or calculated expected energy expenditure. Because these children do not have typical body habitus, I am not sure how useful that comparison necessarily is.

We studied a small group of young adults matched to controls for age, sex, and percentage fat mass. The patients with PHP1A were highly insulin resistant. We could not detect a difference in energy expenditure, although the sample was very small.

So far, I have said that CNS-specific disruption of the maternal Gαs allele produces a severe metabolic phenotype with reduced energy expenditure, obesity, and insulin-resistant diabetes, implicating Gαs imprinting at one or more CNS sites. Gαs deficiency impairs melanocortin-stimulated sympathetic activity and energy expenditure without affecting food intake, suggesting that melanocortins mediate their effects through both Gαs-dependent and Gαs-independent pathways.

### Dorsomedial hypothalamus

We made many knockouts targeting different parts of the brain or different neuronal cell types. We could not reproduce the parent-of-origin obesity phenotype until we disrupted Gαs in the dorsomedial hypothalamus, or DMH.

Because there was no suitable genetic Cre line, we did this by stereotaxically injecting an adeno-associated virus expressing Cre into the DMH. We first confirmed molecularly that Gαs is imprinted in the DMH. Heterozygous loss of the maternal Gαs allele led to obesity, whereas heterozygous loss of the paternal allele did not.

I will not go through all aspects of the paper. The first column shows the maternal brain knockout, and the second shows the maternal DMH knockout. The phenotypes were very similar, especially regarding fat mass and energy expenditure, although there were differences in glucose metabolism and cold tolerance.

We think the phenotype is mediated at least partly by disruption of melanocortin action. A DMH-specific MC4R knockout produced a phenotype almost identical to loss of the maternal Gαs allele in the DMH.

I do not think MC4R is the only receptor involved. Another paper showed that disrupting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor, GIPR, signaling in the DMH produces a similar phenotype. GIPR is another important receptor coupled to Gαs. Multiple neurotransmitters or hormones could therefore be affected in the DMH.

Using phosphorylated CREB as a readout, we showed reduced MC4R signaling in the DMH knockout.

### Gαq/11 signaling in the PVN

For the last part, we will move away from Gαs and discuss something somewhat beyond pseudohypoparathyroidism. MC4R is classically coupled to Gαs, but loss of Gαs mainly affected energy expenditure, not food intake or linear growth.

We hypothesized that MC4R might mediate its effects on food intake and linear growth in the paraventricular nucleus through Gαq/11. Gαq and Gα11 are two closely related, ubiquitously expressed G proteins. Their principal downstream signals involve phospholipase C, intracellular calcium, diacylglycerol, and inositol trisphosphate. We thought this pathway might act at least partly by regulating the transcription factor SIM1, which is critical for the overeating phenotype. Loss of SIM1 in humans or mice causes increased linear growth, hyperphagia, and obesity.

Our first model deleted both Gαq and Gα11 specifically in the PVN. The mice developed massive obesity and increased body length. Unlike the Gαs models, their energy expenditure and sympathetic activity were unaffected. They were hyperphagic and had no primary defect in glucose metabolism.

When we administered an MC4R agonist, we observed the opposite result from the Gαs knockout. The agonist's ability to inhibit food intake was lost or reduced, while its ability to stimulate energy expenditure was unaffected.

We then injected MTII directly into the PVN. Food intake was acutely inhibited in controls, but this effect was lost in the PVN Gαq/11 knockout. A PVN Gαs knockout did not affect the ability of melanocortins to inhibit food intake.

We also had a useful control: the Gαq/11 knockout did not affect the MTII-induced increase in blood pressure, which appears to be a Gαs-mediated response. In the PVN Gαs knockout, baseline blood pressure was lower and the pressor response to MTII was lost.

Thus, different physiologic effects of melanocortins within the PVN are mediated by different G protein pathways. Whether the pathways operate in the same cells remains unclear.

SIM1 expression was lower at baseline in the PVN Gαq/11 knockout and was not induced by the melanocortin agonist. CRH expression showed a similar pattern. In fact, these mice had central adrenal insufficiency.

Other studies have also suggested that canonical Gαs signaling may not be the most critical component of MC4R-mediated body-weight regulation. A study by Sadaf Farooqi and colleagues concluded that 12% or less of variance in adiposity was correlated with canonical MC4R–Gαs signaling.

Another supporting observation is that many naturally occurring MC4R variants associated with severe early-onset obesity—almost half in some series—retain relatively normal cyclic AMP signaling when expressed in cells.

### MC4R F51L knock-in model

To study this in more detail, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate a mouse carrying a naturally occurring MC4R missense variant, F51L.

We first examined the acute ability of an MC4R agonist to stimulate Gαs signaling. In the PVN, using CREB phosphorylation as a readout, the response was intact. The mutant mice had increased baseline phosphorylated CREB after saline. We think this is because F51L does not eliminate Gαs signaling. These mice are obese and therefore have increased leptinergic tone, which by definition produces greater melanocortinergic tone; that may explain the higher baseline signal.

The F51L mice developed severe obesity and a small increase in linear growth. Linear-regression analyses of body weight against food intake or energy expenditure indicated that the primary defect was in food intake: the mice ate too much rather than burning substantially fewer calories.

When we administered an MC4R agonist, F51L disrupted the ability of melanocortin signaling to inhibit food intake, as in the PVN Gαq/11 knockout. It did not affect the response in energy expenditure, heart rate, or blood pressure.

With one exception, the metabolic phenotype of the MC4R F51L mouse was almost identical to that of the PVN Gαq/11 knockout.

### Pharmacologic inhibition of Gαq/11

We then stereotaxically administered the selective Gαq/11 inhibitor FR900359, formerly called UBO-QIC, to normal mice. The cell-based experiment on the left shows that the compound effectively inhibited Gαq/11-dependent IP1 generation.

Injection of FR900359 into the PVN completely reversed MTII's ability to inhibit food intake. FR900359 alone did not affect food intake, and it did not affect the MTII-induced increase in heart rate, which is presumed to be Gαs-mediated. This provides additional evidence that the food-intake response is mediated through Gαq/11.

### MC4R, MRAP2, and signaling bias

Finally, we studied signaling by the F51L variant in cells. MC4R has an accessory protein, melanocortin receptor accessory protein 2, or MRAP2, that is important for full MC4R function.

The solid bars show that MRAP2 increased the potency and maximal response of MC4R-mediated cyclic AMP signaling. F51L produced a small shift in potency or magnitude, but its maximal cyclic AMP response was similar and largely intact.

By contrast, IP1, a readout of Gαq/11 signaling, showed a marked defect. Even with MRAP2, there was essentially no signal except at the highest α-MSH concentration of 10−4 M. The F51L variant therefore produced a clear defect in IP1/Gαq/11 signaling. ERK signaling was more or less intact, making this a relatively selective Gαq/11 signaling defect.

We have studied approximately 12 additional MC4R variants. So far, we have seen a similar pattern in perhaps two or three. It is not universal, but F51L is not the only example. More work is needed.

### Integrated model and therapeutic implications

I will conclude with an overview of what we think is happening. Remember that I am discussing an imprinted phenotype and the effect of heterozygous Gαs disruption. This does not mean that a homozygous Gαs knockout has no major effects. In fact, homozygous Gαs knockout in the DMH produced a much more severe obesity phenotype because Gαs mediates effects of GLP-1 and other MC4R actions.

We think MC4R works through Gαs at sites including the DMH to regulate energy expenditure, brown adipose tissue activity, and heart rate. Other Gαs-coupled receptors, including GLP-1R, may contribute. Gαq/11 may also mediate metabolic effects through receptors such as bombesin receptor subtype 3, BRS3, which my colleague Marc Reitman at NIDDK has studied.

In the PVN or PVH, Gαs mediates cardiovascular functions, whereas most actions on food intake and linear growth appear to occur through Gαq/11 and SIM1. This pathway also affects hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity.

This may be therapeutically important. If we can develop a Gαq-biased MC4R agonist, it might provide a potent satiety drug without some of the cardiovascular and other adverse effects associated with MC4R agonists.

I will close by thanking everyone involved, particularly Min Chen and Hui Sun, who have participated in almost all these projects; Oksana Gavrilova, who performed many of the metabolic studies; and the other group members and clinical collaborators listed on the slide. I will stop and take questions. Thank you.
