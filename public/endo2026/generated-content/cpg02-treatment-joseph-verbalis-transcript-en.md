## Introduction and clinical case

Over to the next speaker, Joe Verbalis.

Dr. Christ-Crain has told you how we recommend diagnosing or ruling out AVP deficiency. It is now my pleasure to tell you how we will recommend treating AVP deficiency.

To make the guideline clinically relevant for clinicians who need to diagnose or rule out AVP deficiency, we are using a case-based approach. You saw that in Dr. Christ-Crain's case. This is my case, involving a patient whom I followed for many years.

A 37-year-old woman presented with polyuria and polydipsia that began after an uncomplicated delivery 5 years earlier. Her principal complaint was that she could not sleep because nocturia woke her 4–5 times each night.

Her baseline evaluation, as described by Dr. Christ-Crain, showed a serum sodium concentration of 147 mmol/L, plasma osmolality of 302 mOsm/kg H₂O, and urine osmolality of 95 mOsm/kg H₂O. She had primary hypothyroidism, with a TSH of 5.6, but her other pituitary functions were normal.

Under the algorithm you have just seen, no further testing is needed to make the diagnosis. A serum sodium concentration of 147 mmol/L with urine osmolality of 95 mOsm/kg qualifies for a diagnosis of AVP deficiency. Neither an overnight water-deprivation test nor stimulation testing is necessary.

Her MRI, which does need to be performed, showed a normal pituitary gland and stalk but an absent posterior pituitary bright spot. The diagnosis from the start of the evaluation was AVP deficiency, probably caused by lymphocytic infundibuloneurohypophysitis, based on the postpartum onset, her sex, and the time course of the polyuria and polydipsia.

## Audience poll: initial treatment

This leads to the question: In this 37-year-old woman with clearly hypotonic polyuria, what would you do next for management?

A. Perform overnight water deprivation to confirm AVP deficiency.  
B. Start desmopressin 10 µg intranasally twice daily.  
C. Start desmopressin 0.1 mg orally or, if sublingual wafers are available, 60 µg sublingually 1 hour before bedtime.  
D. Give prednisone 40 mg orally for 10 days to treat presumed hypophysitis.

Almost all of you selected the appropriate initial treatment. Another confirmatory test is unnecessary. We would not begin with the higher desmopressin dose of 10 µg intranasally twice daily. We certainly do not need to treat hypophysitis that is long past, since the MRI is normal apart from the absent posterior pituitary bright spot.

The correct answer is the lowest desmopressin dose: 0.1 mg orally or 60 µg sublingually. Because her main complaint is nocturia, the optimal timing is 1 hour before bedtime.

## Core desmopressin recommendations

We **recommend**, rather than merely suggest, treating patients with confirmed AVP deficiency with a selective AVP V2-receptor agonist: desmopressin.

At this meeting, you have heard about many new drugs for acromegaly and many new GLP-1 drugs for obesity and diabetes. Here, we do not need a new drug. Desmopressin has been an almost ideal designer drug since it was first synthesized in 1957. Its duration is longer than that of AVP, and it is selective for the renal V2 receptor. It remains the treatment of choice for AVP deficiency.

We recommend starting desmopressin as a single daily dose 1 hour before sleep. The primary initial goal is to let the patient sleep through the night without excessive urination, unless nocturia has another underlying cause.

We suggest—and this is less strong than “recommend”—carefully titrating the desmopressin dose for each patient to avoid its most feared complication, hyponatremia.

## Desmopressin formulations, starting doses, and duration

Several preparations are available. All are effective treatments for AVP deficiency; they differ mainly in the duration of their antidiuretic effect.

- Parenteral desmopressin may be given intravenously, subcutaneously, or intramuscularly. The formulation shown is 4 µg/mL, with a starting dose of 0.25–1 µg. Bioavailability is 100%, the half-life is approximately 2.8 hours, and the antidiuretic effect lasts 8–12 hours.
- Intranasal desmopressin is supplied as 0.1 mg/mL, or 10 µg per dose, with a usual starting dose of 10 µg. Bioavailability is approximately 5%–10%, elimination is biphasic, and the antidiuretic effect lasts 8–12 hours.
- Oral tablets are available in 100 µg and 200 µg strengths, with a starting dose of 50–100 µg. Bioavailability is below 1%, the half-life is approximately 1.5–2.5 hours, and the antidiuretic effect lasts 6–8 hours. An oral liquid formulation has recently become available.
- Sublingual wafers are available in 60 µg, 120 µg, and 240 µg strengths, with a starting dose of 60 µg. Bioavailability is approximately 1%, the half-life is approximately 1.5–2.5 hours, and the antidiuretic effect lasts 6–8 hours.

Shorter duration generally means that dosing will be required more frequently.

## Clinical course: controlling nocturia

In my patient, symptoms improved, but nocturia decreased only from 4–5 times per night to 2–3 times. That was better, but not good enough. The oral desmopressin dose was increased to 0.2 mg 1 hour before bedtime.

The first goal remains control of nighttime nocturia, so an initial dose increase should be made by increasing the nighttime dose rather than adding daytime doses. Her nocturia then decreased to 1–2 times per night, but this was still inadequate.

At that point, she was switched to desmopressin 10 µg intranasally 1 hour before bedtime. Its longer antidiuretic effect was more likely to last throughout the night, and nocturia was eliminated.

One week after starting intranasal desmopressin, her serum sodium concentration was 142 mmol/L and plasma osmolality was 292 mOsm/kg H₂O—both completely normal.

This leads to our monitoring recommendation: after starting desmopressin or changing its dose, we suggest checking serum sodium within 5–7 days unless clinical indicators or symptoms warrant earlier measurement. A patient should not remain for too long on a changed dose without confirmation that serum sodium remains within a safe range.

## Higher doses primarily extend antidiuretic duration

When increasing the dose, remember one central principle: the main effect is not greater short-term efficacy but a longer duration of antidiuresis.

The modeled concentration-time graph shows oral, sublingual, intravenous, and intranasal preparations. The black line at 5 ng/mL represents a desmopressin concentration producing significant, although not maximal, antidiuresis. Any usual preparation initially rises above that threshold and therefore produces good antidiuresis. With oral and sublingual formulations, the level may fall below the threshold after approximately 4–6 hours, whereas intranasal and parenteral formulations last longer.

A typical intranasal 10 µg dose—one spray—maintains the threshold for approximately 8 hours, with interindividual variability. Increasing the dose raises the peak concentration, but the initial antidiuresis is not meaningfully greater: urine cannot be concentrated beyond its physiological maximum, around 800 mOsm/kg. The higher peak instead extends the duration, potentially into the 10-, 12-, or sometimes 14-hour range.

Thus, increasing the desmopressin dose primarily prolongs its antidiuretic effect.

## Clinical course: adding daytime control

The patient's nocturia resolved, but she continued to report daytime polyuria. Intranasal desmopressin was therefore increased to 10 µg twice daily. There was no need to double the nighttime dose because nocturia was already controlled; she instead needed a daytime dose for daytime polyuria.

One week after this increase, serum sodium was 139 mmol/L and plasma osmolality was 288 mOsm/kg H₂O.

We recommend that the treatment goals in AVP deficiency are to restore and maintain adequate fluid and electrolyte balance while optimizing quality of life. This includes reducing urine output to the normal range for people without AVP deficiency.

## Long-term follow-up and intermittent aquaresis

I followed this patient for 3 years. Serum electrolytes and pituitary function remained normal. Annual pituitary MRIs were unchanged, and she reported complete normalization of her usual lifestyle.

After 3 years of intranasal desmopressin, however, routine laboratory testing showed a serum sodium concentration of 131 mmol/L and plasma osmolality of 271 mOsm/kg H₂O. These values were low.

The patient was advised to delay a desmopressin dose until breakthrough polyuria—and sometimes polydipsia—occurred, and to do this once weekly. We have discussed the terminology. I have called it “desmopressin escape,” while committee members use different terms, and we have not yet decided what to call it in the final guideline. The terms all describe the same principle: interrupting or delaying a dose so that antidiuresis ends and an aquaresis occurs.

We recommend building desmopressin treatment interruptions or delays into treatment protocols to lower the incidence of treatment-associated hyponatremia by permitting breakthrough polyuria.

## Evidence for desmopressin interruption

Many of us have used this approach for years, but its importance was demonstrated by an international survey of more than 1,000 patients with AVP deficiency led by Cihan Atila and other committee members.

Patients who routinely omitted or delayed desmopressin one or more times per week reported fewer hospitalizations for hyponatremia. Compared with patients who were unaware of the strategy, the odds ratio was 0.55 (95% CI, 0.39–0.77; p=0.0006). Compared with patients who knew about the strategy but did not use it because it was inconvenient, the odds ratio was 0.46 (95% CI, 0.31–0.69; p=0.0002).

Among patients performing desmopressin escape, 22% reported hospitalization for hyponatremia, compared with 34% among those unaware of the strategy and 38% among those aware but not performing it. Advising patients to interrupt or delay desmopressin can therefore reduce future severe hyponatremia requiring hospitalization.

The patient agreed to do this more regularly and remained stable for 10 years. Follow-up was reduced to annual visits and laboratory testing.

## Severe hyponatremia during travel

During a long summer road trip across the continental United States, the patient was admitted to a hospital in Colorado with a serum sodium concentration of 119 mmol/L.

She was not severely symptomatic. She felt unwell and complained of a new headache but had no focal neurological signs or symptoms. The emergency physician called to ask how the hyponatremia should be managed.

The hospital used 3% saline to raise her sodium to a safer concentration. It was not absolutely required because she did not have severe neurological symptoms, but it was an acceptable choice. She was then placed on fluid restriction. Desmopressin was not stopped. Serum sodium was safely increased to 135 mmol/L over 48 hours, and she continued her trip.

This leads to another recommendation: we recommend checking serum or plasma sodium frequently in patients with AVP deficiency who feel unwell, even when the illness or symptoms appear unrelated to AVP deficiency. The symptoms may be nonspecific. This patient simply felt that something was wrong and had a new headache. Going to the emergency department and measuring serum sodium were appropriate.

## Hyponatremia risk and changing clinical circumstances

Hyponatremia is the most common adverse effect of desmopressin treatment. Reported prevalence is approximately 41%–46%, of which about 14%–15% is significant or severe hyponatremia, defined in the presented datasets by sodium below approximately 125–130 mmol/L.

Monitoring should become more frequent, at least initially, when the clinical situation changes because of a new comorbidity or a medication change.

For example, desmopressin is cleared by the kidneys rather than metabolized by the liver. If a patient develops chronic kidney disease or another illness that reduces kidney function, desmopressin's duration of action may increase. Serum sodium should then be monitored more frequently.

During travel in hot climates, increased fluid consumption may be needed to compensate for evaporative and sweat losses. In clinical experience, however—particularly among marathon and ultramarathon participants—people commonly overestimate sweat loss and overdrink in an attempt to compensate. This can cause hyponatremia, as it did in this patient.

## Managing desmopressin-associated hyponatremia

The first step is to assess the severity of hyponatremia and the patient's symptoms. Severe neurological symptoms require prompt treatment with 3% hypertonic saline, irrespective of the underlying cause.

Less symptomatic patients with mild-to-moderate hyponatremia—approximately 126–134 mmol/L—can be managed more conservatively. This may include reducing the desmopressin dose or prolonging the dosing interval.

In severe symptomatic cases, do not simply stop desmopressin completely while giving hypertonic saline. Complete withdrawal can produce a large aquaresis and dangerously rapid sodium correction, with a risk of osmotic demyelination. Desmopressin can instead be continued or adjusted to control free-water excretion while sodium is corrected.

## Fluid intake after discharge

On further questioning, the patient admitted that she had drunk far more than usual during the road trip because of extreme heat and fear of dehydration. She developed hyponatremia not because the prescribed desmopressin dose itself was excessive, but because she consumed excessive fluid while antidiuresis was maintained.

After discharge, she continued the trip with instructions to drink only when thirsty. She remained normonatremic for many years afterward, so this case had a happy ending.

For most patients, fluid intake should be governed by thirst. They should drink with meals—prandial drinking—or when thirsty, rather than trying to maximize fluid intake in advance to prevent dehydration. Fluid loading, especially in hot environments, is more likely to cause hyponatremia.

## Older adults and reduced thirst

An important exception is the patient with reduced thirst, including patients with adipsic AVP deficiency and many older adults.

We do not like to use the term “elderly” now, but thirst decreases with age. How old depends on the individual, but this is certainly relevant above age 70 and may become relevant above age 65.

This was demonstrated well in an Australian study by Phillips and colleagues. Older and younger participants underwent water restriction and were then given unrestricted access to water. Despite similar deprivation, the older participants reached a higher serum sodium concentration. When allowed to drink, they lowered their sodium, but less than the younger group because their fluid intake in response to dehydration was significantly smaller.

Older patients should generally drink according to thirst, but in situations where dehydration is likely, they may need advice to drink more liberally because their thirst response may be insufficient to normalize fluid and electrolyte balance.

## Prolonged fasting and Ramadan

My final slide concerns prolonged fasting. This illustrates why we want feedback from the audience during guideline presentations.

We did not include this issue during the first presentation in Prague. An audience member from the Middle East pointed out that Ramadan creates a particular problem because patients abstain from drinking as well as eating during daylight hours. If AVP deficiency is not well controlled during the day, prolonged fasting places the patient at risk of severe hypernatremia.

Water taken with oral medication breaks the Ramadan fast. Therefore, patients observing the fast may need to switch from oral desmopressin to a longer-acting preparation, such as intranasal or subcutaneous desmopressin.

Increasing the pre-Suhur desmopressin dose can prolong the antidiuretic effect. A 10 µg intranasal dose—one spray—may last about 8 hours and often will not cover the entire daylight period. Increasing the dose to 20 µg—two sprays, one in each nostril—can extend the duration sufficiently to cover more of the fasting period.

Patients should also limit outdoor activity in hot environments to reduce insensible water loss.

Whether intranasal desmopressin breaks the Ramadan fast is a matter of religious interpretation. Some Islamic authorities do not consider intranasal administration to break the fast, whereas others may. Patients should consult their religious authorities.

If two intranasal sprays are given in the morning before the fast, their effect may last sufficiently to avoid another dose during fasting hours. If these strategies cannot adequately reduce aquaresis, the patient should seek a medical exemption from fasting-related fluid restriction.

This is what we believe is the best advice for prolonged fasting, particularly during Ramadan.
