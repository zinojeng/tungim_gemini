I would like to start by thanking Ana Pinheiro Machado Canton and Ana Paula Abreu, and the whole organizing committee, for this invitation to discuss with you the role of reproductive biomarkers in boys with suspected hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, either during childhood or at the age of pubertal onset.

I have no conflicts of interest to declare related to this presentation, and so we are switching to the pediatric area now.

And I thought, since the audience is broad, that I should start by reviewing the developmental physiology of the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis, and then discuss male hypogonadism and its definition and classification in childhood and adolescence, before talking about the use of biomarkers in central, or hypogonadotropic, hypogonadism.

### Developmental physiology of the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis

So this is what we all learn at university, the typical hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis or testicular axis, but this is the adult axis.

So there are very important changes in the physiology of the axis during the lifespan, with essentially three waves of activation in the male.

One is during fetal life, then after birth for about six months, and then at puberty, with childhood being the period in which the axis is mostly quiescent.

Therefore, the informative biomarkers change depending on the age of the patient.

And particularly during childhood, I hope I will convince you that it is not testosterone or LH, but Sertoli cell markers and FSH, which are most useful.

Given the developmental physiology of the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis, the clinical outcomes differ according to when hypogonadism is established. Very early fetal testicular function is independent of fetal pituitary gonadotropins but dependent on placental hCG; if hypogonadism is established then, we may have XY patients with ambiguous or female genitalia.

If hypogonadism is established later, patients will have male genitalia but different clinical presentations. Late fetal or early postnatal onset may produce micropenis or cryptorchidism; onset during childhood may be clinically unnoticed; and the presentation at pubertal age may be absent or delayed puberty. I'll discuss that further on.

I will not discuss, of course, features in the adult.

The main difference between the prepubertal and pubertal stages of the axis is that before puberty there is no, or almost no, gonadotropin secretion by the pituitary, whereas typical pulsatile gonadotropin secretion is established during puberty.

But there are differences also in the testicular histology.

As we all learn at medical school, the mature adult testis has large seminiferous tubules with lumina; germ cells are the main component of the seminiferous tubules, and typical Leydig cells are present in the interstitium.

This is completely different during childhood, before puberty, where seminiferous tubules are much smaller, there is no lumen, and they are mainly populated by Sertoli cells with only a few premeiotic spermatogonia and no typical Leydig cells in the interstitium.

### Testicular growth at puberty

So, what happens during puberty?

So, the testes are small before pubertal onset, and of course, pediatricians know this very well.

I apologize, but given the broad audience, I will discuss this a little bit more for those who are not completely acquainted with this.

The testes are small before puberty, and their volume is mainly represented by Sertoli cells, whereas the large increase in testicular volume during puberty is due to full spermatogenesis—that is, germ cells.

Now, all this starts with FSH inducing Sertoli cell proliferation, immature Sertoli cell proliferation before pubertal onset, which makes the testicular volume increase slowly from 2 to 3 to 4 mL.

Then we clinically say this is puberty.

LH acting on Leydig cells induces the increase of intratesticular testosterone, not yet serum testosterone, but intratesticular testosterone, which makes Sertoli cells mature.

When Sertoli cells mature, they stop proliferating.

Then AMH is inhibited by intratesticular testosterone, and inhibin B increases.

Of course, germ cells start to proliferate and enter meiosis, and the testicular volume increases from 4 to 6 to 8 mL, and then to 20–25 mL.

Serum testosterone increase is a rather late feature of male puberty.

### Androgen-receptor expression and AMH regulation

So now, why do the fetal testis and the postnatal testis during the first six months, despite exposure to high levels of intratesticular testosterone, not produce full spermatogenesis or downregulate AMH?

Well, this, of course, during puberty we have high levels of testosterone, and all Sertoli cells have androgen receptor expression.

During childhood, Sertoli cells already have androgen receptor expression, but testosterone is low or completely absent.

What happens before that—during fetal life and mini-puberty, the first three to six months of postnatal life—is that Sertoli cells do not express the androgen receptor, although peritubular cells do.

So Sertoli cells are physiologically androgen insensitive and remain immature for about one year after birth.

And the mechanisms underlying downregulation of AMH by testosterone were studied by a group some time ago.

I do not have time to go into details, but just to mention that the androgen receptor bound to testosterone, or DHT—but mainly testosterone—competes with transcription factors that upregulate AMH, such as SF-1.

In the absence of this negative effect of testosterone on AMH secretion, or the positive effect of testosterone on Sertoli-cell maturation, FSH acting on immature Sertoli cells provokes an increase in AMH production.

This is also seen at the level of Sertoli-cell proliferation. Our group studied the underlying molecular mechanisms for a long time, and it is mainly the typical FSH receptor-mediated action through cyclic AMP that makes the testes produce more AMH in response to FSH during the prepubertal period.

### AMH in pediatric practice

A number of observations in the literature showed, for example, that work by Débora Braslavsky in our hospital found AMH below the normal range for boys born with congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism associated with multiple pituitary deficiencies.

There were similar studies by different European groups showing that, in boys with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, AMH and inhibin B were low and increased with FSH treatment.

In collaboration with Jacques Young in Paris, we also showed that, in previously untreated adolescents or young boys with congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, AMH was below the normal range for a prepubertal boy. It increased with FSH alone and then mimicked normal puberty when hCG was added, producing an increase in testosterone and therefore inhibition of AMH production.

So, now going to the clinical features, why should we expect micropenis or cryptorchidism, but otherwise normally virilized male genitalia, in patients born with congenital hypogonadism?

We just have to remember the physiology of the fetal period: during the second and third trimesters, testosterone induces growth of the external genitalia and testicular descent.

### Male hypogonadism and age-specific biomarkers

So, you can imagine that the typical definition of hypogonadism in the adult does not fit pediatric patients, because testosterone and LH are normally undetectable, particularly during childhood.

So, when we think of male hypogonadism in pediatrics, we should think of a disorder characterized by impaired hormone secretion, particularly the Sertoli-cell hormones AMH and inhibin B.

Of course, the rest will apply later.

And so the biomarkers, as I mentioned before, will be different according to the ages.

### Self-limited delayed puberty versus congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism

The main problem pediatric endocrinologists face in a boy aged, let's say, 12 to 14 years is distinguishing self-limited delayed puberty from congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

So, self-limited delayed puberty is by far the most common etiology, let's say, the most common cause.

Approximately three-quarters of these patients will have self-limited delayed puberty, a physiologic condition.

Functional central hypogonadism might be easier to diagnose; it is a reversible situation caused by various chronic or acute conditions.

Of course, when the hypogonadism is primary, it would be easy to diagnose because gonadotropins would be high.

But the problem, as I mentioned, is congenital central hypogonadism, especially when it is isolated.

And why is this important?

Of course, because the self-limited delayed puberty is a normal variant.

It is transient, and watchful waiting is a possibility, whereas hypogonadism, of course, is a pathological condition which needs hormone treatment lifelong.

Some clinical observations may help. Adrenarche at a normal age and delayed bone age favor self-limited delayed puberty, whereas delayed teething or adrenarche, micropenis, cryptorchidism, hyposmia, or anosmia favor congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, including Kallmann syndrome.

So in these cases, some clinical situations might help, but hormone determination is essential.

There is an extensive review by Jennifer Harrington and Mark Palmert from a number of years ago, revisited 10 years later, showing that basal LH or testosterone, or even various tests using GnRH, cannot reliably distinguish all cases of self-limited delayed puberty from congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism because assays and cutoffs vary, and sensitivity and specificity are suboptimal.

### Prospective FSH–Sertoli-cell biomarker study

A number of years ago, with Romina Grinspon, we started from the hypothesis that AMH and inhibin B should be normal in boys with self-limited delayed puberty and low in those with congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

Prompted by Fernando Cassorla from the Universidad de Chile, we began this multicenter, multinational cohort study with Romina in 2008. It took us 16 years of prospective inclusion and follow-up of boys aged at least 13 years with a testicular volume below 4 mL.

We subsequently incorporated younger colleagues and fellows, including Sebastián Castro and Lourdes Correa Brito.

We did finally a nested case-control study of all these boys.

Thirty-two ultimately had a diagnosis of congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism because testicular volume remained below 4 mL at age 18. Thirty-three had self-limited delayed puberty, with testicular volume reaching at least 15 mL by age 18 or at least four years after testicular volume had reached 4 mL.

So long follow-up to have a definitive diagnosis.

And the aim was to validate biomarkers for the differential diagnosis.

During this time, a number of papers appeared in the literature showing low values of AMH and/or inhibin B in boys with congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

So, as expected, the area under the curve for LH was not satisfactory.

But look at this: FSH had a very nice area under the curve.

AMH and inhibin B also performed well. Even better, if you multiply FSH by AMH or FSH by inhibin B, the area under the curve was almost 1, with very satisfactory diagnostic performance. FSH × AMH and FSH × inhibin B had diagnostic efficacy of 93% and 94%, respectively, with similarly high positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity, indicating that FSH–Sertoli-cell biomarkers are highly diagnostic for differentiating between

### Congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism in childhood and infancy

We then extended this study to prepubertal boys aged 1 to 8 years who had at least two of three findings—micropenis, cryptorchidism, or microorchidism—and in whom CHH could ultimately be confirmed by low FSH, LH, and testosterone during mini-puberty.

Of course, you will ask me why if you already have the diagnosis.

This is a validation study, so we needed validated biomarkers.

Alternatively, CHH could be confirmed by a genetic study in the patient or a first-degree relative. We followed these patients as in the previous study.

And as you can see here, LH and testosterone were in all cases within the normal range because the normal range is almost undetectable during childhood.

In these cases, FSH was not as informative, but once again AMH and FSH × AMH were low, indicating that AMH is low in boys aged 1 to 8 years with congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

The LH–Leydig-cell axis is not informative.

There is a very recent French multicenter study showing that, as was already known during mini-puberty, these markers are low.

But in boys aged 6 to 12 months with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, AMH and inhibin B are low.

So we complete the whole prepubertal spectrum: mini-puberty, the first year of life, and ages 1 to 8 years, during which Sertoli-cell biomarkers are essential.

### Genetic phenotyping

What about phenotyping?

And I will finish on this.

We recently performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of boys referred for delayed puberty in whom a sequence variant was found in genes related to hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

The systematic review yielded 775 patients with 1,001 variants in 93 genes, but we reclassified these variants according to ACMG/AMP recommendations, and the number was significantly reduced.

Among 497 subjects with 503 bona fide disease-causing variants in 29 genes for hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, the red flags were described in fewer than 30% of patients. This leads us to say that the absence of cryptorchidism, micropenis, or microorchidism should not rule out the diagnosis of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism in boys.

### Conclusions

So, to conclude, during infancy and childhood, Sertoli cells are the major testicular cell population, and their biomarkers are AMH and inhibin B, together with FSH—not LH and testosterone—during this period of life. Male hypogonadotropic hypogonadism is characterized by low levels of FSH and inhibin B or AMH, which are highly predictive of congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

During childhood, AMH or inhibin B are low in patients with congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and as I said, LH is not informative.

Concerning the anatomic phenotype, the absence of micropenis, cryptorchidism, or other red flags should not rule out the diagnosis.

With this, I would like to thank our collaborators: Fernando Cassorla and his group at the Universidad de Chile, Yee-Ming Chan at Boston Children's Hospital, and Marcelo Martí at the Universidad de Buenos Aires.

Thank you very much for your attention.
