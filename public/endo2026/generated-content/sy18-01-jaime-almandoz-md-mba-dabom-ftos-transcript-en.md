### Moving beyond weight-centric treatment

That is really foreshadowing what we should be paying attention to in obesity care, because we are moving past the older paradigm of behavioral interventions for obesity that are heavily weight-centric, beyond cardiometabolic outcomes, to those that include multi-organ health, function, and quality of life. It is overly basic to ask a question such as, “How much weight loss can we achieve?” Rather, how can we more precisely improve health outcomes across organ systems affected by adiposity while also preserving or preventing against collateral damage?

The challenge remains that many of the patients I see in Dallas come in requesting somewhere in the region of 30% body-weight reduction. We know that there is huge heterogeneity in what we can achieve with currently available therapies, not just in terms of weight reduction, but also tolerability. Beyond that, what is the collateral damage from an unintended change in body composition? By unintended, I mean not desired and not what we would physiologically expect. There is tension in that conversation, and it is really coming up in clinic.

I will have patients present with a BMI of 40 and an A1C of 9% and say, “My primary care doctor is on my case to start one of these GLP-1s, but I am worried about my lean mass.” Hands up if you have had conversations like that recently. How are we going to address this?

When we look at the data that have been presented and nicely packaged, much of this looks at changes in fat-free mass versus fat mass, and it is measured by DXA. But what does that actually mean when we think of it in context? I want us to contextualize this as we are all still ingesting data from the ADA last week, where there is a mixture of DXA and MRI that may have been done from T9 down to the knees, but they are measuring different things. We cannot compare apples with oranges. The question is: do our patients understand that? Do the influencers informing them about therapies understand that too? This should enrich the conversations we have with our patients moving forward.

These are results from the DXA substudy within SURMOUNT-1, looking at tirzepatide in people without type 2 diabetes. Starting body-fat percentage, which is often how many of our patients think of things, was around 48.2% and dropped to about 40.8% at the end. That is a change in fat percentage of about 7.4 percentage points, with a relative increase in fat-free mass. That sounds good. But when we look at absolute reductions and changes, there is a loss of about 6 kilograms of lean mass. Is that bad? This is the conundrum we are in when we try to rationalize what is going on and how we might prevent it.

When people undergo obesity therapies or intentional weight loss, a variety of factors contribute beyond the calorie restriction that goes into it. As we look at growing indications for incretin-based and other therapies, including cardiovascular-risk reduction in people who are post-event, we have an inherently frail population—or implied frailty—who may be at risk for more dramatic weight reduction or adverse body-composition outcomes than we see in carefully selected clinical-trial populations.

### Activin–myostatin signaling as a therapeutic target

I do not expect any of you to read this figure. It shows the complexity of activin-receptor signaling and what that means for muscle. What we know is that activin and myostatin signaling through activin type II receptors regulates skeletal-muscle mass, and these downstream pathways lead to muscle loss. Inhibiting this upstream signaling leads to muscle growth and, theoretically, improves body composition with respect to lean mass. Multiple therapeutic approaches are currently in development, including ligand-, receptor-, and small interfering RNA-based strategies. This is the introduction to where I am going next in the talk.

In an overly simplistic way, this late in the afternoon: inhibit the myostatin system and increase muscle hypertrophy. In addition to other pathways, there is upregulation of GLUT4 receptors and change from white to brown adipose tissue. As we think simply about this, these pathways could theoretically increase energy expenditure, insulin sensitivity, and substrate utilization.

### INHBE and adipose-selective approaches

Before I wrap up, I am going to change pace and talk briefly about the inhibin subunit beta E gene, INHBE, and its downstream protein, Activin E, which acts on fat storage through the ALK7 receptor, also called ACVR1C. These are results from a genome-wide association study showing the association between loss-of-function variants in this gene and more favorable body composition, including waist-to-hip ratio adjusted for BMI. Other studies have shown a lower prevalence of cardiometabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes.

The theory is that Activin E acts through the ALK7 receptor to suppress adipose-tissue lipolysis. That is associated with increased abdominal adiposity, leading to obesity and cardiometabolic complications. Therefore, silencing INHBE and reducing Activin E levels increases adipose-tissue lipolysis, shrinks fat depots, and decreases abdominal adiposity, leading to preferential adipose reduction.

To highlight the associations, these are data from an ongoing study showing correlations between Activin E levels and adverse measures such as HOMA-IR, BMI, waist circumference, and waist-to-height ratio, stratified by diabetes status. You can see an association between higher Activin E levels and adverse outcomes.

With WVE-007, an INHBE-targeting GalNAc-conjugated small interfering RNA, these are results after a single 240 mg dose. At three and six months, on the far right, there were very small changes in body weight: approximately 0.3% and 0.9%, respectively. Waist circumference declined, reaching approximately 3.3% at six months. There were preferential reductions in visceral fat—approximately 14% at six months—and total fat, without a reduction in lean mass; lean mass increased by approximately 2%. We are seeing the possibility of long-lasting changes with a therapy that works in a very novel way compared with what we are accustomed to, not necessarily through overall weight reduction but through body recomposition, if you will.

Here are results from another phase 1/2a study in which tirzepatide 5 mg was given with placebo or different doses of ARO-INHBE. There were reductions in visceral adiposity at weeks 12 and 24. On the right, looking at the liver by MRI-PDFF, there were substantial reductions—up to approximately 46%—in liver fat with a single dose of this agent.

### Body composition, function, nutrition, and organ outcomes

This is a very exciting time to look at preferential loss of fat mass with preservation of lean mass, particularly because there is so much buzz about this among our patients. There is an evolving story regarding how concerned we need to be. I think the joke on the far right captures the environment: “I faced more peer pressure to eat protein as an adult than I did to take drugs in my teens.” Those of us who work in this space hear this every day.

Unfortunately, we are at risk of conflating body composition with function and metabolic health, and we need not do that. What we need from the clinical trials in development are meaningful functional outcomes. We need parallel, evidence-based nutrition guidelines to determine what is optimal so that people are not left to “protein max” on their own, which is likely worsening their adverse events during treatment and dose escalation. We need evidence-based resistance-training programs across the spectrum of functional ability in the patients we serve.

For clinicians, we also need practical, actionable assessment tools that can be incorporated into clinical workflow. If we are worried about loss of lean mass but do not have a good correlation with functional decline, should we be worried? We should assess things meaningful to our patients while recognizing that many clinical trials excluded people with significant functional impairment, so we are not going to see safety signals in those populations. We all see people who are inherently at higher risk. What can we incorporate into day-to-day office care that provides meaningful signals for when we should be concerned? Measures could include handgrip strength, knee-extension strength, gait speed, balance, timed up-and-go, and sit-to-stand testing.

There are many resources for providing nutrition support if you do not have readily available registered dietitians or colleagues with a nutrition interest. I was part of a really great project with wonderful colleagues to provide some guidance. But, of course, what we need is more evidence. This is expert opinion, not necessarily evidence-based guidance. Beyond that, we need to look past simple physical-activity and nutrition recommendations across the spectrum of treatment.

I like that many societies and groups are creating frameworks that do not just ask which agent will help a patient achieve the greatest weight reduction. In the new ADA Obesity Association Standards of Care for obesity pharmacotherapy, the focus is more organ-specific. As we saw with fat reduction in the liver, that might help contextualize treatment selection. But do we also need a parallel approach identifying treatments for people at risk for loss of lean mass? Who may benefit?

Potential future use cases include older adults, people with baseline functional impairment, people who lose lean mass with currently available therapies, those with sarcopenic-obesity phenotypes, and those with post-bariatric weight recurrence who may already have lost significant lean mass.

### Summary

My summary this afternoon is that muscle is emerging as a therapeutic target, but different pathways may improve the quality of weight loss through distinct mechanisms. Activin type II receptor- and myostatin-directed therapies can preserve or increase lean mass while enhancing fat loss. INHBE silencing represents a novel, adipose-selective approach that may reduce visceral fat with minimal impact on muscle.

Body composition alone is an incomplete endpoint. Strength, physical performance, and patient-centered outcomes matter more. The future of obesity care will focus on improving health, function, and organ-specific outcomes beyond simply maximizing weight loss. Thank you so much for your attention.

### Activin, follistatin, low-weight physiology, and target populations

**Discussant:** If I may add something along these lines, as someone who has studied these hormonal pathways for more than 25 years, the main effects are on muscle and bone. I will come back to this in a minute. But as the names imply, and where the field started, activin and follistatin also affect reproduction.

Going from normal to low weight through exercise, semaglutide treatment, anorexia nervosa, or starvation, the role of these pathways is complementary to leptin. Leptin goes down and blocks neuroendocrine function and reproduction. We should not procreate if we are starving. Activin and follistatin affect folliculogenesis and, on top of that, protect muscle and bone so that we are able to go and find food.

With current obesity treatments, we have 25% to 30% weight loss. That is where these medications may find a role. In a way, if I can say this, we are inducing a starvation-like state. We have to consider that exercise and high-protein diets work, but as we grow older, they work less. Older adults are therefore, I think, a focus and target group for the FDA and our colleagues in industry. This also allows us to avoid potential effects these medications may have on fertility. Those are my two cents along these lines: focus on older adults, protect bone and muscle, and avoid adverse effects on the reproductive axis. Sorry; this was not meant to be a lecture.
