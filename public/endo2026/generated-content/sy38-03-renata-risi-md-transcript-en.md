## Introduction

**Moderator:** We are going to move on to our last talk, given by Dr. Renata Risi from Sapienza University of Rome in Italy. The topic is “Fat Mass Excess and Abnormal Distribution in Type 1 Diabetes: Pathological and Clinical Consequences.”

**Renata Risi:** Thank you very much for the invitation. My task is to discuss fat-mass excess and distribution in type 1 diabetes.

## Increasing obesity prevalence and metabolic consequences

Why is this topic important? The previous speaker presented a meta-analysis involving more than 60,000 people worldwide. Obesity prevalence is increasing throughout the world and also among people with type 1 diabetes, who were classically regarded as lean. From 1980 to 2020, the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity reached approximately 30% among children with type 1 diabetes and 50% among adults. In our outpatient clinics, approximately one in three children and one in two adults with type 1 diabetes are affected by overweight or obesity. This is an important clinical challenge.

Obesity clusters with a worse metabolic profile in type 1 diabetes. Data from an Italian AMD Annals cohort involving more than 30,000 people in diabetes outpatient clinics showed that, in both men and women with type 1 diabetes, obesity was associated with older age, longer diabetes duration, worse glycemic and lipid profiles, a higher prevalence of hypertension, worse kidney function, and a greater prevalence of diabetes-related complications. These were cross-sectional, unadjusted data.

The previous speakers discussed how obesity may contribute to the pathogenesis of autoimmune diabetes. Autoimmune diabetes also has specific characteristics that may predispose people to weight gain: intensive insulin treatment and its associated peripheral hyperinsulinemia; frequent hypoglycemic events and fear of hypoglycemia, which can lead to maladaptive eating; continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion and newer technologies that permit a more relaxed lifestyle; and the difficult management of physical activity, which in some cases may contribute to sedentary behavior. These factors can increase body weight in addition to the usual drivers of obesity.

## The adiposopathy model and challenges in type 1 diabetes

To discuss adipose-tissue dysfunction in type 1 diabetes, we can begin with the better-characterized model in type 2 diabetes. In an obesogenic environment, adipose tissue expands to store excess lipid. When its expansion and storage capacity is exceeded, metabolic impairment develops. Lipid spills over toward immune cells, triggering meta-inflammation, and toward target organs such as the liver, heart, and pancreas. Together, these processes contribute to endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular disease.

Can we apply the same model to type 1 diabetes? There are several challenges.

First, autoimmune diabetes is heterogeneous. We cannot speak of only one form of type 1 diabetes. Different endotypes may differ in age at onset, genetic predisposition, immunologic features, C-peptide levels, and BMI. Higher BMI is classically more frequent in latent autoimmune diabetes in adults, or LADA.

Second, cardiovascular risk in type 1 diabetes may be driven by multiple factors, not only metabolic ones. Alongside obesity, insulin resistance, lipotoxicity, and traditional cardiovascular risk factors, type 1 diabetes has additional contributors: glucotoxicity, glycemic variability, hypoglycemia, diabetic nephropathy, and cardiac autoimmunity. These may all affect the relationship among adipose-tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk.

Third, adipose tissue may accumulate and distribute differently in type 1 diabetes, although evidence remains limited.

## Body composition and liver health across diabetes types

In a recently published study at Sapienza University, we enrolled 93 people with diabetes: 31 with type 1 diabetes, 31 with LADA, and 31 with type 2 diabetes. The groups were matched for age, sex, and BMI. Participants underwent dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, hepatic magnetic resonance imaging, and MR elastography.

At the same BMI, the autoimmune-diabetes groups had lower total fat mass and less visceral adiposity than the type 2 diabetes group, suggesting more favorable adipose-tissue distribution. Hepatic steatosis was present in 0% of the type 1 diabetes group, 3.2% of the LADA group, and 54.8% of the type 2 diabetes group. The autoimmune-diabetes groups also had lower liver stiffness.

When we examined predictors of liver health, visceral adiposity was significantly associated with steatosis and liver stiffness in type 2 diabetes, but not in autoimmune diabetes. This again suggests that fat distribution may differ in type 1 diabetes.

There is also limited evidence from comparisons with people without diabetes. A recent study using bioelectrical impedance analysis found that, in both males and females, type 1 diabetes was associated with lower body fat, lower estimated visceral adipose tissue, and a higher skeletal-muscle index.

We observed something similar in a small Sapienza cohort. Using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and bioelectrical impedance analysis, we compared people with type 1 diabetes with controls without diabetes. Participants with overweight or obesity were matched for age, sex, and BMI. The autoimmune-diabetes group had less visceral adipose tissue, lower fat mass, and greater muscle mass.

These findings require further investigation. Possible protective factors for adipose-tissue distribution in type 1 diabetes include a healthier lifestyle or stricter dietary adherence; the altered distribution of exogenous insulin, with relative portal hypoinsulinemia and peripheral hyperinsulinemia, which may reduce hepatic lipogenesis; the higher resting energy expenditure described by the previous speaker; and a possible role for autoimmunity within adipose tissue, which has not been adequately studied.

## Insulin resistance independent of adiposity

The fourth challenge is that insulin resistance may arise through different mechanisms in type 1 diabetes. Several studies have shown that insulin resistance in type 1 diabetes can be independent of adiposity.

One particularly comprehensive study enrolled 35 youth with type 1 diabetes and 22 controls. The groups were matched for age, BMI, pubertal stage, physical activity, visceral adiposity, and liver fat. Participants underwent a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp with stable-isotope glucose and glycerol tracers.

The investigators measured endogenous glucose production to assess hepatic insulin sensitivity; metabolic clearance of glucose to assess peripheral insulin sensitivity; and free-fatty-acid concentration and glycerol rate of appearance to assess adipose-tissue insulin sensitivity.

During the clamp, endogenous glucose production was higher in the type 1 diabetes group, indicating hepatic insulin resistance. Metabolic clearance of glucose was lower, indicating reduced glucose uptake during hyperinsulinemia and therefore peripheral insulin resistance. Serum free-fatty-acid concentration and glycerol rate of appearance were also higher, indicating adipose-tissue insulin resistance. These findings were independent of BMI, visceral adiposity, lean mass, and physical activity, suggesting that other factors contribute to insulin resistance in type 1 diabetes.

Possible contributors include insulin deficiency with relative hyperglucagonemia; exogenous insulin administration, which produces relative portal hypoinsulinemia and peripheral hyperinsulinemia; and autoimmunity with systemic inflammation.

These factors can create abnormalities in key organs. In the liver, gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, and glucose output may increase. In adipose tissue, lipolysis and free-fatty-acid release increase, along with adipocyte stress and inflammation. In skeletal muscle, GLUT4 expression and glucose oxidation decrease, while free-fatty-acid uptake, lipid accumulation, and lipid oxidation increase.

The resulting increase in circulating glucose and free fatty acids produces glucolipotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired β-oxidation, higher reactive oxygen species, and systemic inflammation. These changes both damage target organs and contribute to systemic insulin resistance. Obesity further aggravates this mechanism.

## Ectopic adiposity, cardiovascular risk, and complications

Despite the difficulties in applying the conventional adipose-tissue model to type 1 diabetes, abnormal fat distribution, adipose dysfunction, and insulin resistance do affect cardiovascular risk.

The French SFDT1 cohort included 1,482 participants with type 1 diabetes. Investigators collected BMI and estimated cardiovascular risk with the Steno Type 1 Risk Engine. BMI alone did not predict cardiovascular risk as well as central adiposity. Waist-to-height ratio, an easy measure of central adiposity, showed a direct relationship with estimated cardiovascular risk above a threshold of 0.5 in both men and women.

In an Italian cohort of 80 people with type 1 diabetes, we used dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry to calculate an upper-to-lower-body fat-deposition index as an indirect measure of ectopic adiposity. Participants in the medium- and high-cardiovascular-risk categories had a less favorable index, supporting an association between ectopic fat and cardiovascular risk.

We also studied ectopic fat in relation to cardiac autonomic neuropathy, or CAN. The study included 44 people with autoimmune diabetes and 99 with type 2 diabetes. They underwent dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and screening for CAN. Although overall adiposity was greater in type 2 diabetes, participants with autoimmune diabetes and CAN had more visceral adiposity, greater trunk fat mass, and a higher trunk-to-leg fat-mass ratio than those without CAN. Ectopic fat therefore appeared to be associated with CAN in autoimmune diabetes.

Another large cohort analysis compared classical type 1 diabetes with “double diabetes,” defined here as type 1 diabetes plus metabolic syndrome, including obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. The investigators also analyzed a subgroup with hemoglobin A1c below 7%. Double diabetes was associated with a higher prevalence of both microvascular and macrovascular complications in the full cohort and in the subgroup with good glycemic control, compared with classical lean type 1 diabetes.

Insulin resistance itself is associated with complications. An analysis of 1,227 DCCT/EDIC participants divided people by BMI and estimated glucose disposal rate, or eGDR, into metabolically healthy non-obesity, metabolically healthy obesity, metabolically unhealthy non-obesity, and metabolically unhealthy obesity. Metabolically unhealthy obesity and metabolically unhealthy non-obesity were associated with greater risks of microvascular complications than the metabolically healthy phenotypes.

The same pattern appeared for macrovascular outcomes, including peripheral artery disease, coronary-artery calcification, and severe coronary-artery calcification. Cumulative risks of a first cardiovascular event and a first major atherosclerotic cardiovascular event were highest in metabolically unhealthy obesity, followed by metabolically unhealthy non-obesity and then the metabolically healthy groups.

Insulin resistance has also been associated with mortality. In 17,050 people with type 1 diabetes in the Swedish National Diabetes Register, participants were divided into four eGDR categories. The all-cause mortality proportions were approximately 15% for eGDR below 4 mg/kg/min, 11% for eGDR from 4.00 to 5.99, 6% for eGDR from 6.00 to 7.99, and 1% for eGDR of at least 8. Greater insulin resistance was therefore associated with higher mortality.

## Conclusions and research questions

Obesity is now highly prevalent in type 1 diabetes and poses a major clinical challenge. The adiposopathy model characterized in type 2 diabetes cannot be transferred without qualification to autoimmune diabetes. Autoimmune diabetes is heterogeneous; cardiovascular risk has multiple contributors; adipose tissue may have a different distribution and function; and insulin resistance has a more complex pathogenesis.

Nevertheless, current methods show that insulin resistance and ectopic fat are important cardiovascular risk factors in autoimmune diabetes.

Several questions remain open. What biological factors drive the body-composition phenotype of type 1 diabetes, and what does this phenotype mean for adipose and skeletal-muscle tissue? We know that skeletal muscle is impaired in type 1 diabetes and should be studied further. How does fat distribution across different depots influence insulin resistance and cardiovascular risk? How much does the heterogeneity of autoimmune diabetes matter? Finally, do strategies that reduce adiposity and improve skeletal-muscle function produce meaningful reductions in cardiovascular events and mortality?

Thank you very much. I would be happy to take questions.

## Questions and discussion

**Moderator:** Thank you for that beautiful talk. I am trying to sort out a couple of things in the clinic. You discussed a youth clamp study in which ectopic fat was not increased, yet insulin resistance was present in every tissue. In your adults, you used an upper-body adiposity index, which we may or may not be able to derive from a DXA scan.

The usual clinical parameters look good until metabolic syndrome develops. Is there an earlier stage of upper-body adiposity, before metabolic syndrome, when we could identify people at risk and intervene more aggressively than we do in general practice?

**Renata Risi:** Thank you very much for the question. In type 2 diabetes we have established thresholds. For example, a BMI above 30 kg/m² defines obesity and is associated with increased cardiovascular risk. We know that obesity also increases cardiovascular risk in type 1 diabetes, but we do not know the relevant threshold.

Population studies should focus on this question. The BMI threshold probably differs among individuals, and type 1 diabetes may have other factors that protect against cardiovascular risk at certain BMI levels. We need population studies to answer this, particularly because the answer will influence pharmacologic strategies.

**Moderator:** I will risk asking a second question. What about sex differences? Cardiovascular-disease risk is increased approximately fivefold in women with diabetes.

**Renata Risi:** The case-control studies I showed were matched by sex, so the effect of sex was excluded by design. Population studies are also needed to answer that question.

**Audience member:** One clear difference between type 1 and type 2 diabetes is that hyperinsulinemia in type 2 diabetes has both therapeutic and endogenous contributions, whereas in type 1 diabetes it is entirely therapeutic. Does the insulin therapy we give aggravate the unique insulin resistance seen in type 1 diabetes?

This matters because of how insulin is delivered. Years ago, I was involved in a MiniMed study using intraperitoneal insulin delivery. Those patients required much less insulin and had excellent glycemic control. Should we revisit how insulin is delivered in type 1 diabetes? And should we systematically study insulin sensitizers used in type 2 diabetes—such as metformin or thiazolidinediones—in type 1 diabetes beyond their effects on weight?

**Renata Risi:** Thank you very much. Type 1 diabetes is an insulin-deficient state, so insulin is essential for survival. Insulin therapy is associated with better long-term cardiovascular outcomes, and diabetes technology is also associated with better long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

In research and clinical practice, we should pay more attention to total daily insulin dose per kilogram, especially when type 1 diabetes coexists with overweight or obesity. When those individuals require more insulin, that may aggravate peripheral hyperinsulinemia. Nutritional counseling, physical activity, and pharmacologic strategies may help reduce insulin doses and peripheral hyperinsulinemia. We should combine strategies and monitor daily insulin requirements carefully.

**Audience member:** We know that insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome predict cardiovascular disease in people without diabetes. You showed that people with type 1 diabetes may not have the classic body-fat composition expected in metabolic syndrome. How do we identify which people with type 1 diabetes are most insulin resistant and at greatest risk? Does metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease occur in type 1 diabetes, given their lower liver fat?

**Renata Risi:** The body-composition data I showed come from small populations and must be investigated further. At the same BMI, people with type 1 diabetes appear to have a more favorable body composition. I am more confident about the fat-mass percentage than about the muscle data, because we cannot exclude an effect of glycogen deposition in skeletal muscle.

What is the clinical impact? Body-fat composition may be somewhat protective, but we also know that ectopic adiposity affects health and contributes to insulin resistance in type 1 diabetes. Ectopic fat is probably an important part of the answer.

**Audience member from Brazil:** Some evidence suggests that weight variability is associated with a higher risk of diabetic kidney disease. Do you have any comments about that relationship in type 1 diabetes?

**Renata Risi:** Weight variability probably has a major impact in obesity generally, but evidence in type 1 diabetes is very limited, so it is difficult to answer.

## Session closing

**Moderator:** This concludes our session. Thank you very much to all three speakers.

My first major takeaway is that reducing excess caloric exposure is probably essential for all of us, including people with type 1 diabetes. Second, insulin produced by one's own pancreatic β-cells and delivered to the liver is probably the best insulin. Disease-modifying therapies, and eventually β-cell regeneration or transplantation that delivers insulin to the right place, will therefore be crucial. As long as we are limited to peripheral delivery of our current insulins, we have a problem.

My third conclusion is that we do not know everything yet. There are intriguing concepts in which autoimmunity may contribute to adiposity and adiposity may contribute to autoimmunity. More research is needed. We will also take care to record what people eat in our trials, because I believe that is crucial.
