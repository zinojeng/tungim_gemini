## Background and study objective

It is a great honor for me to present the data from our phase 2 clinical trial in adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), and I will try to convince you that mitotane is not the ACC treater's friend.

To introduce ACC briefly, it is an orphan malignancy. Steroid-hormone secretion by the tumor is frequent and leads to Cushing syndrome or hyperandrogenism in many cases. There is only one approved treatment, oral mitotane. In advanced disease, chemotherapy with etoposide, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (EDP) combined with mitotane is the treatment standard.

The challenge in advanced ACC is exemplified by the poor overall survival of only 15 months with standard treatment in the pivotal phase 3 clinical trial.

The aim of this study was to establish the potential role of cabozantinib, a multityrosine kinase inhibitor, in patients with advanced ACC after failure of this type of standard therapy.

## CaboACC phase 2 trial design

This was an investigator-initiated phase 2 trial conducted across the COVID-19 pandemic and included 37 patients. The sponsor was University Hospital Würzburg, and funding was provided by Ipsen Pharmaceuticals.

We administered Cabometyx 60 mg once daily. As I said, this is a multityrosine kinase inhibitor. Response assessment was performed every eight weeks using RECIST 1.1. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival at four months (PFS4), and the secondary endpoints are listed on the slide.

The main exclusion criterion was a mitotane plasma concentration above 5 mg/L at enrollment. We and others had demonstrated that mitotane is a strong inducer of the hepatic enzyme CYP3A4, which is known to metabolize multiple drugs, including cabozantinib. The exclusion criterion was intended to minimize the effect of prior mitotane use on cabozantinib treatment outcomes.

## Trial population and treatment disposition

We ultimately included 37 patients who received at least one dose of cabozantinib. Thirty-four patients were evaluable for the primary PFS4 endpoint. Three discontinued before that evaluation: one patient died, another discontinued because of toxicity, and a third withdrew informed consent.

Except for one patient who remained on treatment after the trial, 31 patients experienced disease progression and discontinued, and two discontinued because of adverse events.

Regarding patient characteristics, I want to draw your attention to the number of previous systemic therapy lines: 75% had received three or more. Most importantly, all except three patients had previously received mitotane, and 32 had also received platinum-based chemotherapy. Platinum-based chemotherapy was an inclusion criterion unless the patient declined it.

## Primary outcome and RECIST response

The median time to progression was 16 weeks. The primary PFS4 endpoint was reached in 14 of 34 evaluable participants, or 41%, meaning that the study met its primary endpoint. Median overall survival was 48.4 weeks, approximately 11 months, and patients were treated for a median of 22 weeks.

The RECIST response assessment showed two partial responses and stable disease as the best response in 20 patients. Imaging was performed in 36 patients, and 14 had progressive disease.

To summarize this first part, cabozantinib appears to be active: partial remission was observed in two patients, and disease control lasting more than 40 weeks was observed in five. The primary endpoint was reached, and cabozantinib had to be discontinued in two patients because of treatment-emergent adverse events.

## Comparison with the MD Anderson trial

One year before we completed our trial, Mohamed Habra's group at MD Anderson Cancer Center published a parallel study of the same drug in 18 patients. They observed a median progression-free survival of six months and median overall survival of 24 months. This compares favorably with our study, in which the median time to progression was only four months and overall survival was shorter.

What could explain this difference, which was also reflected in the less favorable RECIST response? One possibility was prior mitotane use. Mitotane had been used in two-thirds of the patients in the MD Anderson study and in virtually all of our patients.

The MD Anderson investigators had also studied the potential effect of prior mitotane in their cohort, although only limited pharmacokinetic (PK) data were available. At steady state on Day 29, patients who had never received mitotane had higher cabozantinib plasma concentrations than patients who had received mitotane, indicating that mitotane lowers exposure to active cabozantinib.

## Population pharmacokinetics and CYP3A4-mediated interaction

We used a more extensive approach. We prospectively sampled serum at steady state throughout the study, collecting approximately 270 samples; 269 samples were included in the population PK analysis. We applied a published cabozantinib-specific population pharmacokinetic model to study the impact of mitotane on cabozantinib clearance.

Our reference group comprised the three patients with no prior mitotane exposure together with patients who had no detectable plasma mitotane at baseline. We compared them with patients who had detectable baseline mitotane. Recall that a plasma concentration below 5 mg/L was required for enrollment.

Detectable mitotane increased cabozantinib clearance 1.65-fold, suggesting that a CYP3A4-mediated drug–drug interaction was occurring.

In more detail, when comparing patients with no prior mitotane intake, patients with prior exposure but undetectable baseline mitotane, and patients with detectable baseline mitotane, steady-state cabozantinib exposure was significantly lower in those with detectable mitotane. This suggests that prior mitotane use can severely affect cabozantinib exposure when mitotane remains detectable.

## Exposure–response and exposure–toxicity findings

We also examined outcomes. Patients who achieved at least stable disease at four months showed a trend toward higher cabozantinib exposure than those with progressive disease.

Finally, we performed an exploratory analysis using individual predicted visit-level exposure estimates and treatment-related adverse events of grade 2 or higher during those visits. The probability of grade 2 or higher adverse events increased with cabozantinib exposure. This suggests that cabozantinib exposure may significantly affect not only treatment outcome but also adverse outcomes.

## Conclusions and proposed next step

In summary, median steady-state cabozantinib exposure was lower in patients with detectable baseline mitotane than in patients with no prior mitotane exposure or prior exposure with undetectable baseline plasma mitotane. This suggests a significant drug–drug interaction that may translate into clinical outcomes. Adverse events were also more frequent among patients with higher exposure.

This study may therefore underestimate the clinical activity of cabozantinib because of the drug–drug interaction with prior mitotane. In my opinion, it is important to study cabozantinib in mitotane-naïve patients in the future. I advocate a randomized trial comparing cabozantinib with standard EDP plus mitotane in advanced ACC.

With this, I would like to thank the ACC team, the early clinical trials team at our institution, the clinical pharmacy team, Miriam Reuter, the first author, and all of you for your attention.
