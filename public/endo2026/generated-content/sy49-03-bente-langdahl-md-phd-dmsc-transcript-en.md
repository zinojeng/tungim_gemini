**Bente Langdahl:** Thank you very much. Thank you to the chairpersons for the kind introduction and to the Endocrine Society for inviting me to this meeting.

I am going to return to the clinical side and discuss what we may be able to do to mitigate the overshoot after denosumab discontinuation. Here are my disclosures.

## Early evidence for zoledronate after denosumab

The first small study was really an extended case series from New Zealand. It included six postmenopausal women who had participated in the FREEDOM trial and had received denosumab for seven years. At the time, we knew that the effect of denosumab was reversible and could produce this overshoot. The investigators thought that they should not simply discontinue treatment, so they administered one 5-mg infusion of zoledronate. Nevertheless, these six women still lost BMD.

That study was presented at the ECTS meeting in 2017, when we were trying to decide what to do, and it inspired our first ZOLARMAB study. The major question was the correct timing of zoledronate. We now have much more preclinical information, which would have been very useful then, but we did not have it at the time. We worried that zoledronate could be given too early to attach effectively to bone.

We therefore studied three groups. One group received zoledronate six months after the last denosumab injection, one received it at nine months, and an observation group received zoledronate when CTX rose. Because we were concerned about giving zoledronate before it could bind to bone, we selected a very high CTX intervention threshold: 1.26 micrograms per liter, 50% above the postmenopausal reference range. We now know that this was much too high, but that was the rationale at the time.

We enrolled 61 postmenopausal women and men older than 50 years. They had received denosumab for a mean of 4.6 years and had BMD T-scores above -2.5, as required for study entry. We thought we might need only one or perhaps two zoledronate infusions. In fact, many infusions were given during the two-year study. Repeat treatment was triggered by CTX at or above 1.26 micrograms per liter or by BMD loss of at least 5%.

Despite all our efforts, BMD still declined. Lumbar-spine BMD fell approximately 4% to 5% during the first year. The decline was more rapid in the delayed-treatment groups than in the six-month group. Total-hip BMD fell by approximately 3%. We measured CTX frequently to understand the process. Early treatment suppressed CTX further, but it did not maintain CTX within the premenopausal reference range. In the two delayed-treatment groups, CTX showed an overshoot resembling that in untreated patients. At 24 months, lumbar-spine BMD loss was approximately 4% in all three groups, with no significant difference between groups.

## The Swiss ZOLARMAB study

At the same time, we planned with Serge Ferrari and his group that they would conduct a similar study in Switzerland. Approval took some time, and they had the benefit of seeing some of our results. They chose a lower CTX threshold: not 50% above the postmenopausal range, as we had done, but 50% above the upper limit of the premenopausal range, or 644 nanograms per liter.

Their study included 44 postmenopausal women, of whom 34 were randomized and 10 with prior bisphosphonate exposure self-allocated to a separate observation group. Mean denosumab exposure was 4.9 years. The randomized groups received zoledronate at six months, at nine months, or when CTX exceeded 644 nanograms per liter or BMD fell by more than 5%. The additional group had received bisphosphonates previously.

The early-treatment group still lost approximately 4.5% of lumbar-spine BMD, while the nine-month group lost approximately 8%. The observation group had a similar loss, and the prior-bisphosphonate group lost somewhat less. At the total hip, loss was approximately 2% in the six-month group and 5% in the nine-month group, with somewhat less loss in the observation group. Pretreatment CTX was low in the early-treatment group and had already risen substantially in the later-treatment groups. With repeated treatment driven by the threshold, CTX levels subsequently converged. The median number of zoledronate infusions was two, with a range of one to five.

The Danish and Swiss studies were more alike than different. Participants were around 70 years old. Our Danish study included a few men and some participants who had used alendronate briefly. At the time in Denmark, reimbursement for denosumab generally required prior alendronate treatment that could not be tolerated. Denosumab exposure and spine and hip T-scores were otherwise similar.

Because the Swiss CTX threshold was lower, they administered more zoledronate. Despite that, lumbar-spine and total-hip losses were slightly greater in the Swiss study. I do not have a good explanation, and this requires further investigation. Across the studies, the approximate 12-month lumbar-spine loss was 4% to 5% in Denmark and 4% to 8% in Switzerland. Total-hip loss was approximately 2.5% to 3.5% and 2% to 5%, respectively. There was relatively little additional loss between months 12 and 24.

## Alendronate versus raloxifene after short-term denosumab

Let us consider other options. The CARD study from Benjamin Leder's group included 51 postmenopausal women. Participants received denosumab 60 mg every six months for one year and were then randomized to alendronate 70 mg weekly or raloxifene 60 mg daily for another year.

CTX fell rapidly and markedly after denosumab. Alendronate maintained suppression of CTX and P1NP, whereas raloxifene did not. The same pattern appeared in BMD. The spine BMD gained during one year of denosumab was maintained with alendronate, while it declined with raloxifene. Denosumab-induced gains were maintained at all measured sites with alendronate. With raloxifene, BMD fell by approximately 2.0% at the spine and 1.2% at the total hip.

Raloxifene is therefore probably not the drug of choice when stopping denosumab, particularly after more than one year of denosumab exposure.

## The AfterDmab study

The Greek AfterDmab study was conducted at approximately the same time as our first trial. It included 57 postmenopausal women who had received denosumab for at least two years, with a mean exposure of approximately 2.4 years. They were assigned either another year of denosumab or one 5-mg intravenous zoledronate infusion.

During that year, both treatments maintained serum CTX. After the additional denosumab year ended and no further denosumab was administered, CTX overshot in that group. CTX remained controlled in the zoledronate group. Zoledronate also stabilized BMD at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, whereas BMD fell after the additional denosumab was stopped. These findings followed a relatively short denosumab exposure.

## Romosozumab after denosumab

Another possibility is to move from denosumab to romosozumab. This was first examined in a small number of participants in a phase 2 romosozumab trial. Participants had received several romosozumab regimens, were then randomized to placebo or denosumab, and received romosozumab again during year four.

Romosozumab initially increased P1NP. Transitioning to denosumab suppressed both P1NP and CTX. When denosumab was stopped and romosozumab restarted, CTX rose very rapidly and was not suppressed by romosozumab. At best, lumbar-spine BMD stabilized, while total-hip BMD may have declined slightly. We should therefore think very carefully before using romosozumab alone after denosumab, especially when denosumab has been given for more than one year.

When an ECTS working group reviewed studies published up to approximately 2020, we saw a clear pattern: irrespective of which bisphosphonate followed, longer denosumab exposure was associated with greater BMD loss. The DAPS trial after one year of denosumab showed small changes with one year of alendronate. The AfterDmab trial after approximately two years showed maintained or slightly increased BMD with zoledronate. Observational studies after two to five years showed losses, and after approximately 4.6 years the ZOLARMAB trial showed a lumbar-spine loss close to 5%. A small series after seven years showed considerably larger losses.

## Gradual dose reduction

We have also considered whether denosumab could be tapered. A small French pilot study enrolled 13 postmenopausal women who had received denosumab for more than three years, with a mean duration of 45.2 months. Six months after the last 60-mg dose, they received 30 mg. Six months later they received 15 mg, and denosumab was then stopped.

BMD had increased during full-dose treatment. Even after gradual reduction, BMD subsequently fell at both the lumbar spine and hip. At the lumbar spine, only 39% of the initial gain was preserved one year after the 15-mg dose; at the hip, the loss was approximately equal to the initial gain.

The individual CTX results are concerning. Six months after the 15-mg dose, mean CTX was 166 pg/mL; at 12 months it was 549 pg/mL, with substantial variability. A woman with very high CTX lost approximately 9% of BMD. One participant sustained two vertebral fractures eight months after the final 15-mg dose. If gradual dose reduction is pursued, something more appears to be needed after the taper.

Aliya Khan and colleagues studied a larger group: 114 postmenopausal women at moderate fracture risk, without prior fragility fractures, who had received denosumab for at least one year. Median full-dose exposure was 30 months. Participants then received 30 mg every six months for two years.

The investigators compared women who had received full-dose denosumab for less than three years with those treated for three years or longer. Many had used bisphosphonates previously, and their BMD T-scores were relatively favorable—patients in whom stopping denosumab might ordinarily be considered.

During the first year of the 30-mg regimen, lumbar-spine BMD increased significantly, while hip BMD did not change significantly. Lumbar-spine BMD increased further during year two. Older women appeared to have more pronounced BMD gains during continued reduced-dose denosumab.

The group then evaluated stopping reduced-dose treatment and giving one zoledronate infusion. This smaller follow-up included 20 postmenopausal women who had received 60 mg of denosumab for a mean of 30 months and then 30 mg for a mean of 30 months. Twenty-four months after zoledronate, lumbar-spine BMD had fallen 3.71%, femoral-neck BMD 1.75%, and total-hip BMD 2.16%. CTX was not measured, but alkaline phosphatase rose substantially. Dose reduction may prove useful, but one zoledronate infusion was still insufficient for everyone.

## Adding romosozumab while continuing denosumab

Should we abandon romosozumab as an option? A small, recently published study from Giovanni Adami's group evaluated postmenopausal women who had received denosumab for at least two years. It was not a randomized controlled trial. By physician decision, some women continued denosumab alone while others continued denosumab and added romosozumab for one year.

Both groups continued to gain BMD at the spine and hip, without a significant between-group difference. CTX remained strongly suppressed in both groups. P1NP rose slightly when romosozumab was added. If there is a compelling reason to move toward romosozumab, adding it while continuing denosumab may be a strategy to consider, although the evidence remains limited.

## ECTS recommendations

The 2020 ECTS position statement tried to summarize what we knew.

For young people at low fracture risk, denosumab is generally not recommended because a bisphosphonate can usually be used instead.

After short-term denosumab treatment—up to approximately 2.5 years—in a patient who is now at low fracture risk, switch to an oral bisphosphonate or zoledronate for one to two years. Bone-turnover markers and BMD should guide reassessment, especially with oral treatment.

After longer denosumab exposure or in a patient at high fracture risk, either continue denosumab, with an individualized decision beyond 10 years, or switch to zoledronate. The statement recommended giving zoledronate six months after the last denosumab injection, measuring bone-turnover markers after three and six months, and readministering zoledronate when markers remain high. The preclinical evidence discussed today suggests that treatment might need to begin a little earlier. If bone-turnover markers cannot be measured, at least two zoledronate infusions should be given.

The statement also considered vertebral fractures occurring within one to two years after denosumab cessation. Avoid vertebroplasty because it has been associated with further fractures. Avoid teriparatide as monotherapy. Options include promptly restarting denosumab or treating with intravenous zoledronate or an oral bisphosphonate. One may also consider adding teriparatide to denosumab for two years and then giving intravenous zoledronate. On the basis of the limited new evidence, adding romosozumab while continuing denosumab might also be considered.

## Implementing the recommendations in clinical practice

My PhD student, Natasha Laursen, examined how implementation of the ECTS guidance was working in our clinic. We included 66 patients who had received denosumab for a mean of 6.7 years. Their BMD T-scores were favorable and bone-turnover markers were suppressed. These were patients in whom we would consider discontinuation: 50 had a total-hip T-score of at least -1.5, 14 had unmanageable adverse effects, and two had treatment failure.

Everyone received zoledronate at baseline. Additional treatment was triggered by CTX at or above 0.5 micrograms per liter or by BMD loss beyond the least significant change—at least 5% at the hip or 3% at the spine. After three months, 14% already required another infusion. By six months, approximately half required additional bisphosphonate treatment. At one and two years, some were retreated because of BMD loss, some because of high CTX, and some for both reasons.

The process became complicated. Some patients declined further infusions, often because of the acute flu-like reaction to zoledronate. Others received late infusions because of the logistical burden of blood sampling and infusion appointments. The mean number of zoledronate infusions was three, with a range of one to five.

Despite increases from the suppressed baseline, we kept CTX and P1NP within the premenopausal reference range. BMD loss was mitigated: approximately 2.5% at the lumbar spine and 2% at the total hip by 12 months, with no significant loss at the femoral neck. That seemed reasonably successful, but it required a great deal of work, and not every patient was willing to undertake it.

## New ZOLARMAB2 data

We therefore conducted ZOLARMAB2. These are new data that we calculated only within the past month. Patients stopping denosumab enter one of two strategies. In the fixed-schedule arm, zoledronate is given at baseline and again at three and six months. In the marker-guided arm, CTX is measured and zoledronate is administered when CTX exceeds 0.4 micrograms per liter. At one year, patients will be rerandomized so that we can evaluate what is needed longer term. I will show only the first-year data from the 100 patients in the fixed-schedule arm.

The mean age was 68.4 years, and mean denosumab exposure was 6.1 years. Eighty-four percent had received a bisphosphonate previously, with a median exposure of one year. Their median T-scores were -1.6 at the lumbar spine, -1.2 at the total hip, and -1.8 at the femoral neck, and bone turnover was suppressed.

We are doing somewhat better, but bone loss remains. At 12 months, lumbar-spine BMD had declined 2.3%, total-hip BMD 1.8%, and femoral-neck BMD 0.9%. CTX was maintained at approximately the median of the premenopausal reference range, but P1NP rose, especially during the second half of the year despite zoledronate infusions at months 0, 3, and 6. We will need to examine these data further.

## Practical approach and conclusion

We should expect some bone loss after stopping long-term denosumab and should aim for a higher BMD before considering discontinuation.

After short-term treatment, alendronate for one to two years may be sufficient, but I recommend monitoring CTX. Alternatively, a single 5-mg zoledronate infusion at six months may be enough for some patients.

After more than 2.5 years of denosumab, be very careful and explain that the transition may be intensive for at least one year. Give 5 mg of zoledronate six months after the final denosumab injection, then measure CTX at three, six, and 12 months and retreat if CTX exceeds 0.4 micrograms per liter. If CTX monitoring is unavailable, give a second zoledronate infusion after six months.

Beyond the first year, I do not expect long-term protection against bone loss. Women and men will probably resume age-related bone loss, so we are investigating whether annual zoledronate provides additional benefit.

Denosumab discontinuation produces an overshoot in bone resorption, bone loss, and increased fracture risk. Bisphosphonates can mitigate these consequences but cannot eliminate them. Potential new strategies include gradual reduction of the denosumab dose and adding an anabolic agent while continuing antiresorptive therapy, but both approaches need further investigation.

In clinical practice, we must balance the number of visits and treatments against the amount of BMD loss that may be acceptable. Thank you very much for your attention.

## Session Q&A

### Immune targets and acute-phase reactions

**Moderator:** We seem to have a growing problem as many patients approach 10 years of denosumab. The available data are partly observational, although trials are emerging. We have focused heavily on bisphosphonates, but RANK and RANKL are deeply connected with the immune system. Are there other targets or strategies we should consider, perhaps based on the mouse data?

**Bente Langdahl:** There is clearly immune-system activation involving RANKL, and we must take that into account. When denosumab is stopped and zoledronate is given, approximately 60% of our patients have flu-like acute-phase symptoms—far more than in treatment-naive patients. We now routinely give paracetamol for three days. Preventing those symptoms is important because otherwise some patients want to discontinue the entire transition plan because of adverse effects.

**Albert S. Kim:** I agree. Osteoclasts do more than resorb bone; they arise from the same lineage as many immune cells. If acute-phase reactions after denosumab are an important clinical barrier, we need to investigate how to prevent them. Osteoporosis treatment already has many barriers to uptake, and osteoimmunology is a very active area with many developments ahead.

### Timing and dose of zoledronate

**Yumi Ri, Seoul, Korea:** Thank you for the excellent lectures. Why not give a higher dose of zoledronic acid rather than more frequent doses, and why not give it earlier—perhaps one or three months after the last denosumab injection—to target the rise in preosteoclasts? What are your thoughts about timing and dosage?

**Bente Langdahl:** You may be right. Anyone who can conduct such studies should test these approaches, because we thought we understood what was happening but continue to be surprised. The mouse data are now helpful. In our new study, approximately 40% of patients had CTX below 0.4 micrograms per liter and therefore would not have qualified for marker-triggered treatment, yet they still lost bone.

I do not think bisphosphonates can completely prevent bone loss after long-term denosumab. We could test shorter cycles—for example, two or three years of denosumab, one year of a bisphosphonate, and then another two or three years of denosumab—but these strategies must be studied in patients.

### Oral bisphosphonates and reimbursement constraints

**Moderator:** Several online questions concern the United States, where insurance may not cover multiple zoledronate doses within one year and repeated testing also has costs. Could oral alendronate be used?

**Bente Langdahl:** There has been discussion about using twice the usual alendronate dose, perhaps beginning four or five months after the last denosumab injection. Some clinicians are doing this, and I hope they will publish their outcomes. We do not yet have trial evidence.

In many countries, including mine, denosumab is reimbursed only when a patient cannot tolerate an oral bisphosphonate, which makes such trials difficult. If only one zoledronate infusion is permitted, reduced-dose denosumab might be another option, although that also increases the number of visits.

**Albert S. Kim:** Adherence to oral bisphosphonates is already difficult, and the stakes in this setting are high. Even with motivated patients who want to stop denosumab, adherence introduces another uncertain variable. Intravenous zoledronate may cause an acute-phase reaction, but at least one knows that the drug is on board.

### Continuing denosumab beyond 10 years

**Lorenz Hofbauer:** In sport, there is a saying: never change a winning team. I have treated several thousand women with denosumab and several hundred for more than 10 years. It is well tolerated and highly effective. Why discontinue? I tell patients that stopping takes them into a world of trouble. What is your view?

**Bente Langdahl:** We give our patients the same warning. If they continue for a long time and later stop, they enter a world of trouble. This discussion is essential. A patient who understands the risks may choose to continue, and I would continue treatment in that situation. ECTS is working to collect long-term data, because the more we know, the better our future decisions will be.

### Chronic kidney disease

**Subhash Kukreja, Chicago:** Why not start with zoledronic acid rather than denosumab? More specifically, patients with mild to moderate renal insufficiency are often started on denosumab because bisphosphonates are unsuitable. What do you do if such a patient must discontinue? Do you continue denosumab, or use romosozumab?

**Bente Langdahl:** I did not discuss this in detail because we have no data. Starting denosumab in chronic kidney disease is a serious decision. We generally use it only with a strong fracture indication, such as vertebral fractures, multiple vertebral fractures, or a hip fracture. We do not treat BMD alone with denosumab in this setting. There is also a risk of hypocalcemia.

The indication must be very strong, and I tell these patients that treatment is essentially lifelong unless they receive a kidney transplant. I would not experiment with romosozumab because these patients often already have high cardiovascular risk. This requires a serious discussion before denosumab begins.

### Is modest bone loss acceptable?

**Tabitha, New York:** Denosumab prescribing now sounds frightening. Although bone loss occurs, perhaps a 2% to 3% loss is not clinically important. Many of the strategies discussed may prevent the vertebral fractures that concern us most. That seems somewhat reassuring.

**Bente Langdahl:** I agree. We need to have that discussion among ourselves and with our patients. I do not currently think that any strategy will prevent all bone loss. We must accept some loss.

**Panelist:** Not everyone who loses bone will sustain a vertebral fracture. This is a compromise, and evidence on preventing post-denosumab vertebral fractures remains limited. More data are needed.

### Stopping because of AFF or ONJ

**Audience member:** What strategies do you use when a patient must stop denosumab because of an atypical femoral fracture or osteonecrosis of the jaw? I realize that large studies are unlikely.

**Bente Langdahl:** This is a real clinical situation. I would reassess fracture risk, BMD, prior fractures, and how recent those fractures were. After long-term treatment, one generally cannot stop antiresorptive therapy completely.

With an atypical femoral fracture, I would probably give zoledronate and observe. In cases I have managed, the fracture has healed after intramedullary fixation. Osteonecrosis of the jaw can be more difficult because it requires agreement with the dental team. The dentist must understand that abruptly stopping denosumab after five, six, or seven years creates a high risk of vertebral fracture. A longer healing period may have to be accepted.

One could consider continuing denosumab and adding teriparatide, but we have no evidence. I would find a way either to reduce antiresorptive intensity or to continue it and add teriparatide rather than simply stopping.

**Albert S. Kim:** The contralateral femur also matters. If both femurs have been prophylactically rodded, continuing treatment may be possible. The risk of bone loss is very real, whereas the absolute risk of an atypical fracture in the contralateral femur is lower.

### CTX thresholds and TRAP-5b

**Audience member, Dresden, Germany:** What CTX cutoff should be used? In ZOLARMAB2 you use the upper premenopausal range. What are your thoughts about TRAP-5b? Albert showed that it rises first, and your group also has clinical data.

**Bente Langdahl:** We measured TRAP-5b in both studies. As in the mice, it rose before CTX. It is not a standard assay in our hospital, so we do not use it routinely. CTX and P1NP show the largest and most dynamic changes, even though the pattern is similar. They remain useful markers, provided we remember what they measure: collagen degradation and collagen production. Osteoclast numbers can rise before CTX becomes elevated.

For the CTX threshold, I would now aim for approximately the median of the premenopausal range rather than merely the upper limit. Above the median, bone loss appears more likely. If CTX can be maintained below the premenopausal median, most patients will probably do reasonably well.

**Albert S. Kim:** CTX and P1NP are excellent markers. In this specific context, however, incorporating TRAP-5b measurement may add useful information.

### Timing of add-on romosozumab

**Moderator:** An online participant asks when romosozumab should begin after denosumab and when denosumab should be discontinued.

**Bente Langdahl:** With the very limited evidence available, I would not discontinue denosumab. I would continue it throughout the year of romosozumab and reassess the patient afterward. If BMD becomes very favorable, discontinuation might eventually be considered, or denosumab might simply continue.

Romosozumab can begin at any point in the six-month denosumab dosing interval. I would continue denosumab on schedule and add romosozumab for one year.

### Duration of rare adverse effects and renal safety of zoledronate

**Audience member:** Do we have robust evidence that atypical femoral fracture or osteonecrosis of the jaw increases with the duration of denosumab? The 10-year denosumab data did not appear to show a clear increase. Also, when I have used multiple zoledronic acid doses in older patients, creatinine has increased. Did you observe this?

**Marian Schini:** We have fewer data on atypical femoral fractures with denosumab. Osteonecrosis of the jaw appears to be more common with denosumab than with bisphosphonates. In FREEDOM, these rare events appeared during the extension phase, so duration of treatment is relevant.

**Bente Langdahl:** With zoledronate, good hydration is very important. If kidney function is impaired, we administer the infusion over one hour. The peak bisphosphonate concentration affects the kidney, so a longer infusion time may help.

### Why osteoblast-lineage cells increase RANKL

**Nikki Partridge, Rutgers University:** This was an excellent symposium. My question is for Albert Kim. Why do osteoblast-lineage cells produce more RANKL? Could reverse signaling from the antibody be involved?

**Albert S. Kim:** We do not know. It may relate to activation sites on bone repeatedly signaling that remodeling is needed. The mechanostat concept may be relevant. We induce an extreme physiological state by potently inhibiting osteoclast formation, and the system naturally tries to return toward normal physiology. We know where RANKL is overexpressed, but the exact cell populations and the reason RANKL rises during inhibition remain open questions.

**Nikki Partridge:** Could you study mice to see whether RANKL also rises when bone resorption is inhibited by a bisphosphonate or another agent?

**Albert S. Kim:** That is an excellent and feasible study. I am very open to funding.

**Moderator:** On that note, thank you to all the speakers for an outstanding session and thank you to the audience for your involvement. Good afternoon.
