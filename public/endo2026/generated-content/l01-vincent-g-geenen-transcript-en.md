### The immune system and the changing place of the thymus

Let me now turn to immunity, because that is where the real drama of this story begins. Our immune defenses consist of two major arms: innate immunity, which provides rapid and relatively stereotyped responses, and adaptive immunity, which relies on lymphocytes carrying an enormous diversity of antigen receptors. T cells generate T-cell receptors in the thymus with 10^18 possible combinations, whereas B cells generate B-cell receptors in the bone marrow with 10^13 possible combinations. Very early on, Paul Ehrlich in Germany, one of the founders of immunology, predicted that mechanisms should exist to prevent immune reactions against the host itself.

This was captured in his famous dictum of horror autotoxicus. In 1983, when I was still a young researcher, I attended an international symposium in Brussels on the neural modulation of immunity. It was organized by the Nobel laureate Roger Guillemin, the immunologist Melvin Cohn, and the neuroscientist Theodore Melnechuk under the auspices of the Princess Lilian Foundation. I left this symposium convinced that the endocrine, nervous, and immune systems had to be understood not in isolation, but as interacting networks.

And at the same time, oh. At the same time, I began to suspect that this small and rather mysterious organ, the thymus, might sit precisely at the crossroads of those three systems. The thymus was first described by Galen, who was originally from Pergamon in Asia Minor, but worked mainly in ancient Rome. Through dissections of dogs and monkeys, he identified this organ and named it thymus because it resembled the leaf of Thymus cunila.

Galen considered the thymus to be a kind of protective cushion for the truncal vessels, protecting them from trauma like an airbag. Remarkably, he had already noticed one of the most striking features of the organ, the reduction in size with age. At the beginning of the 20th century, important progress was made by J. August Hammar in Sweden. He showed that thymic remnants persist in elderly individuals, which meant that involution after puberty is incomplete.

He also observed thymic hyperplasia in Graves' and Addison's diseases, acromegaly, myasthenia gravis, and after orchidectomy. Conversely, thymic involution occurs during pregnancy, undernourishment, and several infectious diseases. All these observations are still correct today. These associations forced the idea that the thymus might be an endocrine gland.

And this interpretation was further strengthened in 1946 when Hans Selye, the founder of stress biology, demonstrated that the alarm reaction leads to thymic involution together with adrenal hypertrophy. The next major steps in thymus research came with what were, quite simply, scientific breakthroughs. They were performed by two prominent scientists, Robert A. Good in the United States and the French-Australian Jacques F. A. P. Miller. In 1962, they independently demonstrated, using thymectomy, the importance of the thymus in immune development and function: generation of thymus-dependent T cells, production of antibodies, graft rejection, and resistance to infections.

Good performed pioneering studies on primary immune deficiencies, and Good syndrome associates thymoma, severe immunodeficiency, and some autoimmune manifestations. In 1968, Good led the team that performed the first successful bone marrow transplantation. Miller advanced in 1965 the hypothesis of thymic-specific hormones to regulate T cell differentiation. This was a reasonable hypothesis at the time, but it could not be confirmed thereafter.

Nevertheless, the thymus was considered an endocrine gland, and it retained the status of a primary lymphoid organ.

### Thymic microenvironment and central self-tolerance

Let us now look more closely at the thymic microenvironment, because this is where structure and function come together in a particularly striking and, I would say, elegant way. Thymic epithelial cells, or TECs, form the major stromal component of the thymus, and they are organized into cortical and medullary compartments. All TECs derive from the endoderm of the third pharyngeal pouch and depend on the transcription factor FOXN1. In the absence of FOXN1, the classic nude phenotype appears with severe immune deficiency.

The development of the thymic epithelium is also strongly induced by mesenchyme derived from the cephalic neural crest. Defects in that process underlie the DiGeorge syndrome. In addition to these epithelial cells, the thymus contains migratory populations such as dendritic cells, macrophages, and of course the developing T cells themselves. What is striking about the immense epithelial network surrounding very small thymocytes is that this is not a passive scaffold, but a highly organized educational environment.

And yet, despite Good's and Miller's pioneering works, skepticism about the thymus persisted for quite some time. In 1963, Peter Medawar, himself a Nobel laureate, still described the thymus as a vestigial organ and regarded thymic lymphocytes as an evolutionary accident of no great significance. In sharp contrast, the Nobel laureate Frank Macfarlane Burnet advanced a truly revolutionary idea. He proposed the concept of self-recognition leading to self-tolerance and predicted the thymus must eliminate self-reactive T cells, the so-called forbidden clones.

Even more importantly, he foresaw that failure of this elimination process would lead to autoimmune disease. Experimental proof of negative selection by apoptosis of self-reactive T cells came only in the late 1980s, through four landmark studies. One of the most elegant examples came from Harald von Boehmer's group at the Basel Institute of Immunology. They generated transgenic mice expressing a single T-cell receptor directed against the male H-Y antigen.

The result was striking. Female mice had a normal thymus with few apoptotic T cells, whereas male mice showed massive apoptosis in their thymus. So, when developing thymocytes recognize self, they are eliminated. This was a beautiful and compelling validation of Burnet's prediction.

This slide summarizes our current understanding of T cell differentiation in the thymus. Progenitor cells enter the thymus at the cortico-medullary junction and migrate to the outer cortex where they undergo intense proliferation. Then they cross the cortex and establish intimate contacts with cortical epithelial cells and thymic nurse cells. Thereafter, they enter the medulla where they encounter medullary epithelial cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages.

It is in the medulla that negative selection occurs. The process is extremely stringent, since only 2%–5% of the original progenitors ultimately reach the periphery. The key mechanism of central tolerance is the presentation of self-antigens by thymic epithelial cells and dendritic cells to developing T cells. And at that point, a fundamental question naturally arose: what exactly constitutes the self that is presented in the thymus?

### Oxytocin and the neuroendocrine self

At this point, I need to return to the beginning of my own scientific journey because, in many ways, it all started with oxytocin, the so-called love neurohormone. Oxytocin was the first peptide hormone to be identified in 1952 by Vincent du Vigneaud at Cornell. Its precursor, pro-oxytocin, is synthesized in neurons of the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus. It is then transported along axons to the neurohypophysis, where it is processed into oxytocin and its carrier protein neurophysin.

From the posterior pituitary, oxytocin is released into the circulation and exerts its two classic endocrine functions: milk ejection and uterine contraction. Beyond these classical actions, oxytocin also acts as a neuropeptide found in different central oxytocinergic pathways. These pathways are implicated in several higher cognitive functions, as listed on the right side of this slide. While reviewing the literature on oxytocin, I came across a paper from 1910 by Ott and Scott describing galactagogue effects of thymus and corpus luteum extracts.

That led us to a simple but rather bold question: could oxytocin be produced in the human thymus? The answer was yes.

We identified high, equimolar concentrations of oxytocin and neurophysin in human thymic extracts. Both peptides were localized in thymic epithelial cells, especially in the subcapsular cortex and in the medulla. We also detected pro-oxytocin transcripts in the thymic tissue. Thereafter, additional neuropeptides were identified in the thymus epithelium by us and other authors, and interestingly, a principle of biological parsimony organizes this repertoire.

For each neuroendocrine family, there appears to be a dominant thymic representative. In parallel with these findings, oxytocin immunoreactivity was detected in the cytoplasm of thymic nurse cells located in the subcapsular cortex. Thymic nurse cells are intriguing structures that seem to contribute to T-cell development, in particular CD8-positive T cells.

### From neurosecretion to cryptocrine signaling and antigen presentation

At that stage, however, we ran into what was not just a technical difficulty but a genuine conceptual obstacle. Oxytocin had served as the basic model for neurosecretion, as developed by Ernst and Berta Scharrer, Geoffrey Harris, and Vincent du Vigneaud. So, if oxytocin was present in thymic epithelial cells, one would expect typical neurosecretory granules and detectable secretion. However, electron microscopy and immunogold labeling revealed no secretory granules in TECs, and cultured TECs did not secrete oxytocin.

In 1990, John Funder introduced the concept of cryptocrine signaling, a mode of communication in which the ligand remains associated with the producing cell rather than being secreted in the classical way. For us, this was a genuine turning point. We stepped out of what I sometimes call the endocrine fishbowl and moved fully into immunological reasoning. And a new question became this: could thymic neuropeptides be presented to developing T cells by major histocompatibility complex proteins, as shown on this figure?

In other words, might they function as self-antigens rather than as hormones? Using the reference methodology established by Hans-Georg Rammensee in Tübingen, we were able to demonstrate that neurotensin is indeed presented by MHC class I molecules. In cultured human thymic epithelial cells, neurotensin was detected in the cytosol and in the plasma membrane, but not in the culture supernatants, again arguing against a classical secretion. Biochemical analyses then revealed not only intact neurotensin, but also smaller neurotensin-derived peptide fragments associated with MHC class I molecules.

Importantly, the C-terminal sequence of neurotensin contains amino acids that can bind most MHC class I alleles. Biologically, this was crucial because central tolerance to a self-peptide must be established across the broad diversity of HLA class I alleles present in the human population, rather than relying on a narrow allelic restriction. Applying the same method, we then showed that thymic oxytocin is presented to pre-T cells through a hybrid complex of MHC class I molecules and neurophysin. In the thymus, oxytocin thus acts as the self-antigen of the neurohypophyseal family.

Even more remarkably, neurophysin in the thymus fulfills the same carrier function it performs in the hypothalamic-neurohypophyseal axis: it transports oxytocin to the plasma membrane of thymic epithelial cells. This mechanism has a double selective advantage. First, it allows the presentation of oxytocin, a cyclic peptide; and second, as with neurotensin, tolerance to oxytocin is not tightly restricted by MHC class I alleles.

### Principles of the neuroendocrine self

At this stage, we were able to define what we call the neuroendocrine self. Several principles emerge. First, neuroendocrine self-antigens generally correspond to the dominant member of a neuroendocrine family expressed in the thymus. Second, these dominant members tend to contain amino acid sequences conserved throughout evolution.

Third, we observed that oxytocin transcription appears during development earlier in the thymus than in the hypothalamus, which is conceptually striking. Fourth, these neuroendocrine self-antigens are not secreted, but are presented to developing T cells in association with MHC molecules. And finally, because oxytocin expression is much higher in the thymus than vasopressin, immune tolerance to oxytocin is considerably stronger. This is fundamental since oxytocin intervenes in different stages of reproduction, while the role of vasopressin is restricted to the regulation of vascular tone and water homeostasis.

This may also explain why autoimmune diabetes insipidus is sometimes observed. Whereas autoimmunity directed against oxytocin has never been reported, a similar concept probably applies to IGF-2 and insulin. IGF-2 plays a major role in fetal growth and ontogeny, while the role of insulin, if I can say, is limited to glucose homeostasis. It is extremely difficult to break immune tolerance to IGF-2, whereas insulin represents the major primary autoantigen in type 1 diabetes.

### The dual role of thymic pro-oxyphysin

We can now understand the dual role of the thymic oxytocin precursor pro-oxyphysin during T-cell differentiation. On the one hand, this precursor generates an oxytocin ligand targeted to the outer surface of thymic epithelial cells. We have demonstrated that after contact, the oxytocin receptors on developing T cells transduce this signal via the phosphoinositide pathway and the phosphorylation of several focal adhesion kinases. This leads to the formation of the immune synapse, thereby providing a positive developmental signal.

On the other hand, the same precursor also generates a self-antigen oxytocin presented in association with MHC molecules. This helps delete T-cell clones reactive to oxytocin and supports the positive selection of FOXP3-positive thymic regulatory T cells. So, depending on whether oxytocin acts as a cryptocrine ligand or as a presented self-antigen, one and the same molecular system recapitulates the dual function of the thymus in T-cell development and negative selection.

### Evolution of adaptive immunity and the thymus

Our findings also need to be placed in the larger framework of evolution. Throughout evolution, the neuroendocrine system and innate immunity have coexisted in relative harmony. Indeed, pattern recognition receptors, including toll-like receptors, do not seem to have the ability to react against normal self. Then, about 450 million years ago, adaptive immunity emerged together with the recombination enzymes RAG1 and RAG2, an event sometimes called the Big Bang of immunology.

This innovation created an enormous diversity in T-cell and B-cell receptors—up to 10^18 combinations for T-cell receptors. But it also introduced a vast and potentially catastrophic risk of self-reactivity. In remarkable parallel with Ehrlich's idea of horror autotoxicus, the first unique thymus appeared at precisely that period. Its primary function, therefore, was not simply to generate T cells, but to protect self from the dangers created by adaptive immunity itself.

Even lampreys possess related thymoid structures, pointing to deep evolutionary roots. And so, the transcription and presentation of neuroendocrine precursors by thymic epithelial cells were key mechanisms that allowed a peaceful coevolution of adaptive immunity and the neuroendocrine system.

### Tissue-specific antigens and Bruno Kyewski's contribution

At this point, I would also like to pause and pay tribute to my dear friend Bruno Kyewski in Heidelberg. Bruno made a major contribution by demonstrating that, beyond neuroendocrine self-antigens, the thymus also expresses a variable repertoire of tissue-specific antigens in medullary thymic epithelial cells. This is the case for thyroid, testis, pancreas, or parathyroid specific antigens. This greatly expanded our understanding of central tolerance.

The thymus does not educate T cells only to the neuroendocrine self; it also exposes them to a broad representation of the organism itself. In 2014–2015, with my support, a Princess Lilian Chair visitorship in Belgium was offered to Bruno. Very sadly, my friend died prematurely in 2018, but his contribution will remain foundational forever.

### EURO-THYMAIDE and the genesis of autoimmunity

Between 2004 and 2009, I had the privilege of coordinating EURO-THYMAIDE, a large European integrated project funded by the European Union. The central objective of this consortium was to understand autoimmune diseases through a deep investigation of thymic biology and central tolerance. It brought together 20 academic laboratories and five biotechnology companies, creating a truly multidisciplinary effort around one unifying question: how does failure of thymic education translate into autoimmune pathology? This brings me to what is, in my view, one of the most important and perhaps most far-reaching implications of thymic biology, the origin of autoimmune disease.

As early as 2001, we demonstrated that IGF-2 transcription is defective in the thymus of diabetes-prone BB rats, a classical animal model of human type 1 diabetes. What made this particularly important was that the proportion of rats with thymic IGF-2 deficiency significantly correlated with the incidence of diabetes in that strain. This finding strongly suggested that impaired thymic IGF-2 expression may be a primary event in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diabetes. For obvious reasons, we could not verify this hypothesis in human thymus.

A landmark study by Mark Anderson and Diane Mathis then provided some of the strongest evidence that thymic dysfunction can directly initiate autoimmunity.

### AIRE, FEZF2, and thymic mimetic cells

This work followed the identification of the autoimmune regulator gene, AIRE, by a European consortium. AIRE expression was found to be maximal in medullary thymic epithelial cells. In Aire-deficient mice, intrathymic transcription of many self-antigens was profoundly reduced, including oxytocin, neuropeptide Y, IGF-2, and insulin. Even more compelling, transplantation of Aire-deficient thymic tissue into normal mice induced multiple peripheral autoimmune manifestations.

That experiment established a direct causal link between a defect in thymic self-antigen expression and autoimmunity. This was a decisive confirmation of the conceptual framework we had been building. A second breakthrough came with the identification of the transcription factor FEZF2 as another essential regulator of central immune tolerance in medullary thymic epithelial cells. Central tolerance does not rely on a simple transcriptional program; it depends on at least two complementary systems, AIRE and FEZF2, which together broaden the representation of self within the thymus and strengthen the robustness of tolerance.

The last layer of complexity in this field emerged with the recent discovery of thymic mimetic cells by Diane Mathis's laboratory. This important work shows that thymic epithelial diversity is far greater than we had previously imagined. In some way, this modern molecular view echoes very old histological observations, such as the thymic myoid cells described by Mayer in 1888 and later by Dustin in Brussels in 1914. More recently, single-cell transcriptomic studies have shown that FEZF2 regulates not only tissue-specific antigen expression, but also the differentiation of mimetic cells that resemble peripheral tissues.

The thymus is therefore even more sophisticated than we had imagined. It is a mosaic of cells representing peripheral microenvironments. It is now clear that thymic dysfunction plays a central role in the development of autoimmune disease.

### Thymic dysfunction drives autoimmunity

Under physiological conditions, neuroendocrine self-antigens and tissue-specific antigens are transcribed in thymic epithelial cells, especially medullary epithelial cells, under the control of AIRE and FEZF2. Their presentation promotes both the deletion of self-reactive T cells and generation of self-specific thymic regulatory T cells. By contrast, reduced expression or defective presentation of self-antigen has now been documented in several pathological settings listed on this slide. This leads to the priming of an autoimmune response, with an enrichment of the peripheral T cell repertoire in forbidden self-reactive T cells, together with a decrease in the generation of thymic regulatory T cells.

Therefore, susceptibility to autoimmunity may already be programmed during fetal life. But that initial imprinting is usually not enough by itself to produce overt disease, and environmental triggers are also critically important: viral infections, immune checkpoint inhibitors, nutritional factors, endocrine disruptors, changes in microbiota and chronic stress can all participate in revealing a pre-existing thymic vulnerability.

### Self, non-self, and central immune tolerance

At this point, I would like to submit to you some reflections about the concept of central immune self-tolerance. Burnet famously defined immunology as the science of self and non-self. He never spoke in terms of discrimination between self and non-self, but he was pointing to a biological education to self. A lymphocyte is not intrinsically capable of distinguishing self from non-self.

Within the thymus, only education to self occurs. Outside the thymus, under normal conditions, immune reactivity is directed only against non-self. There is also an interesting semantic issue here. The prefix auto derives from Greek and evokes spontaneity of action, like Aristotle's “first motor,” whereas the term self, from Latin roots, suggests a more reflective and organized identity.

In English, self-antigen and auto-antigen are often treated as equivalent terms, but I firmly believe that a crucial distinction must be made between immunogenic auto-antigens such as vasopressin or insulin and tolerogenic self-antigens such as oxytocin and IGF-2.

### Conclusion

Let me now conclude this long, fascinating, and still unfinished journey through the thymus. What began as the story of a supposedly vestigial organ has, in fact, become the story of one of the most sophisticated regulators of biological identity. The thymus, I would argue, is the great architect of the biological self and the unique orchestrator of central immune tolerance to both neuroendocrine and peripheral-tissue self-antigens. The fundamental mechanism underlying this tolerance is the active transcription and presentation of self-antigens by thymic epithelial cells, in particular medullary thymic epithelial cells.

When this process fails, autoimmunity begins. Environmental factors then act as triggers that reveal a scenario already written in fetal and early life. I believe this concept may open the way to genuinely new strategies for preventing and treating autoimmune diseases, and more broadly, thymic integrity may prove to be one of the great determinants of lifelong immune fitness, longevity, and resistance to disease.
