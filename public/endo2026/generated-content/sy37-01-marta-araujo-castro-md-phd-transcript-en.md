## Definition, etiology, and prevalence

Adrenal incidentalomas are adrenal masses detected on imaging performed for reasons unrelated to suspected adrenal disease. The following situations are excluded from this definition: lesions detected during screening for hereditary syndromes associated with a high risk of adrenal tumors, lesions detected during evaluation or follow-up of extra-adrenal tumor disease, and lesions smaller than 1 cm.

The etiology of adrenal incidentalomas varies and includes benign and malignant lesions derived from the adrenal cortex or medulla, as well as masses of extra-adrenal origin. The proportions of the different etiologies vary by series and definition, but the most frequent cause in all series is an adrenocortical adenoma, representing approximately 70% of cases. We must take into account that approximately 10% of lesions may be malignant, approximately 10% produce overt functional syndromes such as Cushing syndrome, primary aldosteronism, or pheochromocytoma, and 20%–40% are associated with MACS. Thus, the prevalence of non-functioning adrenal incidentalomas, or NFAI, ranges from 40% to 70%.

Adrenal incidentalomas are a frequent reason for consultation in endocrinology. They are present in 2%–3% of the general population and in up to 7%–10% of people older than 70 years. On the other hand, they are uncommon in patients younger than 18 years. Less than 1% of adrenal tumors occur in this age group, in which the risks of both malignancy and hormone secretion are higher. The incidence of adrenal incidentalomas has increased tenfold over the past two decades, in parallel with the increased use of CT and MRI. They are also more frequent in patients with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension.

When an adrenal incidentaloma is detected, two issues should be addressed: whether the lesion is malignant and whether it is functionally active. The latest European Society of Endocrinology guideline proposes that no further evaluation is required if the lesion is benign and non-functioning. During this presentation, however, we will review whether that recommendation is entirely correct.

## Is the 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test reliable?

The first question is whether the 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test, or DST, is a reliable marker of hypercortisolism in these patients. The classical definition of NFAI includes lesions that do not produce excess hormones: no autonomous cortisol secretion, defined by post-DST cortisol below 1.8 µg/dL; no pheochromocytoma when clinical or radiologic findings raise suspicion; no primary aldosteronism in patients with hypertension or hypokalemia; and no clinical evidence of androgen or estrogen excess.

The most important question is whether the DST is a reliable marker of hypercortisolism. False-positive DST results are quite frequent. Many physiologic, pathologic, and pharmacologic situations can produce false-positive results. Drugs that accelerate dexamethasone metabolism include phenobarbital, phenytoin, carbamazepine, pioglitazone, and others. Ideally, the drug would be discontinued and the test repeated to determine whether the result was false positive. If that is not possible, serum dexamethasone can be measured by mass spectrometry.

One study measured both serum dexamethasone and post-DST cortisol in 1,300 patients, 162 of whom were controls. The best threshold for confirming adequate suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis was 140 ng/dL, equivalent to 3.6 nmol/L. With that threshold, 11% of cases were false-positive DST results. The most common causes were failure to take dexamethasone the preceding night, use of interacting drugs, and gastrointestinal tract abnormalities.

Because of our concern about false-positive results, our center performed a prospective cross-sectional study of 140 consecutive patients with adrenal incidentalomas who had simultaneous serum dexamethasone and post-DST cortisol measurements. Using a serum dexamethasone threshold of 3.3 nmol/L, 3.6% of patients had inadequate dexamethasone exposure and therefore false-positive DST results.

There is no consensus about the serum dexamethasone threshold that confirms an adequate level for suppressing the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. Ueland and colleagues proposed 3.3 nmol/L, whereas Ceccato and colleagues proposed a higher threshold of 4.5 nmol/L.

A retrospective study of 1,603 patients with adrenal incidentalomas found a significant nonlinear effect of age on post-DST cortisol, although age explained only 2.4% of the variance. Patients older than 70 years consistently exhibited post-DST cortisol levels above 1.8 µg/dL, both at baseline and during follow-up. The investigators concluded that post-DST cortisol increases with age and highlighted the possible need for age-tailored diagnosis and management of MACS in older patients.

In another study, we evaluated whether DST was a reliable marker of comorbidities in patients with adrenal incidentalomas. We included 823 patients and found that the diagnostic accuracy of DST for predicting MACS-related comorbidities was poor, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, or AUROC, of approximately 0.6. The best combination of sensitivity and specificity for identifying patients with at least one MACS-related comorbidity occurred at 1.5 µg/dL, below the conventional threshold of 1.8 µg/dL. This suggests that the sensitivity of the 1.8-µg/dL threshold may be insufficient for detecting cardiometabolic burden.

Another study assessed post-DST cortisol across body mass index categories and its association with metabolic parameters. It included 352 patients. The frequency of false-positive results was low: 0% in patients with a body mass index of 30–40 kg/m², 1% at 40–50 kg/m², and 2.3% above 50 kg/m². Elevated post-DST cortisol in patients with obesity should therefore not be attributed to body weight alone; it requires appropriate evaluation to rule out hypercortisolism.

The other possibility is that DST produces false-negative results. Several studies propose that a lower threshold, around 1.2 µg/dL, might better capture at-risk individuals, although this would reduce specificity and increase false-positive results. Those studies found greater risks of hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease among people with post-DST values above approximately 1.2 µg/dL, and sometimes at still lower thresholds.

## Metabolomic evidence in apparently non-functioning lesions

In relation to possible false-negative results, I will discuss metabolomic studies in adrenal incidentalomas. In a study performed at our center, we included 24 patients with NFAI, 24 controls without adrenal incidentalomas, and 25 patients with MACS, matched for age, sex, and body mass index. Compared with healthy controls, patients with NFAI had lower androgen excretion and higher urinary free cortisol excretion. Patients with hypertension in either the NFAI or control group had lower androgen-metabolite levels and higher urinary free cortisol than participants without hypertension.

These findings suggest that many patients with so-called NFAI may overproduce hormones in quantities too small to be detected by classical tests but sufficient to adversely affect their cardiometabolic profile.

Similarly, the Kotłowska study included 20 patients with NFAI and 25 controls. Total urinary cortisol metabolites were significantly increased in patients with NFAI. The pattern suggested impaired 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 activity and slightly increased 5β-reductase activity.

In another study, we analyzed the relationship between DST and the urinary steroid profile. We included 25 patients with MACS and 24 with NFAI. Post-DST cortisol correlated positively, but modestly, with total glucocorticoid-metabolite excretion, with the strongest correlations for tetrahydro-11-deoxycortisol, or THS, and tetrahydrocortisol, or THF.

Post-DST cortisol alone had fair accuracy for predicting MACS-related comorbidities, with an AUROC of 0.767. Accuracy improved to an AUROC of 0.853 when post-DST cortisol was combined with urinary cortisone, α-cortol, THS, and serum dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, or DHEAS.

The main studies comparing urinary steroid profiles in patients with NFAI and controls without adrenal disease show increased urinary cortisol metabolites, a tendency toward increased tetrahydrometabolites of cortisol and cortisone, and lower androgen-metabolite excretion in NFAI.

## Cardiometabolic risk

Although the evidence is less extensive than it is for MACS, apparently non-functioning adrenal incidentalomas are associated with greater cardiometabolic risk than the general population, including higher risks of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease.

Ribeiro Cavalari and colleagues evaluated 74 patients with NFAI and 90 controls. The prevalence of metabolic syndrome was significantly higher in patients with NFAI than in controls across several definitions of metabolic syndrome. Another study included 154 patients with NFAI and 462 controls. Diabetes and hypertension were significantly more prevalent in the NFAI group; hypertension was present in 37.0% versus 20.1% of controls.

The Favero meta-analysis likewise found that diabetes and prediabetes were approximately twice as prevalent in patients with NFAI as in controls. In a cohort with a mean follow-up of 7.2 years, NFAI was associated with a significantly greater risk of incident composite diabetes—diabetes or prediabetes—than no adrenal tumor, with an adjusted relative risk of 1.87. The prevalence of diabetes increased with higher post-DST cortisol, even while values remained below 1.8 µg/dL. The group with values from 1.4 to 1.8 µg/dL had the greatest risk.

Another meta-analysis found that patients with NFAI had approximately twice the odds of type 2 diabetes and had higher fasting plasma glucose and HOMA values than controls. Among patients with NFAI who did not have glucose disorders at baseline, the pooled incidence of type 2 diabetes was 6%.

Regarding hypertension, one study included 40 patients with NFAI and 40 controls without adrenal disease. Hypertension was significantly more prevalent in the NFAI group, 72.5% versus 47.5%. Resistant hypertension and the use of three or more antihypertensive drugs were also more frequent. Patients with NFAI and hypertension had higher mean post-DST cortisol than those without hypertension, approximately 1.3 versus 1.0 µg/dL. Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified post-DST cortisol of 1.03 µg/dL as the best predictor of hypertension in these patients.

In the Tuna study, hypertension was present in 39.3% of patients with NFAI versus 12.2% of controls. Masked hypertension has also been described more often in NFAI: 25.5% versus 9.5% in one study. A meta-analysis reported a 1.9-fold higher prevalence of hypertension in NFAI. Studies of resistant or severe hypertension also found a significantly greater risk in NFAI, with an odds ratio of approximately 5.

A more recent study included 80 patients with NFAI and 80 controls matched for age, sex, and body mass index. Patients with NFAI had higher Framingham and American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology risk scores. Within the NFAI group, independent predictors of the Framingham risk score were DST and waist-to-hip ratio. Predictors of the AHA/ACC score were DST, waist-to-hip ratio, and fasting plasma glucose.

In a Spanish study of 593 patients with NFAI, cardiovascular disease was significantly more prevalent among those with post-DST cortisol above 0.9 µg/dL than among those at or below that threshold, even after adjustment for age. A threshold of 1.4 µg/dL was useful for predicting MACS: patients above 1.4 µg/dL developed MACS in 23.5% of cases, compared with 7.44% among those at or below 1.4 µg/dL.

Considering all of this evidence, the classical tests used to assess steroid excess in adrenal incidentalomas may lack the sensitivity and specificity to identify subtle but clinically relevant alterations in adrenal steroid metabolism. The term *non-functioning adrenal incidentaloma* may therefore be inaccurate and misleading. We have proposed the term *adrenal lesions of undetermined secretion of adrenal steroids*, or ALUSAS.

## Adrenalectomy in NFAI

Some studies report improved blood-pressure control after adrenalectomy in patients with NFAI. One study included 271 patients who underwent adrenalectomy—127 with Cushing syndrome, 45 with MACS, and 99 with NFAI—and 813 matched controls. Antihypertensive-drug use decreased after adrenalectomy in all patient groups, including NFAI.

In the well-known Bancos meta-analysis, eight studies reported clinical outcomes before and after adrenalectomy in NFAI. Five studies included 54 patients with hypertension; 21 patients, or 42%, had improved blood-pressure control after adrenalectomy. The differences for type 2 diabetes and dyslipidemia were not statistically significant.

At our hospital, we evaluated blood-pressure outcomes after adrenalectomy in 16 patients with NFAI and compared them with 470 patients managed conservatively. The surgical group had a significant improvement in systolic blood pressure relative to the conservative group, with a mean decrease of 11.1 mmHg. A more recent study reported that 50.9% of patients with non-functioning adrenal adenomas and hypertension had improved blood-pressure control one month after surgery.

## Risk of progression to MACS and tumor growth

The Elhassan meta-analysis reported that 4.3% of patients with NFAI progressed to MACS during a mean follow-up of 50.3 months. The risks of progression to an overt functional syndrome—Cushing syndrome, primary aldosteronism, or pheochromocytoma—were negligible, below 0.1%. Only 2.5% of adrenal incidentalomas increased in size by at least 10 mm during a mean follow-up of approximately 41.5 months.

A later 10-year longitudinal study reported progression to MACS in 15 of 67 patients, or 22%. The risk was higher among patients with overweight or obesity: 42.1% versus 18.2% among those of normal weight. Enlargement of NFAI occurred in 56.7%, but clinically significant enlargement of at least 10 mm occurred in only six patients.

We also reported data from a Spanish series of 654 patients with NFAI. After a median follow-up of 41.3 months, 10.5% developed autonomous cortisol secretion. Sixty percent of the cases occurred during the first five years of follow-up. The risk was higher among patients with higher post-DST cortisol at diagnosis. Nevertheless, the AUROC of baseline DST for predicting autonomous cortisol secretion was only 0.69, and the best threshold was 1.4 µg/dL.

Significant tumor growth occurred in 5.2% of cases. It was more common in women than in men: 10.5% versus 1.0%, with a hazard ratio of 10.7.

## Conclusions

Patients with NFAI have a higher prevalence of insulin resistance, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular alterations than healthy controls without adrenal tumors. They have subtle alterations in cortisol metabolism that are not detected by classical tests and that may contribute to this adverse profile. A comprehensive metabolomic approach may detect these alterations. Adrenalectomy may improve blood-pressure control in some patients with NFAI. Depending on the definition and duration of follow-up, the risk of progression to MACS is approximately 5%–20%.
