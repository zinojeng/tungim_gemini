## Androgen-receptor signaling in ER-positive breast cancer

**Dillon P. Boulton:** Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women in the United States, with an estimated 300,000 new diagnoses in 2026. Approximately 80% of breast cancers express estrogen receptor (ER). Endocrine therapies that target ER directly, or suppress estrogen synthesis through aromatase inhibitors, have greatly improved prognosis. Despite these therapies, however, up to 52% of women may still experience recurrence with metastatic disease.

One of our laboratory's main goals is to develop new therapeutic options, with particular focus on compensatory androgen-receptor (AR) transcriptional activity. Our group and others have shown that AR is expressed in approximately 90% of ER-positive breast cancers and can drive endocrine-therapy resistance, tumor progression, and metastasis.

An active area of investigation is how AR transcriptional activity within breast-cancer cells affects the surrounding tumor microenvironment. This matters because immunotherapies that harness the immune system to clear tumors are notoriously ineffective in ER-positive breast cancer. These tumors contain many pro-tumor immune cells and relatively few anti-tumor immune cells. We asked whether inhibiting compensatory AR signaling could shift that balance and make the tumors more responsive to immunotherapy.

## Fulvestrant with or without enzalutamide

We collaborated on a randomized phase II neoadjuvant clinical trial with two treatment arms: the ER degrader fulvestrant alone, or fulvestrant combined with the AR antagonist enzalutamide. We collected pretreatment and on-treatment biopsies and used them for a multipronged analysis of tumor transcripts and multiplex immunohistochemistry to determine how AR inhibition affects the tumor immune microenvironment.

Fulvestrant alone did not substantially alter the immune-cell populations in the microenvironment. Adding enzalutamide invigorated the immune environment: the combination reduced inhibitory pro-tumor populations—including regulatory T cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells—and increased anti-tumor immune structures, including tertiary lymphoid structures.

Our future work will examine the mechanisms by which AR signaling in breast-cancer cells shapes the surrounding immune microenvironment. The ultimate goal is to determine whether targeting AR itself or its downstream mediators can best synergize with immunotherapies that are otherwise ineffective in ER-positive breast cancer.
