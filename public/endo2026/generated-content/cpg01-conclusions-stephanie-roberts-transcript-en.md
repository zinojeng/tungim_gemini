## Handoff and conclusions

**Anders Juul:** I will now leave the floor to my co-chair, Stephanie Roberts, who will conclude what we have just told you.

**Stephanie A. Roberts:** Great. Thank you. We will wrap up with conclusions, limitations, and research needs. We also want to share some of the available resources that summarize our recommendations.

In summary, for girls ages 7–8 years with initial Tanner stage B2 breast development, we suggest watchful waiting with periodic physical examinations rather than immediate laboratory or radiologic evaluation. For girls younger than 7 years with initial Tanner B2 breast development, we suggest a 4- to 6-month period of observation to distinguish unsustained or slowly progressive puberty from rapidly progressive puberty before beginning diagnostic evaluation.

When further evaluation is indicated, we suggest obtaining a basal serum LH concentration using an ultrasensitive assay. We suggest that routine brain MRI should not be performed in girls ages 6–8 years or boys ages 8–9 years with central precocious puberty and no CNS findings.

We also suggest against routine genetic testing, although it should be considered when there is a positive family history of central precocious puberty. These diagnostic recommendations are summarized in an algorithm included in the guideline.

GnRH agonists remain first-line treatment for central precocious puberty, but treatment should be individualized because gains in adult height may be modest in some patient populations. If treating, we suggest beginning with the formulation intended for long-term use and monitoring treatment with clinical assessment alone unless there is concern for inadequate pubertal suppression.

We suggest against routine addition of adjunctive recombinant growth hormone. We also suggest against routinely continuing GnRH agonist treatment beyond chronological age 10–11 years in girls or 11–12 years in boys and/or bone age 11–12 years in girls or 12–13 years in boys. A second figure in the publication summarizes these treatment recommendations.

## Guideline strengths and limitations

I want to highlight several strengths of the guideline. We used the best available evidence collected through careful systematic literature reviews, in consultation with the Mayo Clinic Evidence-Based Practice Research Program. The panel adhered to a rigorous guideline-development methodology—the GRADE methodology introduced earlier. Panel members made good-faith efforts to document key considerations and judgments, explicitly tie decision-making to the available evidence, and transparently communicate the rationale for each recommendation.

There were also several limitations. Direct evidence was unavailable for some clinical questions. The panel wanted to examine certain study subgroup analyses, such as different age categories, but this was not possible for many outcomes. Few identified studies had large patient cohorts or randomized controlled designs, with the caveat that such rigorous clinical studies are less common in pediatric endocrinology overall.

Most reported studies in central precocious puberty included only girls. Consequently, for some clinical questions, data were extrapolated to boys or no recommendation was provided for boys.

## Research considerations

These evidence gaps generated several research priorities:

- Investigate rates of progression, age at menarche, and long-term outcomes in girls with onset of thelarche between ages 7 and 8 years.
- Conduct randomized controlled trials comparing GnRH agonist treatment with observation alone.
- Study short- and long-term outcomes, including bone mineral density, cognition, and psychosocial well-being, in boys and in girls stratified by age at onset of thelarche.
- Conduct randomized controlled trials examining discontinuation of GnRH agonist therapy according to chronological age versus bone age, predicted adult height, and/or midparental target height.

In addition to the guideline, which was published this morning, the systematic review referenced throughout this presentation was published as a separate paper. A pocket guide is available here today, along with a patient resource and a decision aid to support clinical decision-making.

Thank you for your attention. We will now open the question-and-answer portion.

## Audience Q&A

### Observation and the risk of rapid progression

**Moderator:** If you elect a period of observation before workup, might you lose valuable time if someone has rapidly progressive puberty?

**Panelist:** It all depends on how long you wait. If you wait a very long time, a patient could shift to rapidly progressive precocious puberty without you recognizing it, and that may become a substantial problem. But if you plan follow-up in 4–6 months and are confident that the patient will return within that interval, the likelihood of losing time and potentially losing some adult height is much lower. If follow-up occurs within the proposed timeframe, I do not think there is a major risk of losing valuable time before starting therapy.

### Value and limitations of an initial bone age

**Moderator:** Wouldn't a bone age obtained at initial presentation provide valuable information that might guide how closely to monitor the patient?

**Panelist:** There are relatively little data on the validity of bone age when a patient presents at a young age with Tanner stage B2 breast development alone. If the patient has only breast development—with no pubic hair and no acceleration in height velocity—the likelihood of a markedly advanced bone age is low.

Even if bone age is not advanced, however, the patient may later develop rapidly progressive central puberty. Bone age has relatively low sensitivity and specificity for determining whether rapidly progressive puberty will develop. It may be useful, but it is not a gold-standard marker. Bone age can be completely compatible with chronological age, and the patient may still later show rapid pubertal progression.

### Selecting genes for familial central precocious puberty

**Moderator:** If you perform genetic testing, would you test `MKRN3` first, or should all three genes be tested at the same time?

**Panelist:** It depends on the family history. A clear history of early puberty on the paternal side increases the likelihood of an `MKRN3` mutation, both mechanistically and statistically. In an ideal world, it would be helpful to have a panel that tests multiple genes simultaneously, but I do not believe such a panel is currently available everywhere, including in the United States.

**Panelist:** Familial cases are diagnosed from a history of central precocious puberty in first-, second-, or third-degree relatives. Familial central precocious puberty is fairly common—around 25% of cases. I agree that `MKRN3` should be evaluated in familial cases with paternal or undetermined transmission.

**Moderator:** If genetic testing identifies a mutation, what would you recommend for family members?

**Panelist:** This creates an opportunity for preclinical diagnosis in siblings and other close relatives. Once a mutation is recognized in an index case, relatives—especially brothers and sisters—can be offered genetic testing and followed. If they carry the variant, the likelihood of premature sexual development may be high, particularly in girls. Reported penetrance of pathogenic `MKRN3` variants has been very high in girls, which allows earlier clinical surveillance and diagnosis in close relatives.

### Which age should guide MRI decisions?

**Moderator:** Regarding MRI and other age-based decisions, should the decision be based on age at presentation or estimated age at pubertal initiation?

**Panelist:** We often do not know exactly when central precocious puberty began. The decision is therefore based on age at presentation, which is also what the systematic review used.

### Who is most likely to benefit from GnRH agonist treatment?

**Moderator:** What characteristics suggest that GnRH agonist treatment might be preferred, versus characteristics of patients who are less likely to benefit?

**Panelist:** I assume the question refers to predictors of the best effect, for example preservation of adult height. The best predictors—although the evidence is not strong—are younger chronological age at diagnosis and greater bone-age advancement. These features predict more impaired adult height and may therefore suggest greater potential benefit. But the evidence regarding who benefits most is not very strong.

### Individualizing when treatment stops

**Moderator:** Why wouldn't the decision to stop treatment be individualized?

**Panelist:** In most cases, it should be individualized. In the guideline, however, we tried to provide age ranges that clinicians could use as guidance. We found no evidence that continuing therapy longer preserves adult height, which is why we suggest against routine continuation beyond those ranges. There are very few studies and no randomized controlled trials. That is also why comparing different stopping time points is a research recommendation.

**Moderator:** So this is a suggestion, and clinicians can proceed as appropriate for an individual patient.

### Cosyntropin stimulation testing and nonclassic CAH

**Moderator:** Is there a role for cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation testing to exclude nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia?

**Panelist:** In boys, pubic hair tends to be a relatively late event in normal central pubertal development. In girls, breast development is the best clinical marker of ovarian function. If a patient has only breast development, or a boy has testicular enlargement at an otherwise appropriate stage, the likelihood of congenital adrenal hyperplasia is relatively low.

It is different when a patient presents with pubic hair, body odor, acne, and accelerated height velocity, together with relatively small testicular volumes in a boy and/or no breast development in a girl. In that setting, an ACTH stimulation test—or even an initial 17-hydroxyprogesterone measurement—would be very appropriate. In the absence of pubic hair or marked bone-age advancement, and when breast development or testicular enlargement supports central puberty, an ACTH test may not be especially helpful.

### Psychosocial outcomes and family anxiety

**Moderator:** Did the panel consider psychosocial parameters in crafting its recommendations?

**Panelist:** The systematic review found no studies that allowed a recommendation regarding psychosocial sequelae. Psychosocial outcomes were definitely outcomes of interest, and the reviewers looked for evidence, but there is no consensus.

One could argue that central precocious puberty and early menarche are associated with considerable immediate psychosocial discomfort for many girls, as well as an increased risk of mental health disorders later in life. But no studies have established whether GnRH agonist treatment affects these outcomes.

**Panelist:** In this setting, I am sometimes more concerned about the psychological well-being of the family than that of the patient. Mothers and fathers may be distraught that their 7-year-old has breast development and assume she will have menarche at age 8. They therefore feel that something must be done. This is a major issue that we all face in practice: families are often more concerned than the patient about sexual development.

We need to respect biology and physiology and explain that this may be within the range of normal. We may not need to intervene immediately. The likelihood that a girl will have menarche at age 8 simply because breast development began at age 7 is relatively low. Menarche may occur early, but generally not that early. We should take our time, follow the child, and address the anxiety that families experience.

### Continuing treatment in an 11-year-old girl

**Moderator:** What about a girl who is 11 years old but whose bone age remains more than 1 year advanced? Wouldn't it be better to treat longer if predicted adult height is improving and height velocity remains normal?

**Panelist:** There was no evidence that prolonging therapy beyond the age of peers provides benefit for either psychosocial or adult-height outcomes. That is why we suggest against routinely prolonging therapy and inducing delayed puberty. Again, it is a suggestion, not an absolute rule.

### Ultrasensitive sex steroids versus ultrasensitive LH

**Moderator:** Please comment on the importance of ultrasensitive estradiol or testosterone compared with an ultrasensitive LH assay. These are typically obtained together during the initial laboratory evaluation, not LH alone.

**Panelist:** Testosterone measurement is important and valuable for diagnosing central precocious puberty in boys. We do not have the same experience with estradiol in girls. Girls with central precocious puberty can have a normal estradiol concentration; approximately 40% have estradiol values in the prepubertal range. Estradiol is therefore not a reliable standalone diagnostic marker for central precocious puberty.

**Panelist:** I would add that there is a paucity of data using estradiol measured by mass spectrometry. That limitation probably contributes to our conclusion that estradiol is not useful in this condition, because very few studies used mass spectrometry, which is the reference method.

### Ethics of a treatment-versus-observation trial

**Moderator:** Is it ethical to randomize patients to treatment versus no treatment in a proposed randomized controlled trial?

**Panelist:** It is an intriguing suggestion. I do not think an institutional review board would approve such a study now. That ship has sailed. But I wish we could go back and perform it.

### Evidence for treatment-stopping cutoffs

**Moderator:** Can you show the data supporting the suggested cutoffs for stopping treatment?

**Panelist:** Are you referring to the slide? The supporting discussion is in the paper. I did not show a systematic review because there were no eligible data. The publication reports the papers and considerations on which this judgment was based.

**Panelist:** The panel concluded that there is no good evidence of benefit from extending GnRH agonist therapy beyond the suggested ranges.

This question comes up frequently: What should we do when the evidence has very low certainty? Some people are tempted to say that perhaps we should do nothing because we lack good data. Although it is disappointing that we do not have high-quality evidence that gives us confidence in the estimates, most of us do not feel comfortable simply throwing up our hands and saying that nothing can be done.

We look as carefully as possible for the most relevant, best available evidence and make judgments from it. If that low-certainty evidence still gives reasonable confidence that people who choose one option are more likely to derive benefit than harm, clinicians should help patients work through those tradeoffs and suggest the option more likely to help.

In general, if a panel can reach a recommendation that helps guide clinicians, I favor doing so. A panel can state that either option is acceptable, but that may leave clinicians uncertain about what to do. If the panel can reasonably support one option over another, I favor making a conditional suggestion even when certainty is low or very low.

**Moderator:** I think that is all the questions. Thank you to all the panelists, and thank you to the audience for staying and asking these wonderful questions.
