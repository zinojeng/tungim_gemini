### CHH and GnRH-neuron development

And so congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism is a rare genetic condition characterized by absent or incomplete pubertal development due to impaired GnRH production, secretion, or action.

It is a clinically and genetically heterogeneous condition, more common in males than in females, with a proportion of about 5 to 1, and it can be sporadic or inherited in different patterns as X-linked, autosomal recessive, and autosomal dominant.

And although it is a rare condition, its complexity is a great model for us to study the biology of the gonadotropic axis, especially the GnRH neuron network, and this knowledge can be expanded for diagnosis and treatment in novel reproductive disorders.

In about half of the cases, CHH is associated with olfactory dysfunction, anosmia or hyposmia, characterizing Kallmann syndrome, as opposed to normosmic CHH when olfaction is normal.

And this happens because in the embryonic life, GnRH neurons originate outside the central nervous system in the olfactory placode and migrate alongside the olfactory fibers through the cribriform plate until they find their destination in the hypothalamus.

So a defect in any factor, any protein that is involved in the regulation of this process can cause Kallmann syndrome.

### Evolution of molecular diagnosis

Talking about the genetic landscape, 25 years ago, the landscape was pretty much empty like this.

So on one side we had ANOS1, at the time still called KAL1, causing the X-linked form of Kallmann syndrome.

And then since 1997, biallelic rare variants in GNRHR were shown to cause normosmic CHH, until 2003 when rare variants in FGFR1 came to show us, by causing both Kallmann syndrome and normosmic CHH, that these are not two separate pathophysiological conditions as we previously thought.

And then the number of candidate genes increased very rapidly, and this is how we were 10 years ago.

And here we were in the transition era between Sanger sequencing and next-generation sequencing.

So this figure shows us the evolution of the techniques of molecular diagnosis in CHH.

So until 2010, we used Sanger sequencing when you had to sequence each exon of each gene for each patient separately in a very time-consuming process, and MLPA and later chromosomal microarrays to look for large deletions.

And then in the mid-2010s, the advent of NGS, it was a milestone in molecular research in CHH.

And then first with candidate gene panels, target gene panels, we were able to sequence a lot of different genes in different patients simultaneously, and later this evolved to whole exome sequencing with target genes and also CNV analysis, and this is pretty much where we are now.

And some emerging techniques are whole-genome sequencing and long-read sequencing, which are already being done, but mostly in research centers.

### CHH-associated genes and diagnostic yield

So, after this, this figure shows us the evolution of the number of candidate CHH genes.

And now we have more than 60 genes that are associated with the pathogenesis of CHH, and they can be classified into two large groups: the genes that are involved in neurodevelopmental pathway regulating the migration and specification of GnRH neurons, and the genes involved in the neuroendocrine pathway regulating production, secretion, and action of GnRH, causing essentially normosmic CHH.

Apart from increasing the number of genes, the first consequence of NGS was the increase in the diagnostic yield.

### Clinical heterogeneity and oligogenic inheritance

We have marked clinical and genetic heterogeneity.

We have sometimes one gene causing apparently different phenotypes, even in the same family, or different phenotypes attributed to one gene, different genes in the same phenotype.

So this, we don't know exactly the explanation for this variability, but some clues we have are incomplete penetrance, the severity of the mutations, if they are complete loss of function or partial loss of function, or oligogenicity.

So, oligogenic inheritance is now known for many years.

We know that about 10 to 20% of patients may carry rare variants in more than one CHH-associated gene, and these variants may act synergistically, contributing to the phenotype.

And these are seen with different genes.

The most commonly affected ones are FGFR1, PROKR2, CHD7, ANOS1, and other genes of the FGF signaling pathway.

And they might contribute to explain the great phenotypic variability and severity seen sometimes within the same family.

### Reversal of CHH

Can genetics be important in this issue?

So we know now, we know that spontaneous recovery from the reproductive function is seen in 10 to 20% of men with CHH, usually after a period of testosterone replacement, and reversal occurs even in patients with confirmed pathogenic variants in traditional CHH in several CHH genes.

We don't know the exact mechanism, but the theory is that testosterone replacement may prompt neuronal network neuroplasticity.

So this study, this was a large study as a consortium from six countries in which we participated with Brazilian patients.

Then we studied 87 patients with CHH reversal compared to 108 patients without reversal and found some predictors.

So clinical predictors of reversal were partial CHH and testicular growth during testosterone replacement.

And negative genetic predictors were the presence of an ANOS1 variant, as ANOS1 is always associated with a very severe reproductive phenotype, and oligogenicity was also a negative predictor.

On the other hand, in this study, GNRHR variants were more associated with reversal, so they were a positive predictor.

And not in this study, but in other studies, TAC3, TACR3, and IGSF10 variants were more associated with reversal.

And here, coming back to the clinical setting, to the clinical practice, what should we do when you have a patient, when you suspect, an adult patient you suspect congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism?

### Take-home messages

So my take-home messages are that CHH remains fundamentally a clinical diagnosis, remembering that the molecular diagnosis is available only in about 50% of cases.

CHH has evolved from a traditional Mendelian monogenic inheritance to an oligogenic model.

Syndromic and isolated forms of CHH exist on a pathophysiological spectrum.

Phenotype-guided whole-exome sequencing is currently the molecular diagnostic tool of choice.

Advances in molecular tools have improved diagnostic yield, but also increased the burden of variants of uncertain significance, so we should be very careful in the interpretation of these results.

And important to remember that genetic findings can prompt us to reverse phenotyping.
