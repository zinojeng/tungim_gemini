### GLP-1 processing and receptor pharmacology

I am going to age myself with this animation, but our understanding begins with the fact that GLP-1 is secreted as an active hormone. Its activity depends on its two N-terminal amino acids, which are cleaved by dipeptidyl peptidase-4, or DPP-4. The GLP-1(9–36) peptide is essentially inactive. Some data suggest that it may be vasoactive in rodents, but the human data are not sufficiently clear to establish a vascular effect. It clearly does not have a metabolic effect.

Circulating GLP-1 has a half-life of probably less than one minute because DPP-4 is ubiquitous. If you were designing a GLP-1-based therapy, you would have to infuse GLP-1 continuously, inhibit the main enzyme responsible for its degradation, or—and this shows how old this slide is—use a GLP-1 analog that is relatively resistant to DPP-4. The first such compound used clinically was Byetta: exenatide, or exendin-4, approved in 2005. These agents have therefore been around for quite a while.

Another useful tool for studying actions mediated by the GLP-1 receptor, or GLP-1R, is a truncated exendin-4 analog, exendin(9–39). It is a competitive antagonist of GLP-1 at its receptor.

### Direct metabolic effects

I have always been interested in the pancreatic and extrapancreatic actions of GLP-1. My first experiment as a fellow in Bob Rizza's laboratory asked whether GLP-1 had direct effects on glucose metabolism. We studied people with diabetes on two occasions, with somatostatin on both occasions. In one group, we held insulin constant; we called this the basal-insulin group. In the other, we infused insulin to mimic its systemic appearance after a meal. Because we used somatostatin and replaced insulin, insulin concentrations were matched on the two occasions. Somatostatin essentially prevented endogenous insulin secretion, as shown by C-peptide, and we also matched glucagon concentrations.

Any difference in glucose concentrations would therefore have represented a direct effect of GLP-1, independent of its stimulation of insulin secretion and suppression of glucagon. The result was an exercise in superimposable curves. Glucose concentrations overlapped, and there was no difference in glucose disappearance or endogenous glucose production.

From my point of view, GLP-1's effects on metabolism are mediated through GLP-1R. GLP-1R is present in the pancreas, certain parts of the brain, and the gastrointestinal tract, but GLP-1 has no otherwise demonstrable direct effect on glucose metabolism.

We repeated a similar dose-response experiment examining the insulin effect on glucose disappearance in the presence of saline, GLP-1, or exendin-4, which became Byetta, or exenatide. At overlapping insulin concentrations there was no real difference among the three study days because we blocked endogenous secretion and replaced insulin and glucagon. Endogenous glucose production was also similar. Again, we found no direct effect of GLP-1 or GLP-1 receptor agonists on glucose metabolism.

### Criteria for additional mechanisms

If we invoke a new mechanism for GLP-1-dependent actions, we should identify clinical effects that are independent of changes in glucose metabolism or weight and, if possible, show whether the effects are independent of GLP-1R. That is difficult in humans because the only GLP-1R antagonist currently available is exendin(9–39). It must be infused continuously, and the FDA limits its use to a six-hour infusion at 750 pmol/kg/min.

The effects should also be attributable to GLP-1-like properties and should not differ from those of the native hormone or one of its fragments. Those conditions are difficult to fulfill experimentally in humans. To date, much of the evidence has depended on animal experiments that may not extrapolate to humans. There are, however, some caveats, which I want to discuss.

As many of you know, proglucagon is processed differently depending on location to produce either glucagon or GLP-1. The conventional view is that the intestinal L cell expresses prohormone convertase 1/3, or PC1/3, and its main proglucagon products are oxyntomodulin, GLP-1, and GLP-2. The pancreatic alpha cell expresses prohormone convertase 2, or PC2, and its major active fragment is glucagon.

This is not always the case, because PC1/3 can be induced. In settings in which proglucagon is expressed and inflammation is present, PC1/3 is also present. Two themes arise from this slide: one concerns oxyntomodulin and the other concerns ectopic GLP-1 expression.

### Oxyntomodulin and dual agonism

The previous speaker discussed a difficult-to-pronounce triple agonist of the GIP, GLP-1, and glucagon receptors. The parent molecule is oxyntomodulin. Professor Sir Stephen Bloom in London showed long ago that oxyntomodulin causes weight loss through both reduced food intake and increased energy expenditure. Oxyntomodulin reduces food intake and produces a small but significant increase in activity-related energy expenditure.

Oxyntomodulin is unique because it is a dual agonist at GLP-1R and the glucagon receptor. If one residue in the peptide is modified so that it cannot bind the glucagon receptor, most of its metabolic effects disappear. This was the first in-vivo demonstration of a dual agonist.

In practice, endogenous circulating oxyntomodulin does not produce effects of this magnitude. These experiments concern pharmacology, not physiology, at the administered doses. Dose-response studies compared native oxyntomodulin with modified OXMQ3E, which has markedly reduced binding to the glucagon receptor, by examining glycogen depletion in a perfused liver. Superficially the curves appear similar, but the modified peptide's affinity for the glucagon receptor is approximately 1,000-fold lower.

In rodents, native oxyntomodulin produces substantial weight loss, whereas the modified peptide that does not bind the glucagon receptor has much less effect on weight. The effect also depends on GLP-1R: GLP-1R-knockout mice show very little weight loss. This work helped spur the development of dual agonists. Another experiment, using liraglutide as a comparator, showed that a dual agonist binding both GLP-1R and the glucagon receptor was more potent.

### GLP1R genetic variation and fasting physiology

When I returned to Mayo after a sabbatical in Cambridge studying the genetics of type 1 diabetes, I decided to apply genetics to my favorite receptor. We used 18 tag single-nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs, together with the database of known nonsynonymous SNPs to test responses to infused GLP-1. Sathananthan and colleagues showed that people carrying the rs3765467 Arg131Gln variant were hyper-responsive to GLP-1. The variant is rare in White populations but common in Asian populations. Anyone who heard the Banting Lecture at the ADA meeting last week would have heard this variant discussed.

The variant is located in the hinge region of GLP-1R. It does not substantially change affinity for the native ligand or cyclic AMP generation. It changes β-arrestin recruitment and increases the time that active GLP-1R remains at the cell membrane and signals. In a sense, this is biased signaling, except that the receptor itself is biased toward a greater response to GLP-1.

This remained an orphan observation until a Korean group reported in 2018 that the same variant lowers fasting glucose. A variant in the receptor for an incretin hormone, theoretically active in the postprandial period, was therefore associated with lower fasting glucose. Three months later, a Japanese population study again showed that this variant was associated with protection from type 2 diabetes; this year's Banting Awardee was the senior author.

The variant appeared to lower fasting glucose. Another interesting observation is that DPP-4 inhibitors, which should be active only when GLP-1 is being produced, also lower fasting glucose. Dr. Rizza repeatedly asked me how this could occur when no circulating GLP-1 is detectable in the fasting state.

My good friend Dr. Salehi, now in San Antonio, also showed—although she did not comment on it—that during intravenous glucose administration, beginning an infusion of the GLP-1R antagonist exendin(9–39) in the fasting state produced changes in both glucose and insulin concentrations. Clearly, GLP-1 is active in the fasting state or during a nonoral glucose challenge. Dr. D'Alessio's group showed a related result with an arginine challenge in the presence or absence of exendin(9–39).

### Intra-islet GLP-1

There were data suggesting GLP-1 staining within the pancreatic islets. As nonobese diabetic, or NOD, mice became more diabetic, PC1/3 expression and GLP-1 staining increased. The GLP-1 assays available when that work was published were somewhat nonspecific, but an Oxford group recently showed a similar pattern in humans. As people progressed from normal glucose tolerance to impaired glucose tolerance and diabetes, GLP-1 staining in the islets increased.

In our own study, we examined people on two occasions with or without exendin(9–39), both in the fasting state and during hyperglycemia. We saw no real change in circulating GLP-1 concentrations. The GLP-1 assay is rather poor, and in the fasting state it measures such small quantities that the signal may be background noise.

What was interesting was that blocking GLP-1R in people without diabetes raised fasting glucose. The effect was more pronounced in people with type 2 diabetes. In people without diabetes, blockade also affected the surrogate measure we used for first-phase insulin secretion. People with type 2 diabetes had no first phase, so we saw no effect there. In people without diabetes, glucagon regulation remained intact. In people with type 2 diabetes, however, GLP-1R blockade during intravenous glucose and in the fasting state caused marked dysregulation of glucagon secretion.

These are important observations to consider when invoking mechanisms for GLP-1 effects outside metabolism.

### Vascular and inflammatory evidence

In apolipoprotein E-knockout mice fed a high-fat diet and genetically predisposed to atheroma, GLP-1 reduced vascular lesions. Blocking GLP-1R with exendin(9–39) inhibited that effect, suggesting that GLP-1 may mitigate atherosclerosis. In a living animal or human, however, chronic therapy makes it difficult to separate direct effects on a disease process from changes in glucose, circulating free fatty acids, caloric intake, and weight.

Some data suggest that liraglutide decreases inflammatory markers. The example I chose involved people with type 2 diabetes. Inflammatory markers declined during GLP-1 therapy, and the changes did not correlate with the degree of weight change or improvement in glycemic control. This may be evidence of a direct effect.

A randomized crossover study used positron emission tomography to examine myocardial perfusion and energetics. Forty-one patients with type 2 diabetes were treated for 16 weeks with liraglutide or pioglitazone and then crossed over after a washout. Liraglutide increased myocardial perfusion and improved myocardial energetics, whereas pioglitazone was neutral. Again, this suggested effects of GLP-1 outside glucose metabolism.

Vascular smooth muscle expresses GLP-1R, and GLP-1 has small direct vasorelaxant effects that are difficult to measure in humans but easier to demonstrate in rodents. In one example, mice were made hypertensive with angiotensin II. Co-administration of liraglutide mitigated the rise in blood pressure. These were nondiabetic mice, so this was additional evidence of benefit independent of changes in glucose and insulin.

Angiotensin II also impaired vasorelaxation. Liraglutide reversed that effect and restored acetylcholine-mediated vasorelaxation. When GLP-1R was deleted from vascular smooth muscle, the effects of GLP-1 on blood pressure and natriuresis disappeared. These findings suggest that some cardiovascular benefits are mediated through GLP-1Rs on vascular smooth muscle.

### Conclusions

I will conclude by saying that GLP-1 and GLP-1 receptor agonists differ in their interaction with and effect on GLP-1R. We do not understand why exenatide has a higher affinity for GLP-1R than native GLP-1. We do not understand why the small molecule orforglipron binds a small portion of GLP-1R yet reproduces most effects of a conventional peptide GLP-1 receptor agonist.

It is important to distinguish physiology from pharmacology. Most of what we deal with today is pharmacology, not physiology. It is also important to recognize the large gaps that remain in our understanding of GLP-1 physiology. There is currently no clear unifying pathway or mechanism explaining direct effects on the vasculature and inflammatory pathways beyond GLP-1R expression in vascular smooth muscle.

I will end by thanking my laboratory, my collaborators in interventional radiology, my study volunteers, and my collaborators in Italy. Thank you very much for staying. I will be happy to answer questions.
