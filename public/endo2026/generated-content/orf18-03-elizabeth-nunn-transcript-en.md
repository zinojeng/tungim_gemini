GLP-1 receptor agonists and multi-agonists have proven to be extremely effective therapeutics for obesity, promoting clinically significant weight loss and improvements in metabolic health. Recent clinical data has shown that some amount of the weight loss with these drugs can be attributed to a loss of fat-free mass, which is comprised largely of muscle mass, and this has sparked interest in pathways which regulate muscle mass and alter body composition, or the amount of muscle versus fat mass that one has. We are interested in the activin type 2A and 2B receptors, which are expressed in many tissues and bind TGF-beta family ligands such as myostatin and activin A, and these dimerize with activin type 1 receptors to propagate signaling. In skeletal muscle, this pathway is well known to negatively regulate muscle mass. Deletion of these receptors in muscle has been shown to both increase muscle size as well as have a secondary effect on suppressing gain of fat mass. Work from our group and others has shown that systemic antibody blockade of this pathway can effectively protect muscle mass during weight loss while further increasing loss of fat mass in mice. However, these receptors are also expressed in mature adipocytes and are hypothesized to play a direct role in regulating fat mobilization. However, published mouse models have incompletely targeted this pathway, leading to conflicting results. Recently, a liver-specific gene called Inhbe, which encodes for the TGF-beta family member activin E, has been found to play a role in regulating fat distribution. Loss of function mutations in this gene have been associated with improved waist-to-hip ratio in humans. This suggests that there is important liver-to-adipose tissue communication during obesity. However, the role of the activin type 2 receptors themselves in hepatocytes is unknown. Based on this, we created three tissue-specific activin type 2A and 2B receptor knockout mouse models to assess the cell-autonomous roles of these receptors during obesity. Muscle-specific deletion of the type II receptors resulted in increased muscle mass and protection from gain of fat mass when mice were placed on a high-fat diet. This change in body composition in the knockouts was associated with improved insulin sensitivity during a glucose tolerance test. These muscle knockout animals had significantly larger muscles, primarily due to myofiber hypertrophy, but interestingly, this increase in overall muscle mass, or lean mass, was not associated with an increase in systemic energy expenditure that could potentially explain the protection from gain of fat mass. Finally, we did not observe any upregulation of genes related to lipolysis in the adipose tissue of these muscle knockout animals, so further work is needed to explore the mechanism protecting them from gain of fat mass. Deletion of the type II receptors in mature adipocytes surprisingly did not result in changes to muscle or fat mass after 12 weeks on a high-fat diet. Similarly, there were no striking effects on glucose tolerance, and the fat knockout animals did display significantly increased lipolysis-related gene expression in visceral white adipose tissue, but this was not associated with an overall change in body composition. So this suggests that the activin type II receptors in the liver play a role in the regulation of activin ligands, and loss of these receptors in the liver then alters adipose tissue behavior through reduced Inhbe expression and activin E.
