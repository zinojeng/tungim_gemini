## Standardized diagnosis and ICCR core elements

It is very important to standardize the diagnosis and classification of adrenocortical carcinoma, or ACC, particularly the distinction between benign and malignant disease. Organizations including the European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumors, or ENSAT, the World Health Organization, or WHO, and others are working toward classification and standardization. The International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting, or ICCR, provides a basis for the next WHO classification and clinical guidelines. I am an ICCR member and will give you a preview.

The ICCR emphasizes *core elements*: findings that must be provided in the pathology report for patient management. I will cover the elements we have established.

First, clinicians must provide the pathologist with adequate clinical information. Information that is sometimes omitted includes a history of other malignancies, which is necessary to assess the possibility of adrenal metastasis, and CT or MRI findings, which guide gross examination of the specimen. Clinical findings are also important, particularly combined steroid excess. Co-secretion is critical for assessing malignancy, even from a pathologist’s standpoint. The operative procedure must also be provided because resection-margin status is an important prognostic factor.

The next question is whether a tumor is of adrenocortical origin. First, check the patient’s history of other malignancies. Melanoma, lung adenocarcinoma, and other tumors may metastasize to the adrenal because of its abundant blood supply.

Steroidogenic factor 1, or SF-1, is the most useful immunohistochemical marker of adrenocortical origin. Melan-A and synaptophysin are less specific: Melan-A is also a melanocytic marker, and synaptophysin is expressed in neuroendocrine tumors. Inhibin-α and calretinin are less sensitive and specific than SF-1. If there is doubt about adrenocortical origin, ask the pathologist to perform SF-1 immunohistochemistry. SF-1 is highly specific, although fixation and other preanalytical conditions may affect staining.

For example, a 66-year-old woman had a large retroperitoneal mass. A needle biopsy was initially diagnosed as renal cell carcinoma, and extensive molecular profiling was performed. SF-1 showed clear nuclear immunoreactivity and established adrenocortical origin.

Specimen integrity is another core element. A specimen should be described as intact, disrupted, fragmented, or artifactually damaged. Laparoscopic surgery may disrupt the tumor capsule and lead to peritoneal dissemination, so specimens obtained laparoscopically require particular attention. If ACC is suspected preoperatively, many surgeons use an open approach.

Tumor dimensions and weight are core elements. Maximum tumor diameter is important for TNM staging and should be measured in the gross specimen. Histologic processing causes shrinkage, while radiologic measurements depend on image-section thickness. The specimen should be weighed after grossly benign adipose tissue has been removed.

## Histologic classification and the Weiss system

The WHO 2025 classification recognizes ACC not otherwise specified, oncocytic ACC, myxoid ACC, and sarcomatoid ACC. A pragmatic term has also been introduced: *adrenocortical neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential*. This is not a distinct WHO entity and is not an ideal diagnosis. However, many difficult adrenocortical tumors are sent for external consultation, which may take months. During that interval, this terminology can be used for a tumor that does not meet criteria for an outright diagnosis of ACC but has unusual or atypical features for an adenoma.

Despite the molecular advances of the past 40 years, the Weiss criteria remain the gold standard for distinguishing benign from malignant resected adrenocortical tumors. Other approaches include the reticulin algorithm and Helsinki score, and the Lin–Weiss–Bisceglia criteria are used for oncocytic adrenocortical tumors. The Weiss system tends to overdiagnose malignancy in pediatric adrenocortical tumors, and reliable criteria for pediatric disease remain lacking.

Some Weiss parameters are subjective. Capsular invasion in ACC generally implies full-thickness penetration, although the definition is not fully standardized. Lymphatic, sinusoidal, and vascular invasion can also be difficult to assess. A tumor nest attached to the vessel wall, with or without endothelial coverage and admixed thrombus, may support vascular invasion. If a report states that invasion is present, ask whether D2-40 was used for lymphatics and whether CD31 or ERG was used for blood vessels. Without supporting immunohistochemistry, assessment can remain subjective, even with deep-learning image analysis.

The Ki-67 proliferative or labeling index is important in ACC. ENSAT guidance uses 10% as a clinically important threshold; adjuvant mitotane is generally considered when Ki-67 exceeds 10%. Two practical issues are how Ki-67 is evaluated—as a hotspot or an average—and which antibody is used. Most outcome data are based on the MIB-1 antibody, whereas some laboratories use the SP6 clone. Before making a postoperative mitotane decision, check whether the reported value represents hotspot or average labeling, whether it was evaluated visually or by image analysis, and which antibody was used. Ki-67 should be included in every adrenocortical-neoplasm pathology report.

Resection-margin status, pathologic stage, and lymph-node status are also core elements. Margins should be categorized as uninvolved, microscopically involved (R1), macroscopically involved (R2), or indeterminate. The number of lymph nodes examined and involved should be reported, together with extranodal extension when present.

The most important prognostic factor in 2026 remains ENSAT TNM stage, based on the system reported by Fassnacht and colleagues in 2009. It continues to perform in European, Japanese, and American cohorts. Accurate staging is therefore essential, particularly accurate evaluation of resection margins.

## Molecular characteristics of ACC

When young pathologists encounter a difficult tumor today, they may move rapidly from light microscopy to DNA, RNA, and methylation profiling. Extensive molecular data have been generated for many tumor types. A recent review in *Pathology—Research and Practice* summarizes the molecular characteristics of ACC.

Important genetic events include TP53 alterations, sometimes with AURKA, KIF23, or POLD1 abnormalities; alterations of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, including CTNNB1 mutation and ZNRF3 deletion; and IGF2 overexpression. Numerous epigenetic alterations have also been reported. In contrast to lung, colorectal, and other common cancers, ACC lacks a single dominant driver alteration and usually lacks a readily druggable mutation such as BRAF or RAS.

Excellent work from Tom Giordano, Gary Hammer, and colleagues at the University of Michigan has shown the importance of methylation and other epigenetic abnormalities. Global DNA methylation and the CpG island methylator phenotype, or CIMP, can define prognostic molecular subgroups. However, these findings have not yet translated into routine selection of postoperative therapy.

Molecular analysis of ACC has several limitations. First, there is no definitive precursor lesion comparable to those in thyroid, prostate, or breast cancer, apart from tumors arising in Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome. There is no established sequence of progression from adenoma to carcinoma. Second, robust in vivo and in vitro models remain limited, although organoid studies are promising. Third, ACC shows marked intratumoral heterogeneity.

For example, a large tumor from a 24-year-old woman with Cushing syndrome contained a yellow area that appeared histologically adenomatous and a hemorrhagic red area that met Weiss criteria for carcinoma. ACC therefore shows striking heterogeneity in histology, molecular characteristics, and steroid profiles. Gross examination is critical because every later result depends on which part of the tumor is sampled.

## Liquid biopsy and minimal residual disease

Liquid biopsy is attractive because it is less invasive than tissue biopsy and might provide real-time information. Circulating tumor DNA, or ctDNA, containing somatic alterations such as TP53 or CTNNB1 can theoretically identify recurrence before it is visible on CT. MicroRNAs such as miR-483-5p and miR-210 have also been studied, but their specificity is limited. At present, neither ctDNA nor circulating microRNA reliably identifies minimal residual disease in ACC or determines when adjuvant mitotane can be stopped.

Signatera is a personalized, tumor-informed ctDNA assay that is commercially available, approved for clinical use in several settings, and reimbursed by some health plans. It retrieves a patient’s prior tumor block, establishes that patient’s genomic profile, and then searches the blood for the selected tumor-specific variants. It may prove useful for minimal residual disease and decisions about stopping adjuvant therapy. However, no ACC study has yet demonstrated a relationship between Signatera results and long-term prognosis or recurrence. It is promising, but the question remains open.

## Tumor microenvironment and immunotherapy

The last part of my talk concerns the tumor microenvironment, or TME. Carcinoma cells cannot survive alone; they interact with fibroblasts, blood vessels, lymphatics, inflammatory cells, cytokines, and chemokines.

The adrenal gland is generally immunologically cold because corticosteroids suppress T cells and increase regulatory T-cell activity. Paradoxically, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are more abundant in cortisol-producing adenomas than in aldosterone-producing adenomas. We used deep-learning analysis to document this phenomenon. Excess cortisol induces cellular senescence and DNA damage. Senescent cells produce a senescence-associated secretory phenotype, or SASP, including chemokine signals that attract lymphocytes. The CXCL12–CXCR4 pathway may bring T cells into the lesion to remove senescent cortisol-producing cells. This is interesting biologically, although it has not yet translated into routine prognosis or treatment.

ACC is also immunologically cold and usually contains few tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, particularly when the tumor produces glucocorticoids. The situation is complicated by marked intratumoral heterogeneity and disorganized steroidogenesis. Different tumor areas produce different amounts of cortisol and steroid precursors and therefore create different local immune environments.

The clinical question is whether patients benefit from immune-checkpoint inhibitors, particularly pembrolizumab or avelumab. Across studies, objective response rates are only about 14%–15%. PD-L1 expression is a useful biomarker in some cancers, but it is not a reliable routine predictor in ACC. Tumor mutational burden can be obtained through platforms such as FoundationOne, but small ACC series have not shown it to be a reliable marker either. Several ACC trials of immune-checkpoint inhibitors have been terminated or have had limited accrual.

Mismatch-repair deficiency is a remaining source of hope. Under strict Amsterdam criteria, ACC occurs in approximately 2.6% of people with Lynch syndrome. More importantly, somatic microsatellite-instability or mismatch-repair abnormalities may be detected in sporadic ACC.

Mismatch-repair proteins can be assessed in routine practice by immunohistochemistry for MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2. This is currently the most practical biomarker for selecting patients with ACC for immune-checkpoint inhibition, although not every mismatch-repair-deficient tumor responds. We are incorporating mismatch-repair immunohistochemistry into routine evaluation, particularly for large ACCs or tumors with Ki-67 above 10% that are more likely to recur or metastasize.

## Summary

Clinical information—including endocrine findings, combined secretion of cortisol and sex steroids, and imaging data—must be provided to the pathologist. Pathology reports should follow the ICCR core elements. ENSAT TNM stage remains the gold standard for predicting clinical outcome, and every effort should be made to obtain an accurate stage.

Molecular profiles and the ACC tumor microenvironment remain less well established than they are in common malignancies, largely because ACC is rare. Multicenter collaboration through groups such as A5, ENSAT, and others is essential to standardize clinical guidance and refine management.

Thank you very much.

**Audience member:** Thank you for this interesting talk. Can you discuss IGF2 overexpression or IGF2 immunohistochemistry in the diagnostic spectrum from adenoma and uncertain malignant potential to carcinoma?

**Hironobu Sasano:** IGF2 is difficult because it is a small, secreted peptide. It is difficult to quantify by immunohistochemistry because, once produced, it leaves the cell. We have also examined messenger RNA. IGF2 is often abundant in large tumors, but the diagnostically important problem is the subtle distinction between an adenoma and a carcinoma. In our experience, IGF2 is not sufficiently useful for that distinction. If IGF-targeted inhibitors are introduced in the future, it could perhaps become a useful surrogate marker.

**Audience member:** I have another question. I have encountered discordance between Ki-67 and the mitotic rate. This changes my management; for example, the mitotic rate may exceed 20 while Ki-67 is only 10%. How should a clinician interpret that?

**Hironobu Sasano:** In the United States, pathologists often regard the mitotic count as the gold standard. In other parts of the world, Ki-67 is often favored because mitotic counts are difficult to quantify and are influenced by fixation and other conditions. Check whether Ki-67 was measured with MIB-1, where the tissue was sampled, whether hotspots were used, and whether it was assessed visually or by image analysis. Those details may provide more reliable information. I may be criticized by the pathology community in the United States for saying this, but I trust Ki-67.
