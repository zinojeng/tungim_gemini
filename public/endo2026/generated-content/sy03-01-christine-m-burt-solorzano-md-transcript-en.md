### Cardiometabolic risk in adult women with PMOS

Let's talk a bit about the data for metabolic risk associated with PMOS in adult women.

The Lancet Commission in 2021 on women and cardiovascular disease highlighted that cardiovascular disease in women is prevalent, understudied, underdiagnosed, and undertreated worldwide.

The report recognized that PMOS is a separate sex-specific risk factor for cardiovascular disease.

We recognize that PMOS increases the risks of well-established risk factors like hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and obesity.

A meta-analysis from the Netherlands examined 23 studies in about 17,000 women with PMOS from seven countries, looking at cardiometabolic implications of PMOS.

They found that women with PMOS faced substantially elevated lifelong risks of cardiovascular and metabolic disease that accumulate with age.

They found that women with PMOS have a 75% higher risk of hypertension and a three times higher risk of type 2 diabetes compared to the general population.

They also saw a 41% increased risk of stroke, which means that a personal history of PMOS leads to a similar risk for stroke as having a family history of coronary vascular disease.

A 15-year survey of 1 million people in the UK looked at 2,300 women with PMOS.

They found progressive increase in the percentage of women with PMOS who have metabolic disorders through the decades.

There were three and a half times more women who have type 2 diabetes at age 55 to 64 than the youngest PMOS group, and 10 times more by age 65.

Similar increases in myocardial infarction and angina occurred in PMOS women as they became older, with the risk of myocardial infarction at age 65 being 13-fold higher than the general female population.

A similar study in Australia looked at 700 women with PMOS compared to 7,700 non-PMOS women, all of whom did not have diabetes at the beginning of a 15-year survey.

They found that the incidence of type 2 diabetes is higher in PMOS women, regardless of their weight status.

However, lean women with PMOS actually have the highest incidence rate ratio compared to other lean women, which was 4.7 times higher.

### Cardiometabolic risk in adolescents with PMOS

There are also data supporting metabolic risks associated with PMOS in adolescent girls.

The SEARCH study examined the incidence of type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus in the general population of U.S. youth age 0 to 19.

In a population that was 32% obese, they found that the average incidence of type 2 diabetes was 0.13 per 1,000 person-years.

However, a retrospective chart review by Melanie Cree of nearly 500 girls specifically with PMOS and overweight or obesity demonstrated an incidence of type 2 diabetes of 23 per 1,000 person-years.

This is about a threefold greater incidence than in adult women with PMOS and overweight or obesity, and at least an 18-fold higher incidence than in the general population of overweight or obese adolescent girls.

They also demonstrated that dysglycemia is common and occurs early in adolescent PMOS.

The rate is almost double that of NHANES data of general population U.S. youth.

Other investigators in China and Canada have found similar rates of dysglycemia in PMOS girls.

Compared with girls with a normal hemoglobin A1c, girls who had prediabetes at PMOS diagnosis had a 15-fold increased risk of type 2 diabetes within four years.

Additional risk factors include Black race and Hispanic ethnicity, especially if combined with severe obesity and evidence of steatotic liver disease.

The Clinical Adolescent Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (CALICO) project is a multicenter database of electronic medical record data from U.S. adolescent girls with PMOS.

This study, led by Laura Torchen, looked at phenotype differences in girls with PMOS between those who had obesity versus those who were lean.

Girls were excluded who were taking metformin, antihypertension or anti-lipid medications, or any hormones.

Girls with obesity had a younger age at PMOS diagnosis, earlier menarche, and a higher free testosterone levels, mostly due to lower sex hormone-binding globulin levels.

Lean girls had higher total testosterone and androstenedione levels, as well as greater evidence of LH excess.

Not surprisingly, perhaps, the girls with PMOS and obesity had higher hemoglobin A1c, ALT, and triglycerides, lower HDL levels, and more severe acanthosis nigricans, signaling a metabolic phenotype of PMOS.

Whereas, given the higher LH and total testosterone levels, the lean group appeared to have a more reproductive phenotype.

A group in Turkey looked at girls in their clinic.

Although the study did not specifically match for weight, there were no differences in BMI, body composition, or waist circumference between the groups.

Yet, they found that adolescent girls with PMOS had higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to other girls.

Metabolic burdens begin early in girls with PMOS.

Adolescents with PMOS and obesity have a threefold higher risk of type 2 diabetes than adults with PMOS and obesity.

Prediabetes and steatotic liver disease are risk factors for greater risk of type 2 diabetes within four years.

Therefore, there are stronger recommendations for diabetes screening in adolescents with PMOS compared to adult guidelines.

Adolescence represents a critical window for early risk stratification and prevention interventions, with potential implications for long-term metabolic and cardiovascular health trajectories.

### Lifestyle-intervention outcomes

Lifestyle interventions are the foundation of therapy for adolescents with PMOS, regardless of BMI, to improve metabolic health.

Lifestyle interventions improve androgen excess in girls.

Our group looked at free testosterone levels in the general population of U.S. girls using the NHANES dataset.

Looking at data from nearly 1,300 girls aged 6 to 18, we found that girls with obesity have higher free testosterone levels, whether split into groups of age, of likely puberty status, or when looking at them by year of age.

Using the NHANES dataset, we calculated metabolic syndrome severity scores for girls with available metabolic data.

We found that free testosterone was a significant predictor of metabolic syndrome severity.

We found that elevated free testosterone also predicted individual components of metabolic syndrome: higher systolic blood pressure, triglycerides, and HOMA-IR, as well as lower HDL.

Therefore, androgen excess predicts metabolic syndrome severity in girls in the U.S. population.

Higher free testosterone predicts greater metabolic syndrome severity independent of fat, age, or gynecological age.

Reinehr et al. in Germany also demonstrated androgen excess in girls with obesity.

They studied 169 girls aged 7 to 12 before and after a comprehensive lifestyle intervention with behavioral counseling.

After the year-long program, prepubertal girls had reduced testosterone levels with any amount of weight loss, both minimal and substantial.

Yet postpubertal girls still reduced testosterone levels only after substantial weight loss, and postpubertal girls without weight loss had increased testosterone levels despite the lifestyle intervention.

This potentially suggests that metabolic-associated hyperandrogenemia may become more difficult to correct with age and/or exposure time.

And, compared to other girls matched for obesity, girls with PMOS have even worse metabolic parameters, elevated LH, and greater androgen excess.

However, girls with PMOS who had weight loss were most likely to reduce their testosterone levels, compared to other girls with obesity who also lost weight, and compared to PMOS peers without weight loss.

Girls with PMOS who had successful weight loss were able to improve their insulin sensitivity, glucose tolerance, and cardiovascular risk factors.

Girls with PMOS and obesity who had weight loss saw greater reductions in total testosterone and increases in SHBG.

Successful weight loss also ameliorated LH excess and improved menstrual regularity.

Improved insulin sensitivity correlated with reduced testosterone levels and increased SHBG.

Carotid intima-media thickness was positively and significantly associated with BMI, diastolic blood pressure, and testosterone levels, suggesting translation of these risk factors into noted anatomical changes.

A meta-analysis of lifestyle interventions in 16 intervention groups and 3 control groups found improvements in clinical and biochemical hyperandrogenism, decreased LH excess, and improvements to regularity in girls undergoing various lifestyle interventions.

In summary, weight loss in girls with obesity is associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity and metabolic features, as well as lower androgen levels.

However, hyperandrogenemia may become more difficult to correct with age or exposure time, despite similar amounts of weight loss.

Metabolic abnormalities are more tightly related to total testosterone levels and other features of androgen excess in girls with PMOS versus other girls with obesity.

Girls with PMOS who are able to achieve weight loss with lifestyle intervention see decreases in androgen excess, lower LH levels, normalization of menstrual cycles, and reduced hirsutism.

Likewise, testosterone levels are more sensitive to weight loss and improved insulin resistance in PMOS girls.

### Combined oral contraceptive outcomes

Combined oral contraceptive pills are currently first-line therapy for irregular menses and hyperandrogenism in adolescent PMOS.

Lower ethinyl estradiol dose preparations are preferred.

In a small RCT with combined oral contraceptive pills versus metformin for six months in adolescents with PMOS, OCPs did a better job of regulating uterine bleeding, decreasing acne, and raising SHBG.

In this study, there were no differences between the groups in weight reduction, decreases in hirsutism or biochemical androgen excess, or in metabolic parameters, suggesting no inferiority of OCPs in metabolic outcomes.

Ibáñez and de Zegher found that lowering the androgenic activity of OCP progestins decreased abdominal fat deposition when switching to the anti-androgenic progestin drospirenone.

OCPs in adolescents with PMOS have reproductive and androgen excess symptom benefits.

A meta-analysis demonstrated OCPs resulted in modest improvements in acne scores.

OCPs were superior to metformin for treating hirsutism.

Compared to metformin, OCPs have benefits in improving regularity of uterine bleeding, although metformin also increases rate of menses.

OCPs raise sex hormone-binding globulin levels.

As far as metabolic effects, in small trials, OCPs are not superior to metformin in improvement in BMI, total or free testosterone, insulin sensitivity, or lipids.

Some OCP formulations have been associated with unfavorable effects on insulin sensitivity indices and lipid profiles, although a Cochrane review concluded that OCP use does not increase metabolic risk based on limited evidence.

Using less androgenic or anti-androgenic progestins may lead to decreased abdominal fat, even in non-obese PMOS adolescents.

### Metformin outcomes

Metformin is considered to be first line for metabolic comorbidities and could be considered in adolescents with PMOS for cycle regulation.

A meta-analysis of metformin versus OCPs found that metformin improved BMI, dysglycemia, and lipid levels better than OCP, but OCP was favored to reduce acne scores.

There was no inferiority of metformin in hirsutism scores.

In the CALICO database, now using 17 sites and nearly 1,200 girls, we examined outcomes of girls with PMOS who were prescribed estrogen-containing medications—a pill, patch, or ring—versus those prescribed metformin.

We found that girls prescribed metformin tended to have higher weight and be more likely to have obesity, suggesting evidence for personalized approach to therapy.

We used linear mixed effects models to detect differences in weight or BMI change trajectories over time between ever versus never users of either estrogen-containing medication or metformin.

All models were adjusted for age, race, and insurance status.

EC models were adjusted for metformin use and vice versa.

In these models, concurrent use of the opposite medication did not significantly alter the slope of effects.

We found opposite effects on weight between estrogen use and metformin, with girls taking estrogen-containing medications gaining weight over time, whereas those taking metformin lost weight.

This was true even when we looked at the models separately for girls with and without obesity.

We found that obesity status and medication start did not affect weight trajectory in girls with PMOS.

Those with and without obesity gained weight if using ECs, but lost weight with metformin.

A systematic review of nonhormonal pharmacological interventions by Rhea Sibal, Jan Idkowiak, and colleagues from this year showed that metformin monotherapy results in greater weight loss, decreased abdominal adiposity, restoration of regular menses and ovulatory cycles, and decreased clinical and biochemical hyperandrogenism.

Ovulation events increased from 0 to 39%.

When metformin is added to OCP, an Italian study of girls with PMOS and hyperinsulinemia found that BMI decreased by 11%, insulin resistance, ovarian volume, and total testosterone decreased, and SHBG increased.

These benefits persisted for 24 months after metformin was stopped.

Metformin is recommended to be used first line for metabolic comorbidities.

As such, girls prescribed metformin tend to be higher weight and more likely to have obesity.

Regardless of obesity status at medication start, metformin use is associated with weight loss, whereas estrogen users may have less weight loss or even weight gain.

Meta-analyses show that metformin decreases lipids, central obesity, and insulin resistance, restores menses and ovulation, and improves clinical and biochemical androgen excess, although OCPs reduce acne and free testosterone more.

The addition of metformin to OCPs improves weight and insulin sensitivity, decreases ovarian volume and total testosterone, and increases SHBG, with persistent benefits for two years after metformin is stopped.

Using both combined OCPs and metformin may be considered for adolescents with BMI greater than 30 kilograms per meter squared, diabetes risk factors, or impaired glucose levels.

### Combination therapy and antiandrogens

Antiandrogens may have added benefits for hirsutism or female pattern hair loss.

In a study by Ibáñez et al., Catalan girls with PMOS were given either a combined OCP or a combination of spironolactone, pioglitazone, and metformin called SPIOMET for 12 months, with 12 months of follow-up after medication discontinuation.

In the SPIOMET group, regular menses returned in 94%, hirsutism scores decreased by 40%, and acne scores, abdominal obesity, and testosterone levels decreased.

Notably, after medication discontinuation, ovulation events were two and a half times higher in the SPIOMET group than in the former combined-OCP group, and there were six times as many normo-ovulatory girls in the SPIOMET group.

Here you can see that SPIOMET decreased hepatic fat during and after use and led to persistently decreased androgen levels.

OCP users had rebound of high androgen levels after medication discontinuation.

By the same group, the use of flutamide instead of OCP in teens or in addition to OCP in young women was studied.

The use of flutamide plus metformin led to gains in lean mass and reduced body and abdominal fat mass.

These metabolic improvements were additive to those of the fourth-generation antiandrogenic progestin drospirenone.

In lean girls with PMOS and hyperinsulinism, the same group also studied whether flutamide or metformin led to further metabolic gains when added to combinations of the others, including a combined OCP.

Yes, both flutamide and metformin provided additional benefits to lean body mass and reduction in body fat and abdominal fat mass when added to the other medications.

### GLP-1 receptor agonist outcomes

GLP-1 receptor agonists are a biologically plausible therapeutic approach for PMOS.

A meta-analysis of adult women with PMOS taking GLP-1 receptor agonists, not surprisingly, showed that they are effective for weight loss and reduction in abdominal adiposity in this group.

They also decreased triglycerides and total testosterone levels, demonstrating reproductive as well as metabolic improvements.

Melanie Cree's group studied oral semaglutide in adolescents for four months compared with a dietary intervention.

Oral semaglutide had a beneficial effect on weight in PMOS girls, with over half of girls losing 5% or more body weight in 16 weeks.

Total and free androgen index decreased similarly between groups, despite greater weight loss.

In the oral semaglutide study, both short-term semaglutide and lifestyle therapy improved reproductive markers.

This affirms the importance of dietary change and weight loss to reduce androgen excess in adolescent PMOS.

Melanie Cree and Nanette Santoro are partnering to study semaglutide in youth and women with PMOS over 10 months.

They are looking at ovulation events, ovarian volume, metabolic features, and reproductive hormone levels.

They have just reported the data from the first eight participants out of 11 total studied to lose at least 10% of body weight.

The preliminary data from this study, not separated by age, found a 16% weight loss, which was 42 pounds, and a 52% reduction in free testosterone after 40 weeks of semaglutide.

Five of eight normalized their free testosterone levels.

They also had more menses and decreased ovarian volume, suggesting reproductive clinical improvements.

GLP-1 receptor agonists represent a biologically plausible therapeutic approach for PMOS by targeting adipose dysfunction, hyperinsulinemia, and chronic inflammation that drive pathophysiology.

However, adolescent-specific data are emerging.

### Metabolic bariatric surgery outcomes

Metabolic bariatric surgery, MBS, could be considered for weight loss, hypertension, diabetes, hirsutism, irregular menses, and ovulation defects in women.

PMOS diagnosis as a metabolic condition should lower the threshold for indication of MBS.

In a study of the effects of metabolic bariatric surgery by sleeve gastrectomy, adult women with PMOS had two and a half times more spontaneous ovulations than women receiving lifestyle and medical therapy with metformin, orlistat, or both.

Compared to the medical group, women in the surgical group had 80% higher SHBG, 30% lower total testosterone, and 60% lower free androgen index.

Metabolic improvements, including weight loss from MBS, translate into reproductive benefits for women with PMOS.

In a study of adolescents with PMOS undergoing one-anastomosis gastric bypass, girls had remissions of PMOS along with lower hemoglobin A1c and remissions in diabetes and hypertension, sustained after four years of follow-up.

MBS has been shown to improve weight loss, hypertension, diabetes, hirsutism, irregular menses, and ovulation defects in women.

Emerging data of benefits are also there for adolescent girls.

PMOS diagnosis as a metabolic condition should lower the threshold for indication of MBS.

### Clinical implications

How should these data translate to clinical care?

Adolescence represents a critical window for early risk stratification and preventive intervention in PMOS, with potential implications for long-term reproductive and metabolic health.

Treatment for PMOS should be individualized according to clinical presentation, metabolic profile, contraindications, and patient preferences, with shared decision-making prioritized.

Comprehensive metabolic and hormonal evaluation beyond anthropometric measures is essential for early detection and management of cardiometabolic risks.

Here are recent recommendations for adolescent-specific screening guidelines for cardiometabolic features of PMOS, including oral glucose tolerance testing or hemoglobin A1c, blood pressure checks, fasting lipids, and ALT plus FibroScan.

Metabolic and reproductive features of adolescent PMOS are intertwined and have potential lifelong impacts.

Creating personal treatment strategies, such as this one published this year, for girls with PMOS based on their predominant features should be devised to curb early metabolic complications of PMOS.

Use of combined treatment strategies may be warranted to best prevent abdominal and liver fat, as well as lower testosterone and improve ovulation.

Tailoring care to patients' needs also provides greater satisfaction with care for a syndrome with multiple manifestations.
