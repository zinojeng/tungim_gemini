## Session introduction

Good afternoon, everyone. If you could kindly take your seats, we are going to start this session. It is 4:30. It is a real pleasure to introduce this joint guideline on the diagnosis and therapy of AVP deficiency.

My name is John Newell-Price from the University of Sheffield, and I am here with the chairs and members of the guideline committee. To give you some background, this is a joint guideline between the Endocrine Society and the European Society of Endocrinology. This arrangement was put in place in 2020. There have been two other joint guidelines: one on glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency and another on preexisting diabetes and pregnancy. These were presented at both the European meeting and the Endocrine Society meetings.

These are evidence-based guidelines, and they seek to ensure that we can give you the best possible guidance on how to manage conditions that are relevant globally. It is interesting that arginine vasopressin deficiency, as a name, was revised within the past three years. Two people on this panel, Joseph Verbalis and Mirjam Christ-Crain, chaired that renaming effort. It was done for patient safety, to ensure that patients receive appropriate care.

## GRADE methodology

Thank you, John, for the kind introduction. Colleagues and members of the Endocrine Society, I am very happy to give you a brief update on the ongoing joint guideline. I will introduce the methodology and update you on how we performed the systematic reviews and extracted the evidence.

We adopted the GRADE approach, a transparent and structured framework for assessing the quality of research and ultimately making recommendations. The GRADE approach is organized around four main steps. The first is to define an explicit clinical question. The second is to perform a systematic review. The evidence identified through the systematic review is then assessed for certainty in four categories—high, moderate, low, or very low—and, finally, a recommendation is made with one of two strengths.

I want to focus a little more on the strength of recommendations. There are two options. A strong recommendation is phrased in the guideline as **“we recommend.”** Reasonably informed people—including clinicians, policymakers, and patients—would want management to accord with that recommendation. A weak, or conditional, recommendation is phrased as **“we suggest.”** Most people would still act in accordance with the guideline, but a substantial number would not.

It is important to mention that recommendations and suggestions can be bidirectional. For example, we can strongly recommend against a procedure, but we can also strongly recommend for a specific procedure.

A common misconception is that high-quality evidence translates directly into a strong recommendation. This is not always true because the evidence is weighed against multiple factors, which I will explain in a moment. Sometimes low-quality evidence can support a strong recommendation, whereas high-quality evidence may support a weaker recommendation if, for example, we have serious doubts about implementation.

The evidence is therefore weighed together with values, patient preferences, clinical experience, costs, and resources to produce the final recommendation.

## Evidence and expert guidance in AVP deficiency

For AVP deficiency, most of the available evidence concerns diagnostic testing, while evidence on optimal treatment is weak. However, high-quality evidence will not be available in every situation. We still need expert opinion and careful synthesis of lower-quality evidence so that we can guide clinicians appropriately. This is also explained in the rationale accompanying a recommendation.

For AVP deficiency, we chose to develop a guideline rather than a consensus statement. Guidelines are generally based on solid evidence, whereas consensus statements are preferred when evidence is absent. However, there is no logical process that automatically transforms evidence into a recommendation. Every recommendation requires consensus among experts. In the absence of strong evidence, we cannot simply abstain from guidance; we still need a solid expert opinion and consensus regarding the recommendation.

## Clinical question 1a: who should be evaluated?

For this guideline, we focused on four main clinical questions. The first was: **Who should be evaluated?** What symptoms suggest AVP deficiency, and what patient characteristics are relevant?

No studies evaluating the diagnostic value of different clinical symptoms were identified for this question.

## Clinical question 1b: baseline testing

The second question was: **What baseline testing can be performed to confirm or exclude AVP deficiency?**

We included 10 studies. Most investigated baseline sodium, plasma or urine osmolality, and copeptin levels. Although we identified useful evidence, most studies had not been externally validated; therefore, the overall certainty of evidence was moderate.

## Clinical question 1c: differentiating AVP-D, AVP-R, and primary polydipsia

The third question was: **How can we differentiate AVP deficiency, AVP resistance, and primary polydipsia?**

This question had the largest evidence base. Twenty-four studies involving 1,691 patients were included. Some studies used urine osmolality after a water-deprivation test, while most investigated AVP or copeptin. Copeptin was assessed with several forms of stimulation, including hypertonic saline, glucagon, water deprivation, and urea. Again, most studies had not been externally validated, so the overall certainty of evidence was moderate.

## Clinical question 2: optimal treatment and follow-up

The final question concerned **optimal treatment and follow-up**, including therapy and monitoring for adults with AVP deficiency.

We included 20 studies involving 1,992 patients with AVP deficiency. Most compared intranasal with oral desmopressin. Desmopressin is effective in reducing polydipsia and thirst while improving nighttime rest and quality of life. Hypernatremia above 150 mmol/L occurs in approximately 1%–2% of patients in the outpatient setting and is more common among inpatients.

The literature does not support a firm conclusion regarding the optimal treatment dose, algorithm, or follow-up strategy. The certainty of evidence for those issues is therefore very low.

## Postoperative AVP deficiency

A special situation is postoperative AVP deficiency. Approximately 20% of patients develop AVP deficiency after pituitary surgery, often transiently. Evidence regarding the optimal diagnostic criteria and optimal treatment in this setting is of low quality. This will be explained in more detail later.

With this final slide, I would like to hand over to Professor Mirjam Christ-Crain.
