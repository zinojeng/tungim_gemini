### Reproductive-axis control and energy balance

As I'm sure this audience is aware, over the past few decades, there's been an alarming trend toward earlier breast development in girls.

And while obesity, which will be the focus today, is thought to be the primary driver of earlier puberty, a number of other factors such as endocrine-disrupting compounds, stress, early life adversity, and changes in sleep patterns or increased light at night have also been implicated.

And before diving into how obesity affects puberty, I just want to step back for a moment and review the main components of the reproductive axis.

So the reproductive system is activated by a network of GnRH neurons at the base of the hypothalamus that then stimulate the pituitary and then the gonads.

And kisspeptin is the most potent stimulator of GnRH secretion, and one level above that is MKRN3, which acts as a brake on the reproductive axis.

And because reproduction is so energetically costly, particularly in girls and women, the hypothalamus is tightly regulated by a number of internal and external signals listed here.

And there's also a strong genetic component to pubertal timing and to the ongoing function of this axis.

So how does obesity or overweight get communicated to the brain?

Well, this topic could be a talk all on its own, and I will point you to two recent review papers that are excellent on this topic.

But I'll go over some of the main factors that we know play a role.

So this is a diagram of KNDy neurons talking to GnRH neurons, and in general, signals that tell the body about energy excess communicate that to the brain and are associated with earlier puberty, whereas signals that convey energy deficiency tend to delay puberty.

And I think we're familiar with some of the main actors that convey energy excess, such as IGF-1, insulin, leptin, and alpha-MSH.

And then those conveying energy deficiency include NPY, shown in red here, ghrelin, and FGF21.

But I just want to point out that over the past few years, there have been a number of additional actors that have been identified.

The majority of these also appear to act at the hypothalamic level, with the exception of ceramides, which may have a direct ovarian effect.

There was also a similar study published in 2024, a population-based study in Germany that similarly found that overweight and obesity were associated with increased 11-oxygenated androgens in a group of healthy girls.

Overweight and obesity among peripubertal girls cause increased androgen production through several mechanisms.

Insulin resistance and inflammation directly increase theca cell androgen biosynthesis, and adipocytes can also synthesize androgen de novo either through the classical androgen pathway or the 11-oxygenated androgen pathway.

The microbiome also appears to have a role, as several recent studies have shown reduced gut microbial diversity correlates with higher testosterone levels in obese women and adolescents, and the mechanism remains unclear, but it may be related to bile acid profiles, differences in steroid metabolism, and/or inflammation.

And lastly, individuals with overweight or obesity may be more susceptible to endocrine-disrupting compounds because these can accumulate in fat tissue.

Taking this one step further, increased prepubertal androgens in overweight and obese girls are also associated with earlier puberty, both earlier breast development and menarche.

### Conclusions

So, in conclusion, what we know is that obesity is associated with earlier pubertal onset, and a subset of girls with obesity or overweight develop peripubertal hyperandrogenemia.

There are distinct reproductive developmental trajectories that exist after menarche.

And what remains unknown is why only some of these girls develop androgen excess, whether these trajectories predict persistent ovulatory dysfunction, or PMOS, and whether early intervention can redirect reproductive maturation.

I want to thank and acknowledge the many people who have helped with my clinical research studies, including students and fellows, study coordinators, lab staff, bioinformatics support, and my collaborators and mentors.

Thank you for your attention.
