### Male obesity-related secondary hypogonadism

Let's start by talking about male obesity-related secondary hypogonadism.

You all know that this condition is highly prevalent, with up to 45% of patients with moderate-to-severe obesity having hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

Hypogonadism and obesity have a two-way relationship, with hypogonadism leading to an increase in fat mass and loss of fat-free mass that can facilitate obesity.

And obesity has several features that actually can lead to hypogonadism.

The first proposed mechanism was increased estrogen conversion due to aromatization of androgens into estrogens in adipose tissue.

Supporting this, selective estrogen receptor modulators can raise testosterone levels in patients with functional hypogonadism.

But in some cohorts, patients with metabolic hypogonadism have lower estrogen levels than patients without hypogonadism, challenging this hypothesis.

A second mechanism is low-grade systemic inflammation.

An overfed rabbit model has shown a form of hypothalamic inflammation, and anti-inflammatory treatment targeting interleukin-1 appears to raise testosterone levels.

Other associated factors include hyperinsulinemia, which has been linked with secondary hypogonadism in patients with functional hypogonadism, and high leptin levels associated with leptin resistance.

But among all these factors, we often neglect one which is very important that has been associated with hypogonadism, which is the topic of our talk, which is obstructive sleep apnea, OSA.

### Obstructive sleep apnea

So let's talk about obstructive sleep apnea.

Obstructive sleep apnea is a sleep-disordered breathing condition characterized by episodes of complete or partial upper-airway obstruction that impair normal ventilation during sleep and disrupt normal sleep patterns.

We know that obstructive sleep apnea increases in patients with obesity, with prevalence in males ranging from about 7% in lean patients to about 45% in patients with obesity.

Obstructive sleep apnea is also linked with comorbidities such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cerebrovascular disease, as well as an increased risk of death.

And it is a major cause of excessive sleepiness, which during work can be very risky for subjects in high-risk jobs.

So we were talking about the bidirectional link between obesity and hypogonadism, and so we have to add something else.

Obesity can lead to obstructive sleep apnea, which in turn is associated with hypogonadism and can further complicate the picture.

Here I show the American Academy of Sleep Medicine flowchart to highlight the importance of close collaboration with sleep physicians and of raising suspicion early in these patients.

There are many features and many symptoms that should lead, should prompt us to suspect obstructive sleep apnea.

The main features suggesting moderate or severe OSA are excessive daytime sleepiness together with at least two of the following three criteria: habitual loud snoring; witnessed apnea, gasping, or choking; or diagnosed hypertension.

From the same guidelines, I report you the classification of OSA.

This classification is based on the number of apneas or hypopneas, measured using the apnea-hypopnea index or the respiratory disturbance index, or RDI.

And if we have less than five events per hour, this is a normal subject.

If it is between 5 and 15, we have mild OSA.

Between 15 and 30 events per hour indicates moderate OSA, and more than 30 indicates severe OSA.

This is different in children, just to let you know.

In children, even the presence of one event per hour is diagnostic for obstructive sleep apnea.

### Effects on the reproductive endocrine system

But how does OSA affect the reproductive endocrine system?

OSA has been associated in the literature with disruption of both the somatotroph and gonadotroph axes because these axes are deeply linked to sleep architecture.

Talking about its association with hypogonadism, it has been known for a long time.

Here I show studies from the early 2000s by Luboshitzky and colleagues. In the first, from 2002, they measured LH and testosterone every 20 minutes in patients with obstructive sleep apnea and in healthy controls. Both the mean LH and testosterone levels and the areas under the curves were significantly lower in patients with obstructive sleep apnea and were linked more closely to the respiratory disturbance index than to the degree of hypoxemia.

So it seems that sleep fragmentation is the main driver of hypogonadism in obstructive sleep apnea, rather than the degree of hypoxemia reached.

Another study from the same group a few years later confirmed the first findings. The main differences in sleep stages between patients and healthy subjects involved stage 2 and slow-wave sleep, whereas REM sleep was comparable across groups.

A small randomized trial by Alvarenga et al. compared patients with obstructive sleep apnea with healthy subjects who underwent either REM-sleep deprivation or total sleep deprivation. Total sleep deprivation, rather than REM-sleep deprivation, mimicked the effect of obstructive sleep apnea on testosterone.

So it seemed that it is the loss of the deep sleep that impairs testosterone production in these patients.

This further supports the idea that the frequency of apneic and hypopneic events, rather than the degree of hypoxemia, contributes to hypogonadism.

Several studies reviewed in this paper show different HIF-1α activation and degradation under intermittent versus persistent hypoxia.

With hypoxia, HIF-1α is translocated to the nucleus, where it induces transcription of several factors in response to hypoxemia. This also downregulates its own transcription as a form of negative feedback, which is not present with intermittent hypoxemia; intermittent hypoxemia instead increases HIF-1α transcription.

Sorry.

Obstructive sleep apnea and intermittent hypoxia also lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, increasing reactive oxygen species and oxidative stress and producing low-grade systemic inflammation. This ultimately leads to endothelial dysfunction and sympathetic activation, which we have already seen as mechanisms of functional hypogonadism.

### Female reproductive function

We've been talking about the repercussions of OSA in men, but what about women?

Recently, some authors have proposed the existence of FOSH, F-O-S-H, as the female counterpart of male MOSH: female obesity-related secondary hypogonadism, in which metabolic and functional inhibition of the gonadal axis leads to a PCOS-like phenotype that, the authors say, is quite different from true PCOS.

And I'm afraid to say that unfortunately the data available for women in this field are even more scarce than the ones that we have for male.

But we can say that there seems to be a suppression of the gonadotropic axis even in this case, similarly to what happens to the male with alteration in the menses.

An interesting Mendelian randomization study found a bidirectional causal relationship between PCOS and OSA in women. I find this interesting in light of the proposed FOSH condition, but that is a topic for another talk.

And other evidence is actually linked AMH to apnea-hypopnea index, with an AMH which is reduced in female subjects with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome.

We know that although AMH is increased in patients with PCOS, it is reduced in patients with obesity.

So it seems to be something linked also to obesity, but to be exacerbated by obstructive sleep apnea.

In the other direction, we have stronger evidence.

We know that women have a steep increase in OSA prevalence after menopause.

Here I show another population study of more than 700 participants, in which higher female sex-hormone levels were associated with less OSA symptomatology.

So female sex hormones appear to be protective against obstructive sleep apnea.

### Clinical significance

So what is the clinical significance of OSA-related hypogonadism and reproductive dysfunction?

Okay, here the quality of the evidence is very low, so we have to say so.

In women, OSA is more likely to be linked with PCOS, as we have already said, but it is also associated with diminished ovarian reserve and infertility in epidemiologic studies.

Patients with PCOS and OSA also appear to require higher gonadotropin doses while yielding fewer retrieved oocytes and fewer good-quality embryos during ovarian stimulation for in vitro fertilization.

In men, OSA appears to be associated with a lower proportion of normal oval spermatozoa and a potential reduction in sperm concentration, although these findings were not confirmed after adjustment for BMI and may be more closely related to BMI.

Fatigue appears to be closely related to testosterone levels in OSA. This study also confirmed that severe obstructive sleep apnea is associated with hypogonadism.

In multivariable analysis, physical fatigue and reduced activity were associated with testosterone levels rather than with the apnea parameters themselves.

So it seems to be independently associated through testosterone levels.

Let's talk about erectile dysfunction.

We know that there is a high prevalence of erectile dysfunction in men with obstructive sleep apnea, and this study tried to understand this association.

They studied more than 200 patients and found that patients with severe OSA had reductions across all erectile-function dimensions in the questionnaires. Stepwise multiple logistic regression showed associations with age, as expected, but also with morning tiredness and the apnea-hypopnea index, reflecting OSA severity.

So erectile dysfunction appears to be closely linked to obstructive sleep apnea itself rather than only to associated features.

### Management of OSA-related hypogonadism

So how should we manage OSA-related hypogonadism?

The first thing that one may think about this is, I have low testosterone, I could give replacement therapy.

When testosterone is given to these men, it increases sexual desire, but some quality-of-life measures improve only in patients whose baseline testosterone is in the hypogonadal range.

However, we have to take into account that testosterone may have potentially deleterious effects in patients with obstructive sleep apnea.

Some authors have different ideas in this field on the clinical relevance of this effect, but this is an effect that is real.

In this randomized, placebo-controlled crossover trial, the investigators gave weekly high-dose testosterone injections of 500 mg, 250 mg, and 250 mg. In these healthy older men, high-dose testosterone increased respiratory-disturbance events by approximately seven per hour, which is not a small increase.

The treatment also prolonged hypoxemia, so this is a real potential effect.

Another consideration is that giving testosterone to patients with obstructive sleep apnea appears to increase the risk of elevated hematocrit.

So this can produce polycythemia, and it's something that we have to consider.

This retrospective study included 474 patients.

The investigators evaluated men with hypogonadism receiving testosterone therapy and found that OSA was significantly associated with polycythemia, with an adjusted odds ratio of approximately 2.1.

So, does correcting OSA with ventilation therapy correct testosterone?

Because maybe we should just treat the cause.

An important, relatively recent meta-analysis by Cignarelli et al. did not find a significant association between CPAP therapy and restoration of testosterone levels.

However, most studies in this meta-analysis did not adequately evaluate adherence to CPAP therapy.

For CPAP therapy to be beneficial, it should be worn for at least four hours per night on at least 70% of nights.

Adequate adherence was documented in only three of the studies.

Resolution of nocturnal hypoxia and adequate treatment settings were also not assessed in most studies.

Moreover, a weight-gain effect has been reported with CPAP therapy.

It is not sure why it is, but actually it has been reported, and this could act as a confounder as well.

### Severe-obesity cohort and CPAP follow-up

So this is a study that we conducted last year.

Our center treats patients with grade II complicated or grade III obesity. We enrolled 204 men with a BMI of at least 40 kg/m², or a BMI of at least 35 kg/m² plus obesity-related complications. We excluded patients with conditions or treatments that could interfere with the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis or introduce other confounding factors. We evaluated the association with OSA severity and, in a subgroup with newly diagnosed OSA who started CPAP therapy, evaluated gonadal function after three months of treatment.

We had 14 patients with a new diagnosis and sufficiently successful CPAP treatment to complete the longitudinal study.

And of course, we confirmed that there were lower testosterone levels in patients with severe obstructive sleep apnea.

### Conclusions

So, going to the conclusions, what should we do when we encounter functional hypogonadism?

I'd say that we should look for obstructive sleep apnea.

We should consider it.

We should look for possible symptoms of obstructive sleep apnea, so excessive daytime sleepiness, if there is, you know, witnessed apnea from the partner, if there is nocturia, if there is morning headache, if there are risk factors for obstructive sleep apnea, such as obesity or large neck circumference, or other factors such as craniofacial abnormalities or resistant hypertension.

If there are diseases known to be linked with obstructive sleep apnea, such as acromegaly, we should perform diagnostic testing; the standard diagnostic test is polysomnography.

We can start with a screening questionnaire and refer the patient to a sleep physician, but a questionnaire alone does not establish the diagnosis.

OSA-related hypogonadism may benefit from CPAP therapy because successful treatment may quickly reverse the hypogonadism.

We found that three months were enough to produce a clinically significant increase in testosterone. Treating OSA also addresses cardiovascular risk, which can be substantial in these patients.

Weight loss may further increase testosterone levels over time and may also help reduce OSA, perhaps even making CPAP therapy unnecessary in some patients.

Although testosterone replacement rapidly increases testosterone levels, it may worsen OSA and increase hematocrit. OSA should therefore be evaluated and treated before supplementation, as the guidelines state, particularly when OSA is severe and untreated.

So look for it and treat the cause.
