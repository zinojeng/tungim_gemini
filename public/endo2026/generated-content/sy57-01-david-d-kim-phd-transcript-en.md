### Cost-effectiveness plane and thresholds

Let us start with how we measure value using cost-effectiveness analysis. The fundamental framework for understanding the economic value of an intervention is to compare it with something else and ask whether the additional cost is justified by the additional benefit.

Look at the figure. One axis plots whether the intervention of interest provides additional benefit, on the positive or negative side. The other axis shows whether the intervention requires additional resource use; you can think of that as cost. This gives four quadrants. There is a standard of care. In the GLP-1 era, you can think of lifestyle modification, diet, exercise, or sometimes no intervention as the standard of care.

We compare that standard of care with intervention A in the northwest quadrant, which is more costly and less effective. Intervention B is in the southwest quadrant: less costly but less effective. Intervention C is less costly and more effective. Intervention D is more costly and more effective. For intervention A, you would not use it because the alternative is cheaper and more effective. We should use intervention C because it is less costly and more effective. The interesting decisions involve the northeast quadrant, where many new innovations fall. Newer technologies are generally more effective, but they carry a cost. How should we decide whether the additional benefit justifies the additional cost?

In health economics, we often use a cost-effectiveness benchmark: how much society is willing to pay to gain one year of life in perfect health. We need such a benchmark because resources are limited and there are many worthwhile things to pay for. Different countries use different cost-effectiveness thresholds. In the United States, a reasonable estimate is $100,000 to $150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year, or QALY. In other words, society is willing to pay $100,000 to $150,000 in return for one year of life in perfect health. Anything below that is deemed good value. Anything above that is not as good a value because those resources could be used for something else with a better return on investment.

Anything above the threshold line can be considered low economic value; anything below it can be considered good economic value. But this is tricky. I am probably the only health economist in this room who thinks about it this way. Clinicians often think about whether something has low or high clinical value, because an intervention that provides benefit has clinical value. The policy challenge arises when an intervention is in the clinically favorable green box but in the economically unfavorable red triangle: it provides some, or perhaps a lot of, clinical value, but the price cannot be justified. That is the central issue with GLP-1 RAs.

Think about where GLP-1 RAs were on this plane about two years ago. They produced a positive incremental benefit, but the cost was above the cost-effectiveness line. The natural question is whether we can make them high value. There are two pathways. One is to generate more benefit for the same resources, moving to the right on the horizontal axis. The other is to keep the benefit the same but bring the cost down, moving vertically below the threshold. Both can happen: we can recognize more benefits over time while prices decline. That is how GLP-1 RAs have been evolving over roughly the past three years.

### Clinical evidence and the pricing problem

Few medications have received as much coverage as GLP-1 RAs. I acknowledge that much of this talk focuses on the United States, although there is an international audience here. About five years ago, media reported that the FDA had approved an obesity drug that helped people lose 15% of their body weight. About a year later, The New York Times reported that patients taking an experimental obesity drug lost more than 50 pounds, according to the manufacturer. By the end of 2023, people such as Elon Musk and Kim Kardashian were promoting these weight-loss drugs on social media. Demand became extremely high, with related compounding issues.

But GLP-1 RAs are not just media hype. We have very strong evidence of significant weight loss. SELECT and SURMOUNT showed benefits of semaglutide and tirzepatide. Over time, we also learned that these are not just weight-loss drugs. They provide benefits beyond weight loss, including secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease among people with established cardiovascular disease, efficacy in obstructive sleep apnea, slowing the progression of chronic kidney disease, and, most recently, reducing fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis.

The clinical benefits are well established, but the problem I am focusing on is price, value, and affordability. When we conducted our study about two years ago, the net price of semaglutide in the United States was about $700 per month. The net price is already discounted from a list price of more than $1,300 and includes rebates and discounts. In the United States, Senator Bernie Sanders convened a committee investigation into why this was happening. The Economist asked what good whizzy new drugs are if the world cannot afford them. This is the real issue: we have drugs that everyone is excited about, but many cannot access them because the price is high and payers cannot afford broad coverage.

The policy questions are therefore: Based on the clinical evidence, are GLP-1 RAs for obesity cost-effective compared with standard care? If they are not cost-effective, at what price would they become cost-effective if all benefits remained the same? What is the estimated budget impact, and why are payers so hesitant to cover them? What are their long-term impacts? Many argue that GLP-1 RAs will improve long-term cardiometabolic outcomes and reduce incident diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Do those benefits save enough money to offset the initial investment? These are empirical questions. And what can we do to ensure that people have access to GLP-1 RAs?

### DOC-M microsimulation and lifetime outcomes

We conducted a modeling study using a microsimulation model I had developed a few years earlier. We tracked individual health trajectories over time, modeled how taking a GLP-1 RA changed each trajectory, and aggregated the results to estimate population-level and subgroup effects. Today I will focus on the population-level effects. The paper was published about a year ago, but it already feels outdated.

We used a nationally representative US population. The target population represented about 126 million adults aged 20 to 79 years with obesity, or with overweight plus at least one weight-related comorbidity, aligned with the FDA-indicated population. We modeled lifetime outcomes. We compared second-generation GLP-1 RAs—semaglutide and tirzepatide—plus lifestyle modification with lifestyle modification alone, following the pivotal trials.

The key question was how to model treatment efficacy. We modeled initial weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors for the first two years. As long as people remained on treatment, they maintained those benefits after two years. Trials also showed discontinuation, and we now know more about what happens after discontinuation. In the model, after treatment was stopped, weight and cardiometabolic risk factors returned to baseline and then followed their underlying temporal trends. We expressed costs in 2023 US dollars, reflecting the time of the study, and captured background health care costs. Even if the risk of diabetes or cardiovascular disease is reduced, people do not stop using the health care system. As people age, they continue to incur health care costs, and we wanted to capture that.

What about lifetime health outcomes? In our microsimulation, obesity cases were reduced by about 40%: in a population of 100,000, approximately 40,000 cases were averted. Diabetes cases were reduced by about 20%, and cardiovascular disease cases by about 10%, or roughly 10,000 cases per 100,000 people. Those are large numbers and make headlines, but how do they translate into the outcomes that matter: how long people live, how well they live, and how much we must pay?

We quantified those outcomes. Treatment extended life by about six months. I will not say whether that is a large or small gain, because it is of value to society. If we consider not only how long people live but also how well they live, we use the QALY, which adjusts remaining life-years for quality of life. The model estimated approximately 0.35 additional QALYs for tirzepatide, or roughly four additional months of life in perfect health.

The problem is cost. Over a lifetime, even after discounting future costs, treatment requires a substantial financial commitment. Based on the clinical trials and the head-to-head SURMOUNT-5 evidence, tirzepatide was more effective, but its incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was still about $200,000 per QALY gained—above the usual US cost-effectiveness threshold. We therefore asked what monthly price would make the drugs cost-effective, holding their benefits constant. For semaglutide, the estimated price was about $220 to $280 per month; for tirzepatide, about $290 to $374 per month. The higher value-based price for tirzepatide reflected its greater clinical benefit.

### Evolving evidence on economic value

As I mentioned, we are building the plane while flying it. By the time our study was published, new clinical evidence had already expanded the recognized benefits. The economic value itself evolves over time. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, or ICER, repeated its assessment because many new findings had emerged since its earlier analysis. I advised its modeling team.

In the updated analysis presented here, injectable semaglutide, oral semaglutide, and tirzepatide were all below $100,000 per QALY, indicating good economic value. Their value-based prices also increased, potentially above prior net prices, because we now recognize greater benefits. The slide reflects the September 2025 draft assessment available when this talk was prepared; later final estimates differ slightly.

There is substantial heterogeneity in the value-assessment literature, so we must be careful about what each analysis evaluates. Studies use different target populations and different comparators: lifestyle modification, usual care, semaglutide, or oral semaglutide. The resulting value propositions differ. Even so, most recent estimates in US health care settings suggest that GLP-1 RAs provide good value.

Are GLP-1 RAs good value for money? Yes, they appear cost-effective. But that does not mean society can afford them, because cost-effectiveness does not equal affordability.

### Medicare affordability and policy

Let me walk through what is happening in policy. Even if a therapy offers good value, many insurers still hesitate to cover it. We conducted an additional study of the impact of expanding GLP-1 RA coverage for obesity treatment in Medicare. For the international audience, Medicare is the US national insurance program primarily for older adults and also for certain people with permanent disabilities or end-stage renal disease.

When Congress created the Medicare prescription-drug benefit in the Medicare Modernization Act of 2003, the law explicitly permitted drugs used for anorexia, weight loss, or weight gain to be excluded from coverage. Twenty-three years ago, weight loss was not regarded as an important cardiometabolic treatment target; it was often viewed as cosmetic. Our understanding of obesity has changed greatly. Everyone in this room recognizes obesity as a disease and an important risk factor that must be addressed.

People have tried to change the coverage exclusion because it is written into law. Before leaving office in 2024, President Biden proposed a rule that would allow Medicare to cover obesity medications. The incoming administration did not implement that proposal, but developed its own approach. On November 6, 2025, President Trump announced policies intended to bring most-favored-nation pricing to American patients. Many people were confused, and I want to clarify that there is no single most-favored-nation price for every GLP-1 RA.

The actual cost depends on the product, indication, eligibility criteria, payer, and implementation pathway. TrumpRx is a direct-to-consumer option. It is like going to a website and buying the drug directly, and it applies to people without insurance. The prices shown here are $150 for oral semaglutide and $350 for injectable semaglutide. An uninsured person would still have to pay $350 per month, so even positive claims about this policy have equity implications.

For eligible people with Medicare, the Medicare GLP-1 Bridge provides a price of $245 per month with a $50 beneficiary copayment. The patient pays $50 and the program is responsible for $195. People whose drugs are indicated for type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, obstructive sleep apnea, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis can receive coverage through their usual Part D benefit. What they pay still depends on the details. There is also the BALANCE Model and its particular implementation pathway. For weight reduction and long-term weight maintenance, eligible beneficiaries can enroll in the Bridge and obtain a drug with a $50 copayment.

Why do payers hesitate to cover these drugs? The concern is the effect on premiums and overall affordability, not necessarily whether the drugs have value. To estimate budget impact, we must consider the size of the eligible population, how many people will use treatment, and how treatment changes net resource use, including downstream health care cost offsets.

Our analysis included 15.6 million existing eligible Medicare beneficiaries and 14.4 million people projected to become eligible, for a total of about 30 million. We excluded people who could already access a GLP-1 RA through diabetes or cardiovascular disease indications. Even with the new price, expanded coverage added an estimated $17.6 billion to Medicare spending over ten years. Medication spending was approximately $73.9 billion. Much of this spending occurs during the maintenance phase: people initiate a GLP-1 RA to achieve weight loss, but they must continue taking it to maintain that loss. That has important policy implications.

Many people claim that the drugs save money by improving health. The model did estimate $56.3 billion in health care cost offsets, but that was not enough to offset all medication spending. The net cost remained $17.6 billion when we conducted the study. At that time, we did not know exactly how beneficiary cost sharing would work. We therefore calculated the monthly price needed for Medicare to achieve cost neutrality: $187 per month. Under the $245 Medicare price, subtracting the patient's $50 responsibility leaves $195 for Medicare or the plan—very close to the cost-neutral estimate.

### Key implications

To summarize, the value of GLP-1 RAs is changing dynamically. Clinical benefits extend increasingly beyond weight loss, so their economic value has improved. Prices have also declined through policy actions and increasing market competition, and patent expiration in the US market is expected around 2032. Yet affordability and limited insurance coverage remain major barriers to equitable access, especially for people with limited health care resources. Can they truly use TrumpRx? Probably not. Without sustained coverage, the policy may exacerbate health inequities.

Medicare is a useful example because, once enrolled, beneficiaries generally remain in Medicare for the rest of their lives. Private insurers face member churn: average enrollment may last only two or three years, and 50% of enrollees change health plans. From a private insurer's perspective, the plan may not recoup its initial investment, which creates tension and helps explain why many plans still have not covered these drugs.

To achieve sustained and equitable outcomes, we must consider both access to effective pharmacotherapy and how to make long-term weight maintenance affordable. That may include combinations of nutrition and behavioral interventions and step-down approaches that are now being tested. Those strategies can help support sustainable and equitable access to GLP-1 RAs. Thank you.
