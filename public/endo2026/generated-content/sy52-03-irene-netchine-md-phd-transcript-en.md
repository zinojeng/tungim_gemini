Her final height is 145 centimeters—much better than her parents and we had expected, given the severity of her presentation.

This little girl has an imprinting disorder.

So what is it about? Well, you know that most of our genes are biallelically expressed, but some genes are expressed only from the paternal or the maternal allele. This monoallelic expression is secondary to epigenetic modifications, such as allele-specific DNA methylation at imprinting centers in CpG islands and specific histone modifications imposed on differentially methylated regions or imprinting-center regions. We can measure this differential methylation at an imprinting center by calculating the methylation index, which is 50% when it is well balanced.

Why do we have imprinted genes? One explanation is the kinship, or parental-conflict, theory. Maternally expressed genes generally restrain fetal growth, whereas paternally expressed genes enhance fetal growth. These genes are important for embryonic and placental development and for regulating growth, metabolism, puberty, and cognitive development.

When these genes are dysregulated, they cause syndromes that we know well because most produce undergrowth. The molecular mechanisms of these imprinting disorders include duplication or deletion of regions containing imprinted genes, mutations in imprinted genes, uniparental disomy of chromosomes containing imprinted genes, and epimutations with loss or gain of methylation at the genes' imprinting centers.

### Silver–Russell syndrome and Temple syndrome

Today we will focus on Silver–Russell syndrome (SRS), called Russell–Silver syndrome in the United States, and Temple syndrome. Although SRS was described in the 1950s by Drs. Silver and Russell, the first international consensus statement was not published until 2016. It was developed on behalf of several international pediatric endocrinology societies and five patient support groups. The consensus retained a clinical definition using the six-item Netchine–Harbison Clinical Scoring System: being born small for gestational age (SGA), postnatal growth failure, relative macrocephaly at birth, protruding forehead, body asymmetry, and feeding difficulties and/or low BMI in early childhood. The diagnosis should be considered when at least four of these six criteria are present. The system was designed to be highly sensitive rather than highly specific so as not to miss cases.

The main molecular cause of SRS, identified by our group in 2005, involves epigenetic abnormalities in the 11p15.5 H19/IGF2 imprinting region.

So this is an imprinting region. There is methylation on the paternal allele, which expresses IGF2, while the non-methylated maternal allele expresses H19, which is a non-coding RNA. And so what we identified is that half of the patients with Silver-Russell syndrome have loss of methylation at this imprinting center region at IGF2, which is linked to diminished IGF2 expression. And since it's an epigenetic anomaly, it occurs as a mosaic, thought to be arising at the postzygotic period. There are also genes, mutations of genes that are part of this HMGA2-PLAG1 IGF2 pathway, and we have another identified rare mutation in patients suspected of Silver-Russell syndrome. Those genes are HMGA2 and PLAG1 transcription factor that enhance the production of IGF2, and so you have to have a balanced expression of these three genes. If they are overexpressed, it gives cancer, and we have shown that if you have mutation in one of these three genes, it gives restricted growth and a clinical picture very similar to Silver-Russell syndrome.

The first molecular defect identified, in the 1980s, was maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7, written upd(7)mat. The individual has two maternally derived copies of chromosome 7, and this accounts for approximately 10% of molecularly confirmed cases.

To date, we do not know which gene is principally responsible for growth restriction in upd(7)mat.

So now to come to Temple syndrome. Temple syndrome, in fact, has a lot of overlap with Silver-Russell syndrome that you can see here in black on this slide.

Children with Temple syndrome are frequently overweight after age two, have severe neonatal hypotonia, and almost all develop precocious puberty; behavioral disturbances have also been described. Temple syndrome results from abnormalities of the imprinted DLK1/MEG3 region at chromosome 14q32.2. As at the 11p15.5 H19/IGF2 region, the paternal allele is methylated and expresses `DLK1`, whereas the maternal allele expresses noncoding RNAs. We realized that about two-thirds of patients with Temple syndrome scored positive using the SRS clinical scoring system. Laboratory work showed diminished `IGF2` expression in Temple syndrome, almost as severe as in SRS with H19/IGF2 loss of methylation. Increased expression of maternal noncoding RNAs from the DLK1/MEG3 region appears to reduce `IGF2` expression, helping explain the phenotypic overlap.

So recently, we decided to update this first SRS consensus because there were new data in terms of molecular diagnosis and in terms of also recommendation and publication on treatment. And so, about a year ago, we met in Cambridge with a group of doctors and also patient organization. And again, we had the chance of being endorsed by the major international pediatric endocrine societies. And we did this consensus meeting with academic funding only. And the patient organization also participated very actively.

One recommendation from the updated consensus is that SRS should now be a molecular diagnosis, suspected initially through the clinical scoring system. Given the clinical overlap, Temple syndrome should be screened for during first-line molecular evaluation when SRS is suspected.

With a positive clinical score suggesting SRS, first-line DNA methylation testing should encompass 11p15.5, chromosome 7 markers, and the chromosome 14q32.2 region associated with Temple syndrome. If an abnormality is identified, the diagnosis is confirmed and management follows either the Temple syndrome pathway—which substantially overlaps with SRS care—or the SRS pathway for chromosome 11 or 7 abnormalities. If no methylation abnormality is identified, testing proceeds to copy-number variants (CNVs), single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), and uniparental disomy (UPD), potentially identifying rare genetic causes. Maternal UPD of chromosomes 14, 16, or 20 may provide differential diagnoses. If all testing is negative, SRS may still be suspected, but the diagnosis should remain under regular review because an alternative diagnosis may require different treatment. Because epigenetic abnormalities can be mosaic, testing a second tissue, for example after skin biopsy, may also be important.

### Nutrition, growth, puberty, and metabolism

For multidisciplinary care and follow-up of SRS and Temple syndrome, it is important to establish international cohorts because these syndromes are rare and such collaboration supports clinical trials and future guideline development. There is a real interaction among nutrition, growth, metabolism, and puberty. They are all linked, and there is a narrow window in which to optimize nutritional status and support growth without driving overweight, metabolic syndrome, or early pubertal onset with rapidly advancing bone age.

So we know that these children, and it's part of the scoring system, can be very undernourished, and then it can complicate with fasting hypoglycemia. They can be tired. They have muscle hypoplasia.

It is important to begin nutritional intervention for severe SRS before GH therapy. The consensus recommends addressing nutrition before starting GH, avoiding enteral tube feeding when possible because it can have many complications in this population, and deferring GH until caloric deficits have been addressed.

Now, being addressed, that doesn't mean have been solved, and we will see that it can be helpful to open the appetite to give GH, but you want to first start to address the nutrition before you start growth hormone. And for this, we have a publication now in this group of patients on cyproheptadine, which is an appetite stimulant drug previously used in pediatric condition with malnutrition, and that has been shown to be effective and improve weight and growth velocity in children with SRS. As you can see here, that during the start of treatment, they improve their weight and they even improve slightly their height.

But we have to be extremely careful because fetal programming is modified by the severe in utero growth restriction.

So we need to improve the nutritional status, but they have abnormal body composition, low muscle mass, high risk of developing type 2 diabetes, and they can really switch from absence of hunger and denutrition to overfeeding and overweight.

So we gave a lot of recommendations about this. Parents and caregivers should be informed that the nutritional goals will change over the lifespan. The evolution of food behavior should be explained. We can, as mentioned, do a trial of cyproheptadine that we will discontinue if it's unresponsive or if the BMI is excessive during treatment. And we also have to advise everyone about the risk of insulin resistance associated with excessive weight gain, overfeeding, and we have to screen for physical and biochemical indicators of insulin resistance annually, including during GH treatment, and consider measurement of body composition, of course, if available.

GH is prescribed for this population under the SGA indication in both the United States and Europe. Treatment aims to improve linear growth, but also—very importantly—to improve body composition, especially lean mass and appetite, and reduce the risk of hypoglycemia. It not only improves height but also improves weight-for-height percentage and BMI and reduces—

**Audience member:** What are your thoughts about gastrostomy tubes?

**Irène Netchine:** I mentioned this very briefly. Thirty or 40 years ago, a child diagnosed with severe SRS would often receive a gastrostomy tube almost systematically. We realized that there are many complications. First, children may stop eating orally altogether. Second, because they can have slow gastric emptying, they may develop multiple complications.

We try to avoid gastrostomy tubes as much as possible. In our experience, fewer than 10% of children now require one.

We must be extremely careful about the risk of hypoglycemia. We give families an emergency card, and the children must not fast.

Avoiding a gastrostomy tube is better for oral feeding development when it can be done safely.

These children are very fragile and should go to the emergency department as soon as they cannot eat or become ill.

**Audience member:** You mentioned in the guidance that nutrition should be improved, but not too much. What is the target?

**Irène Netchine:** For young children around two years of age, we recommend a BMI around 11 or 12—which is very low—and an ideal weight-for-height around 80%.

So this is because they have a very low muscle mass.

So if they have an ideal weight for height of 100%, which is a normal ideal weight for height, you will have the impression that this child is obese.

We recommend supporting muscle development and being very careful not to overfeed. You have seen the BMI curves of some of these children; they can resemble those of a child with Prader–Willi syndrome. This can occur rapidly in Temple syndrome and also in SRS. Many children whom we have followed since birth are doing well, but we have to reinforce this message with parents continually.
