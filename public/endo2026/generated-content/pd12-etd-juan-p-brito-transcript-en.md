### Why an Evidence-to-Decision framework is needed

Thank you, Dr. Maraka, and thank you, everyone, for having me here. Now we are going to move toward the next step in guideline development.

At this point, we have reviewed that we have a PICO question. We have been asked to resolve this question from the guideline. We now have the outcomes of interest. In addition to the outcomes of interest, we also have the evidence regarding benefits, which we call desirable effects; harms, which we call undesirable effects; and how confident we are that the estimates are close to the truth. That is what we have so far.

Now we have to move from that evidence to a recommendation, and to do that we use what is called the Evidence-to-Decision, or EtD, framework. GRADE provides a structured approach to do this. But it is very important—and probably one of the most important aspects—that the evidence itself does not dictate the recommendation. The judgments of the panelists are what actually determine the recommendation.

This is relevant because, if you have two different panel groups, depending on how they value these judgments, you can have two different recommendations. The idea is not to reproduce something in a way that makes every panel agree. It is actually for the panel to voice its judgments in a way that allows anyone to see the values the panel placed on the recommendation and to understand how it arrived there. The main goal is transparency rather than reproducibility, and the process is based on judgment.

### Domains considered by the panel

It is actually quite straightforward. Think about the balance that Dr. Maraka was referring to. On one side of the balance, we have everything that favors the intervention—in this case, vitamin D supplementation. On the other side, we have everything that favors the alternative, which is no vitamin D supplementation. In the middle, you have a group of considerations that are important for decision-making.

The goal of the panel is to make judgments, based on evidence, for each of those domains and to see how those domains move toward either the intervention or the alternative. So far, we have reviewed evidence for only three domains: desirable effects, undesirable effects, and certainty of the evidence.

What happens with the rest? That is when we go back to the panel and ask its members to do a little more work. We tell them: the evidence team has already summarized the desirable and undesirable effects, but now we want you to look for evidence for the remaining important domains. How might vitamin D implementation affect equity? How is vitamin D perceived by patients in terms of their values and preferences? Is it acceptable for clinicians to use vitamin D supplementation in this age group? Is it feasible for health care systems to implement it? Is it costly, and is it cost-effective?

The panel needs to look for the best available evidence for this. Panel members create small groups and start working on it. Based on that evidence and their judgments, they move the different domains toward the intervention or the alternative. We do it this way because, if the balance of effects clearly favors the intervention, we move toward a recommendation for the intervention. At the opposite end, we move against the intervention, as Dr. Maraka was saying. We need to understand how these domains move the balance.

### How GRADEpro records evidence and judgments

We use software called GRADEpro, as Dr. Maraka mentioned. We are going to ask you to participate actively by voting on at least four of these domains. Pay attention to this section because it closely resembles what panelists do.

This is how the software is set up for each domain. On the left, we have the judgment options. In the middle, we have the research evidence that will be populated for the panel. On the right, we have additional considerations.

In real life, we proceed one domain at a time and tell the panelists what the evidence is. We review that evidence. Panelists may say, “This study was done with this particular caveat. The population was not the correct one,” or they may point to a subgroup. They may also add their own experience and perspective to help interpret the evidence. Those are additional considerations. Based on the research evidence and the additional considerations, they put forward a judgment. In this example, the judgment concerns how substantial the desirable anticipated effects are.

We are going to do exactly the same thing with all of you today.

For desirable effects of vitamin D supplementation, you have three outcomes of interest: any fractures, falls, and all-cause mortality. The most important part of the table is the absolute difference in effect.

For any fracture, vitamin D results in 1 more fracture per 1,000 people. For falls, it results in 16 fewer falls per 1,000. For all-cause mortality, it results in 6 fewer deaths per 1,000. Those are the effect estimates for the desirable-effects domain.

Your task is to give me your judgment about whether the overall difference between vitamin D and no vitamin D is trivial, small, moderate, large, variable, or unknown.

To help a little, when we think about effect sizes, we tend to consider fewer than 10 events per 1,000 to be trivial; 10 to 25 fewer events to be small; 25 to 100 fewer events to be moderate; and more than 100 fewer events to be large. Those are rules of thumb.

This has to do with the number of events, but when we consider what is trivial, small, moderate, or large, we also have to think about the importance of the outcome. Preventing skin rashes is not the same as preventing deaths. It is very important to consider not only the frequency of events but also the importance of the particular outcome.

But the panel placed more emphasis on the mortality component. The potential to prevent 6 deaths per 1,000 was not considered trivial; it was considered small. As you can see, different panels could place different weights on the outcomes, and another panel could easily have judged this effect trivial rather than small.

### Patient values and preferences

One of the domains concerns values. This asks to what extent patients value the outcomes, and whether their preferences and the weights they place on those outcomes are similar to those selected by the panel.

In an ideal world, we would have good studies in which thousands of people are asked whether preventing falls, fractures, or respiratory infections is important to them. Usually, we do not have those studies. We still ask panelists to look for them, but they are very difficult to find.

For this particular domain, the panel did not find direct studies describing patients' values and preferences for this exact decision. However, it noted that patients do want to prevent fractures. Some indirect evidence suggested that loss of independence can be considered even more important than dying. The slide cites evidence that 80% of women older than 75 years would rather die than lose independence because of a hip fracture and nursing-home admission.

The panel therefore had to consider whether patients, if asked about these outcomes, would choose differently or apply different weights—for example, whether they would consider mortality less important or respiratory tract infections more important. This is, to some extent, a mental exercise.

The panel judged that there was probably no important uncertainty or variability in these values. It thought patients would value the outcomes similarly to the panel: mortality would remain important, and patients would very likely place substantial value on preventing falls and fractures.

### Cost-effectiveness, equity, acceptability, and feasibility

The panel must consider not only cost but cost plus benefit, which is addressed by cost-effectiveness studies. It could not find a cost-effectiveness study directly evaluating vitamin D supplementation in this age group. However, it found cost-effectiveness studies concerning screening and drew some indirect information from them. The slide notes that targeted screening and treatment is generally more cost-effective than universal supplementation for preventing falls and fractures in older adults, with the greatest benefits in adults aged 80 years or older.

Given that vitamin D is inexpensive and the panel believed it still had beneficial effects, its cost-effectiveness judgment was that it probably favors the intervention.

Equity concerns how implementing an intervention in the current health care system would affect health equity. There was limited direct evidence. The panel noted that vitamin D deficiency is more common in some racial and ethnic minority groups and in lower-resource countries. Disparities in vitamin D supplement use exist, with lower use among Black and Hispanic populations. The trials were mostly conducted in White women, limiting generalizability to other populations.

With that information, the panel judged that recommending vitamin D supplementation would probably increase equity. The rationale was that, if the recommendation promoted supplementation in groups that had been affected by disparities and had received less vitamin D, some of those inequities might improve.

Acceptability asks whether clinicians and other key stakeholders would accept a vitamin D recommendation. It is difficult to find direct studies, but the panel judged “probably yes” based on the relative ease, low cost, and safety record of vitamin D supplementation. It also recognized variation among health systems: in some countries, beneficial medications are provided by government programs; in others, they are covered by insurance or paid for out of pocket. Individual costs could affect acceptability.

Feasibility is not just about clinicians; it is usually about whether the health care system can implement the intervention. Something can be widely accepted by clinicians but difficult for the system to implement because it requires substantial resources. No direct feasibility studies were identified, but because vitamin D is relatively inexpensive and easy to provide, the panel judged that implementation was probably feasible.
