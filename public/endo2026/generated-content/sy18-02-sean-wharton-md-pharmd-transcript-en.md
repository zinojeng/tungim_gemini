### Introduction and objective

**Sean Wharton:** Thank you very much. It is very kind of the Endocrine Society to invite an internist to present, and a Canadian to present. I am here from Canada, and it is exciting to be here. By the way, we tied our game yesterday in the World Cup. You guys won. I cannot wait for America to be on the podium—on the worldwide soccer stage.

I have been asked to speak about oral and non-peptide anti-obesity medications, specifically expanding access and adherence. I will talk quite a bit about expanding access and not as much about adherence. We are going to discuss this emerging field of oral therapies.

I am going to start with my disclosures, but first I want to start with who I am, what I am doing here, and how I got onto this stage. This is my wife; she is a family doctor in Toronto. That is baby James. James was born in September 2020. Right next to that is the Canadian obesity clinical practice guideline. I was the lead author, and it was published in 2020 at the same time. It was a very busy year and a very busy period. It took 72 authors and three and a half years to produce the obesity guideline. It took a little less time to make baby James, and just one other person—not 72.

### Bias, stigma, and access to obesity care

The Canadian obesity guideline really changed the way we think about obesity medicine. It was a watershed moment and a change in practice. We emphasized bias, stigma, and discrimination in the first chapter. We also had chapters on science, pharmacotherapy, diet and nutrition, exercise, maternal health, and many other topics—20 chapters in total. But do not go past chapter 1 if you do not understand that you are biased, that we view stigmatizing images and use stigmatizing words every day, and that this can lead to discrimination.

You do not have to discriminate, but you have to believe that you are biased against people with obesity. I am biased against people with obesity. It was the first guideline that really emphasized this and turned the camera lens onto us, rather than focusing only on the person living with obesity and all of their problems. That was important because this is about access to care.

I run a large clinic in Toronto, Canada, established in 2008. We see many patients, and it works very well. Health care in Canada actually works sometimes. We see approximately 1,000 new patients every month, have about a two-week waiting period, and the care is free to patients. You do not pay for the care; you pay for the medications. The medications are not free, but the care is.

I am showing this because obesity medicine is not something we fully understand. We do not understand the brain, the hormones, or all the receptors yet. We need academic research, which we conduct at the clinic, and our patients contribute to that. We also conduct pharmacotherapy clinical trials with pharmaceutical companies to improve our understanding. We participated in the trials I am going to present regarding oral therapies.

Injectable molecules have been the primary molecules available to us. What expensive injectable obesity medications did was expose disparities in care for chronic conditions, particularly among marginalized people. If you are a Black American woman, you are marginalized. If you are an Indigenous person in Canada, you are marginalized. These are populations with some of the highest rates of obesity and diabetes, and they are often the last to receive the medications on an accessible basis. We always knew there were disparities, but the injectable therapies made them very clear.

In middle-income countries, access to injectable GLP-1 receptor agonists has been limited. Low-income countries have had almost no access. Yet some of the fastest-growing obesity rates in the world are in those countries, partly because they began with lower rates and therefore can rise more rapidly. The question is whether oral medications—which may be easier to produce and cost less to ship and store—can be distributed around the world. Can we get them to the southern United States? Can we get them to Indigenous populations on reserves in Canada, where we cannot even reliably provide clean running water but might be able to provide some of this treatment?

### Three waves of oral GLP-1-based therapy

Here is where the oral medications are. The first wave comprises oral semaglutide and orforglipron. The United States is the first country to have these two medications approved. They were approved quickly and early in the context of efforts to offer a lower price. The rest of the world is approximately a year behind, although some places will receive them sooner.

A second wave is coming, with agents such as aleniglipron, oral VK2735, and NN-9932 that will compete in this space. We will see how well they work and how effective they actually are. A third wave may include oral amylin-based molecules, such as amycretin, and oral polyagonists.

### Oral semaglutide and SNAC

Let us discuss the first wave, beginning with oral semaglutide 25 mg in adults with overweight or obesity. As I mentioned, I am passionate about access to care, the Canadian obesity guideline, and the work I do. I was the lead author of the OASIS 4 paper. I am not resting until there is strong recognition of bias, stigma, discrimination, and the need for access to care. That is how I became the lead author of this paper.

Semaglutide is a large peptide molecule, and normally large peptides cannot be taken orally. We have to inject them. How close are we to oral insulin? We are not close: it is too large, gets broken down, and passes out the other end instead of entering the bloodstream. Semaglutide is somewhat smaller, so perhaps we can get some of it across the gastrointestinal barrier.

The way we do this uses the SNAC molecule—sodium N-(8-[2-hydroxybenzoyl] amino) caprylate. Semaglutide is co-formulated with SNAC into a tablet. Absorption occurs in the stomach. SNAC causes a local increase in pH, which improves semaglutide solubility and protects it from proteolytic degradation. This allows a small amount—about 1% with the first-generation formulation—to be absorbed. Getting a large molecule across the gastric barrier at even 1% is Nobel-level science. If that 1% puts enough medication into the bloodstream, the medicine can be effective.

This introduces the concept of API, or active pharmaceutical ingredient: how much drug must be manufactured and placed in the tablet for the amount that enters the body when the rest passes through? At 1% absorption, 99% is not absorbed, but all of that material still had to be manufactured. That is a lot of active pharmaceutical ingredient.

Because absorption is so low, oral semaglutide must be taken on an empty stomach, fasting in the morning, separated from food and other medications, with up to four ounces of water. We tend to use morning dosing because that is the easiest time for patients to be fasting. As with levothyroxine, another time such as nighttime might sometimes be considered, but for oral semaglutide morning is probably best because of the fasting requirement.

The first-generation SNAC formulation gave approximately 1% bioavailability. That is the formulation used for the oral semaglutide diabetes product Rybelsus at 3, 7, and 14 mg.

The formulation scientists then developed a second-generation formulation that gives approximately 1.7% to 2% bioavailability. That still does not sound like much, but it is roughly double the earlier formulation. Therefore, the corresponding diabetes doses can contain less active pharmaceutical ingredient: 1.5, 4, and 9 mg rather than 3, 7, and 14 mg, while providing similar exposure. The 25 mg oral semaglutide tablet for obesity also uses the higher-bioavailability formulation; we no longer need the 50 mg dose used in the earlier obesity program.

### OASIS 1 and OASIS 4

The OASIS trials evaluated oral semaglutide for obesity rather than diabetes. As with injectable semaglutide, where the diabetes dose is 1.0 mg and the obesity dose is 2.4 mg, we need a higher exposure to treat obesity and act in the brain. The previous 14 mg—or the second-generation 9 mg—would not be enough.

OASIS 1 evaluated oral semaglutide 50 mg with the first-generation formulation. During that period, the second-generation formulation became available. We then conducted OASIS 4 with 25 mg. That dose made the medicine more viable, and the results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

Before I show the weight loss, let us do some mathematics. If oral and injectable formulations put similar amounts of semaglutide into the bloodstream, we should expect similar results. Everyone knows the obesity result with injectable semaglutide 2.4 mg: approximately 15% to 16% weight loss.

With oral semaglutide 25 mg taken daily, multiply 25 mg by seven days and then by approximately 2% absorption. That is about 3.5 mg absorbed per week. I said bioavailability was in the range of 1.7% to 2%, so 3.5 mg is reasonably close to a 2.4 mg weekly injection. What should the weight loss be? It should be similar—and it was. Oral semaglutide 25 mg produced almost exactly the weight loss seen with injectable semaglutide 2.4 mg. That is why it is such a big deal.

In the OASIS 4 trial-product estimand, mean body weight decreased by 16.6% at week 64 with oral semaglutide 25 mg and by 2.7% with placebo, for an estimated treatment difference of 13.9 percentage points. We should expect similar effects on comorbidities and a similar adverse-effect profile to injectable semaglutide 2.4 mg because it is the same active molecule, although those questions need their own evidence.

For side effects in obesity medicine, I keep two reference numbers in my head from STEP 1, because that study set the standard: with semaglutide 2.4 mg, nausea was about 44% and vomiting about 25%. If nausea is 65% or 70%, that is not so good. If vomiting is much greater than 25%, that is not great. I cannot remember every adverse event, so I look at those two.

In OASIS 4, nausea occurred in 46.6% of participants receiving oral semaglutide 25 mg and vomiting in 30.9%. Those values are a little higher but in approximately the same range. This gives us some reassurance that the adverse-effect profile is broadly familiar.

### Switching between oral and injectable semaglutide

An indirect treatment comparison evaluated oral semaglutide 25 mg versus injectable semaglutide 2.4 mg for weight-loss outcomes. The results support comparable efficacy. In the United States prescribing information, a patient receiving injectable semaglutide 2.4 mg can be switched to oral semaglutide 25 mg according to the labeled transition instructions. If someone is having side effects or adapting to the different administration, I would consider going down one dose level and then increasing again as tolerated rather than assuming every transition will be effortless.

The oral product was launched in the United States in January. Look at the launch velocity: it shattered historical pharmaceutical-launch volume baselines, and 76% of users were reported to be completely new to GLP-1 therapy. People were looking for this and waiting for it.

Where will oral semaglutide fit in practice? Canada does not have it yet; we will likely receive it in the first quarter of 2027. It is the same semaglutide molecule with cardiovascular-outcomes evidence. It is the first oral GLP-1 option for weight management, and many people were waiting for it. On the other hand, it cannot be taken with food or other medications, and the large amount of active pharmaceutical ingredient could be limiting. Perhaps a third- or fourth-generation formulation will continue to reduce how much active drug has to be manufactured for each absorbed dose.

### Orforglipron: a non-peptide small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist

The next part of this first wave is a small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist. We are no longer talking about a peptide analog. Dan Drucker, a brilliant scientist in Toronto, helped identify and characterize the GLP-1 receptor. After the receptor was identified, scientists developed peptide analogs that resemble endogenous GLP-1 and fit into that receptor. The scientists in Denmark created a large peptide analog that works.

But could other scientists create a small molecule that fits into the GLP-1 receptor and produces activity on the other side? We do not have a small molecule that enters the insulin receptor and simply works like insulin, and the same is true for many other endocrine receptors. Yet with the GLP-1 receptor, a small molecule was developed that fit into the receptor and produced downstream activity. That is remarkable and very exciting.

Orforglipron is a once-daily, non-peptide, small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist. It is a partial agonist biased toward cyclic AMP activation over β-arrestin recruitment at the GLP-1 receptor. Its chemical backbone differs from lotiglipron and danuglipron, development of which was stopped after liver-enzyme concerns. Orforglipron has a tighter, smaller, different backbone. As additional “gliprons” are developed, scientists are trying to use structures that avoid those adverse hepatic effects.

When thinking about small molecules, remember that they can distribute throughout the body. We therefore look for off-target adverse effects. Larger peptide molecules may bind the intended receptor or albumin and distribute in more predictable ways, whereas small molecules make hepatic effects and the exact chemical backbone especially important.

### ATTAIN-1

ATTAIN-1 evaluated orforglipron for obesity. An important feature was inclusion of more participants in the lower BMI range. Approximately 9% to 10% had a BMI below 30 kg/m². Participants with a BMI of 27 to less than 30 kg/m² had to have at least one weight-related comorbidity.

We often focus on people with BMIs of 30, 35, or 40 and forget the millions or billions of people with a BMI from 27 to less than 30 plus a comorbidity such as prediabetes or hypertension. They are a large part of society and are often overlooked. Many obesity trials include only 1% or 2% of participants in that range; here it was approximately 10%. I would like to see even more in future trials.

Remember that weight loss with semaglutide is approximately 16%. Would we like to see 16% or 20% with orforglipron? Perhaps, but that is not what we saw. At the 36 mg dose, mean weight loss with the efficacy estimand was 12.4% at week 72. That is less than with some injectable GLP-1-based therapies, particularly newer agents, but it is still substantial. A large proportion of people reached more than 10% weight loss, a range where many comorbidities improve. This may be a benefit for the population and for clinicians who want a less aggressive degree of weight loss.

For adverse events, again I think of 44% nausea and 25% vomiting as reference values. In the orforglipron 36 mg group, nausea occurred in 33.7% and vomiting in 24.0%. Those are in the range where I am reasonably comfortable with the gastrointestinal adverse-effect profile. There were no major concerns from those two common events, although this is a new small molecule and surveillance for hepatic and other off-target effects remains important.

### Drug interactions and place in therapy

The FDA approved orforglipron in the United States, making the United States the first country to receive it. Unlike peptide semaglutide, this small molecule has clinically relevant interaction considerations involving CYP3A4 and transport pathways. Strong CYP3A4 inducers should be avoided. With strong inhibitors and combined CYP3A4/OATP1B inhibition, dose restrictions or avoidance may be required; moderate inducers require monitoring. The complete prescribing information should guide these decisions.

Simvastatin exposure increases by approximately 33%, so the simvastatin dose should not exceed the labeled limit. We do not use simvastatin as often now; other commonly used statins do not have the same listed restriction. Levothyroxine has no identified interaction with orforglipron, whereas thyroid parameters should be monitored with oral semaglutide because oral semaglutide can affect levothyroxine exposure.

For oral contraceptives, the current orforglipron guidance calls for a non-oral contraceptive or an added barrier method for 30 days after initiation and for 30 days after each dose escalation. Orforglipron is not recommended in severe hepatic impairment. These are examples of small-molecule off-target and interaction issues we need to examine. It is a new molecule. The FDA and clinicians still need to learn more about it. We do not know as much about it as we do about the larger semaglutide molecule, but it is a big deal.

Where does it fit? It is the first approved small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist and the first GLP-1 medicine approved for obesity before type 2 diabetes. It can be administered without regard to food or water. Weight loss is not as robust as with many newer injectable agents, but is that necessarily negative? It may be positive. Some physicians do not want 30% weight loss and are concerned about muscle changes. A 12% reduction may be something a primary-care physician, gastroenterologist, or cardiologist is comfortable prescribing while still achieving important health benefits.

### Conclusion

We will see what the next waves of oral therapies have to offer. Injectable obesity medications are not accessible to most of the world. Oral medications provide greater choice and may improve access to care. Oral GLP-1 medicines may change the horizon for weight management around the world. Thank you very much for your time.

### Responders, nonresponders, and precision medicine

**Moderator:** Last question: what differentiates GLP-1 receptor agonist responders from nonresponders?

**Sean Wharton:** Does that person want to win a Nobel Prize, or are they a true scientist with a laboratory somewhere working out the genetics of this? I think genetics will be important. Precision medicine will begin to identify genes that tell us who will respond, who will not, and why some people do not respond.

You may be accustomed to waterfall plots in which every participant is plotted. Weight comes down a little in some and a lot in others, but there are a few people at the other end whose weight goes up. In a clinical trial, you wonder whether they actually injected the medicine. How could weight rise with a treatment expected to reduce it? It seems implausible, but it is probably real, because we can measure drug levels and confirm that they are taking it. They are nonresponders.

These nonresponders do not necessarily need a higher dose of the same medicine; they need a different medicine. Each time we move from single GLP-1 receptor agonists to dual and then triple agonists, the group of nonresponders becomes smaller as we engage more targets—GIP and glucagon in addition to GLP-1.

My recommendation for a person who does not respond to a particular medicine is to switch to another medicine. Some people may not respond even to a dual agonist, so try a different drug. Whether oral therapies will become those alternative drugs, I do not know; I do not think we yet have the answer.

From a genetic standpoint, we will learn more about why nonresponders do not respond and what their genetic profiles are. Precision medicine will be the next wave, and it will let us understand treatment response at a much deeper level.
