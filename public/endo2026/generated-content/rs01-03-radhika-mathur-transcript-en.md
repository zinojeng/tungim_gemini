## Familial adrenal-tumor cluster

**Radhika Mathur:** Good morning, everyone. My name is Dr. Radhika Mathur from the USF Ocala Internal Medicine Program. In collaboration with the Carling Adrenal Center, I am here to present a very interesting case that challenges our understanding of a classic adrenal syndrome.

The story begins with a 63-year-old woman who was found to have a massive adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC). Surprisingly, 13 years later, she was found to have another ACC. Her daughter, who was being evaluated for a hypertensive crisis, was also found to have a pheochromocytoma and a cortical adenoma. Three distinct adrenal tumors, two generations, and one gene: this aggressive clustering strongly suggested a familial genetic syndrome.

But what happens when the disease actively deceives the diagnostic tests on which we rely? By investigating this familial cluster, we uncovered three critical clinical lessons.

## Lesson 1: retained MSH6 staining did not exclude a pathogenic variant

ACC is a rare but recognized manifestation of Lynch syndrome, so we considered a mismatch-repair defect. The standard immunohistochemical (IHC) stain showed retained MSH6 protein expression. The clinical picture nevertheless suggested otherwise, so we pursued germline sequencing and found a pathogenic `MSH6` variant in both the mother and daughter.

Why did IHC appear normal? The variant produced a truncated protein that was functionally defective but retained the antigenic domain recognized by the antibody. The stain detected the retained epitope but did not reveal that the protein was truncated and nonfunctional.

## Lesson 2: possible broader endocrine phenotype

We usually associate Lynch syndrome with colorectal and endometrial carcinomas. This mother–daughter pair suggests that an `MSH6` pathogenic variant may be associated with tumors across both the adrenal cortex and medulla, expanding the phenotype that clinicians may need to consider.

## Lesson 3: bidirectional vigilance

In patients with atypical presentations, bilateral tumors, or familial clustering, this case supports considering germline sequencing rather than relying only on IHC. Conversely, in a patient with known Lynch syndrome, the speaker proposed heightened attention to adrenal tumors. This surveillance proposal reflects the speaker's conclusion from this rare family and is not presented here as an established consensus guideline.

This family reveals a potential blind spot in diagnostic surveillance. The next time you see a peculiar adrenal presentation, remember the apparent reassurance of a retained IHC stain. Do not just look at the slide; read the genetic script. In endocrinology, a missed connection is not only a missed diagnosis—it may affect another generation.

Thank you. Any questions?

## Audience Q&A

**Audience member:** In people with Lynch syndrome, what is the incidence of adrenal tumors like these? Conversely, among people with adrenal tumors, what is the incidence of Lynch syndrome?

**Radhika Mathur:** I do not know the latter exact number. A prospective University of Michigan study published in 2013 evaluated approximately 100 patients with ACC and reported Lynch syndrome in roughly 2%–4%.

**Editorial note:** The primary publication evaluated 114 patients with ACC; 94 received genetic counseling, and 3.2% had family histories suggestive of Lynch syndrome with germline mismatch-repair variants.

**Audience member:** If you had to identify one high-impact message from this case, what would you want colleagues to take home about this rare syndrome?

**Radhika Mathur:** Two things. Whenever you see a rare presentation—such as familial clustering, bilateral tumors, or tumors involving the cortex and medulla—consider germline sequencing rather than relying only on IHC staining. Conversely, adrenal glands are not currently a routine part of Lynch syndrome surveillance, and this case raises the question of whether adrenal evaluation should be considered in the diagnostic approach.

Thank you very much.
