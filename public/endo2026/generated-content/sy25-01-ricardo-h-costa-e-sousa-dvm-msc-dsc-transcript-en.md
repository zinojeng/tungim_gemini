**Moderator:** Good morning. Welcome to Chicago and the Endocrine Society meeting in 2026. We have a symposium on thyroid hormone and the brain. Our first speaker is from Boston University. I would like to welcome Ricardo Costa e Sousa, who will talk about neural regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis.

**Ricardo Costa e Sousa:** Thank you. Good morning, everyone. I would like to thank the organizers and the Endocrine Society for the opportunity to share our ideas and some of our results. Today I will discuss the neural regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid, or HPT, axis. I have no conflicts of interest.

The key topics are the interaction between the melanocortin system and the HPT axis, with a focus on arcuate agouti-related peptide, or AgRP, neurons and melanocortin-4 receptor-positive neurons in the paraventricular nucleus, or PVN. I will then discuss thyroid-hormone signaling in the PVN, focusing on the role of thyroid-hormone receptor beta, or THRB, in HPT-axis regulation.

### The melanocortin system and hypophysiotropic TRH neurons

There are extensive data connecting the melanocortin system and the HPT axis. Terminals containing alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, or alpha-MSH, and terminals containing AgRP are in close association with thyrotropin-releasing hormone, or TRH, neurons in the PVN, suggesting a functional connection.

Fasting or negative energy balance suppresses TRH transcription. The in situ hybridization data shown here demonstrate reduced TRH mRNA after fasting, and alpha-MSH can reverse that reduction. Thus, alpha-MSH terminals lie close to TRH neurons and alpha-MSH can regulate TRH transcription.

TRH neurons also express MC4R, which is activated by alpha-MSH; AgRP acts as an inverse agonist. A luciferase assay using the TRH promoter shows that alpha-MSH can, at least in vitro, directly regulate TRH transcription. These morphological and in vitro data support the idea that the melanocortin system could regulate the HPT axis through TRH neurons.

We examined the physiological relevance using chemogenetics. Fasting suppresses the HPT axis, producing decreases in both TSH and T4. If we keep TRH neurons activated during fasting, that response is disrupted and the HPT axis remains active. In other words, preventing the normal inhibition of TRH neurons prevents the fasting-induced endocrine suppression. This demonstrates that TRH neurons are critical for the fasting response rather than being only a marker of it.

We then used chemogenetics to inhibit AgRP neurons in the arcuate nucleus. Inhibiting those neurons also disrupted fasting-induced suppression: TSH and T4 remained near their baseline levels. The chemogenetic experiment does not, by itself, prove a direct projection from arcuate AgRP neurons to PVN TRH neurons, but the result is consistent with the morphological evidence showing AgRP terminals near TRH neurons. A synaptic connection therefore remains an inference rather than a direct demonstration in this experiment. Together, arcuate AgRP neurons and PVN TRH neurons regulate the HPT-axis response to fasting and weight loss, in a circuit conceptually similar to those controlling appetite and food intake.

It remained unclear how extensively MC4R is expressed by TRH neurons. Earlier studies showed some MC4R-positive TRH neurons, but we wanted to quantify the overlap and begin to understand how melanocortin and thyroid-hormone signals might be integrated. We therefore examined MC4R and THRB expression in TRH neurons across the rostral, intermediate, and caudal PVN.

The first group comprised TRH neurons alone; the other groups comprised TRH neurons expressing both THRB and MC4R, THRB alone, or MC4R alone. In the rostral and caudal PVN, most TRH neurons did not express either receptor. Those regions contain multiple TRH-neuron populations, some of which may regulate sympathetic output or motivated behavior. In the intermediate PVN, most TRH neurons expressed one or both receptors. Neuroendocrine TRH neurons are thought to reside mainly in this intermediate region, suggesting that the receptor-positive cells may be hypophysiotropic. Even so, only a small fraction of all TRH neurons expressed MC4R; the slide-based quantification indicated approximately 20%.

### Activating PVN MC4R neurons stimulates the HPT axis

To determine whether the MC4R-positive cells were hypophysiotropic, we chemogenetically activated MC4R-positive neurons in the PVN. The experimental images confirmed the injection site. We performed the experiment in both ad libitum-fed and fasted animals.

Soon after administering clozapine-N-oxide, or CNO, and activating the MC4R-positive neurons, TSH rose rapidly. The slide data showed an approximately fourfold increase in fed animals and a 6.7-fold increase in fasted animals. A later increase in T4 followed. This is a simple but striking result: the hypothalamus and PVN are small structures, and the relevant MC4R-positive TRH population is very small, yet activating those few neurons activated the entire endocrine axis.

These findings confirm that at least some MC4R-positive TRH neurons are hypophysiotropic and regulate TSH secretion. We continue to study their roles in other physiological conditions.

### THRB and negative feedback in the PVN

I will now move to thyroid-hormone signaling. Patients with pathogenic THRB variants can have resistance to thyroid hormone beta, including central resistance to T3. Despite receiving T3, TSH does not decline as expected. Experimental THRB-knockout data support the same concept: mice have higher T4, T3, and TSH, as well as an enlarged thyroid gland. Human and mouse evidence therefore indicates that THRB contributes to negative feedback within the HPT axis.

T3 suppresses TRH expression. In classic experiments, implantation of a T3 pellet in the hypothalamus suppressed TRH mRNA and protein on the treated side while the contralateral side remained normal. An in vitro assay using the human TRH promoter showed that, in the presence of TR beta, T3 regulated TRH transcription; the same effect was not seen with TR alpha. These data supported a direct TR beta-mediated effect at the TRH promoter.

Different THRB isoforms exist. THRB2-knockout mice have increased TRH expression that is not normally suppressed by T3. This led to the prevailing model that T3 directly regulates TRH transcription through THRB2.

We therefore examined THRB1 and THRB2 expression in the PVN. THRB2 is often described as highly expressed in the hypothalamus, pituitary, inner ear, and retina. We used two approaches: isoform-specific in situ hybridization and Thrb1-Cre or Thrb2-Cre lines receiving an AAV Cre-dependent mCherry reporter. To our surprise, THRB1 was much more abundant than THRB2 in the PVN, and the same pattern appeared in other hypothalamic areas. A small population expressing THRB2 could still be functionally important, but by abundance THRB1 appears more likely to be broadly relevant to PVN neurons.

### Adult and developmental THRB deletion

We next asked whether THRB1 or THRB2 directly regulates TRH transcription. We used Thrb-floxed mice and injected an AAV encoding Cre recombinase into the PVN, creating an adult-onset local knockout. GFP confirmed the injection site, and an in situ hybridization probe for the floxed Thrb exon showed that THRB expression was almost completely absent in the targeted PVN. The probe detects both THRB1 and THRB2, so the approach disrupted both isoforms.

Despite the efficient knockout, TRH mRNA did not differ between knockout and control animals. We also saw no change in TSH or the other measurements. This was unexpected. We anticipated that disrupting THRB would impair negative feedback, raise TRH expression, and possibly raise TSH or TSH bioactivity and, subsequently, thyroid-hormone levels.

We considered whether THRB signaling during development might establish the set point for negative feedback. To test this, we crossed Thrb-floxed mice with a Sim1-iCre line, deleting Thrb from the PVN during embryonic development. In situ hybridization again confirmed loss of Thrb mRNA. Yet TRH expression was unchanged relative to controls, and there was no change in TSH, T4, food intake, or body mass over the observation period. We were excited and concerned because this result was completely unexpected. We wondered whether compensatory physiology under euthyroid conditions might conceal a difference, so we challenged the HPT axis.

### Hypothyroid challenge reveals regulation of TSH secretion

We made mice hypothyroid with a low-iodine diet containing PTU and then administered T3 during the final days. The T3 dose was 1 microgram per 100 grams of body weight daily and brought TSH close to the euthyroid range rather than making the mice overtly hyperthyroid.

Both controls and Sim1-driven THRB knockouts showed a normal TRH transcriptional response. Hypothyroidism increased TRH mRNA, and T3 replacement suppressed it similarly in both groups. Thus, we still found no evidence that THRB in the PVN directly regulates TRH transcription.

The TSH result was different. Whether the four groups were displayed separately—control hypothyroid, control hypothyroid plus T3, knockout hypothyroid, and knockout hypothyroid plus T3—or combined before treatment, the knockout mice had higher TSH. Despite unchanged TRH transcription, loss of THRB signaling in the PVN increased TSH. This suggests that THRB signaling regulates TRH neurons through another mechanism, perhaps by controlling TRH release at the median eminence.

Our first thought was that THRB might regulate excitability and firing rate. In collaboration with Joseph Madara and Zongfan Yang at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, we examined the effect of T3 on TRH-neuron firing. We labeled TRH neurons using an AAV carrying Cre-dependent mCherry in Trh-IRES-Cre mice. Animals were divided into euthyroid, hypothyroid, and hypothyroid-plus-T3-replacement groups. The electrophysiology included more than 30 neurons per group from four to five mice.

TRH neurons from hypothyroid mice had an increased firing rate. Restoring T3 suppressed that increase. Our current hypothesis is therefore that THRB signaling in the PVN has no major direct role in regulating TRH transcription but may mediate the effect of T3 on TRH-neuron excitability. We will test that directly in the knockout models.

### Conclusions

The small population of MC4R-positive TRH neurons is hypophysiotropic. Few TRH neurons co-express THRB and MC4R, suggesting that hypophysiotropic TRH neurons may include distinct subpopulations, some responsive to metabolic signals and others to thyroid-hormone signaling.

THRB1 is more abundant than THRB2 in the PVN. We found no evidence that THRB directly regulates TRH transcription in the adult or developing PVN. However, T3/THRB signaling in the PVN regulates TSH secretion, and T3 suppresses the firing rate of TRH neurons. Regulation of neuronal activity and TRH release may therefore be an important negative-feedback mechanism.

I want to thank everyone who collaborated on this work, especially Juliana Romao and Angela Zhang, who performed many of these experiments, as well as Joseph Madara, Zongfan Yang, Anthony Hollenberg, Fredric Wondisford, Bradford Lowell, and the other collaborators and trainees listed in the acknowledgments.
