## 00:03:17–00:10:40 — In Memoriam: foundations of nuclear-receptor biology

We have lost several giants in the field during the past year. Reviewing their work and contributions makes it very clear that we all use their findings in our introductory slides and in the first paragraphs of our grants. They established many of the fundamental principles of nuclear receptors and signaling.

### Bert O'Malley — steroid receptors as transcription factors

First, Dr. Bert O'Malley. During Donald McDonnell's talk on Sunday morning, he presented the concept that O'Malley and colleagues were the first to take a steroid hormone—estrogen in this case—treat chick oviduct with that hormone, and demonstrate that RNA output was among the earliest measurable changes.

One of the first outputs was production of nascent messenger RNA, and this preceded changes in protein. This established the concept that steroid hormones, by binding their receptors, lead to transcriptional changes in the cell and brought forward the paradigm of nuclear receptors as transcription factors.

Dr. O'Malley was also the first to clone a steroid-receptor coactivator. Steroid receptors do not themselves have intrinsic transcriptional activity. They require recruitment of coregulators to bridge to the RNA polymerase II complex and achieve active gene transcription.

Dr. O'Malley was also the first to demonstrate that steroid receptors could achieve ligand-independent activity. This is important in cancer biology, where we inhibit androgen-receptor and estrogen-receptor activity in prostate and breast cancer, but signal-transduction cascades can overcome that inhibition, produce ligand-independent receptor activity, and drive endocrine resistance.

He spent many years demonstrating that coregulators are master regulators of normal biology and of pathological states such as cancer.

### Pierre Chambon — modular receptor domains and activation functions

Pierre Chambon had an established career in transcriptional regulation before entering steroid-receptor biology. He was the first to clone the estrogen receptor. By comparing the receptor's structure and sequence with the previously cloned glucocorticoid receptor, he proposed the A-through-F domain architecture that we now understand and appreciate across the nuclear-receptor family.

The C domain encodes the DNA-binding domain and zinc fingers. The E/F region encodes the ligand-binding domain responsible for ligand binding. Dr. Chambon also cloned the retinoic acid receptor and demonstrated that a vitamin A derivative was its ligand, and that the retinoic acid receptor and estrogen receptor signal by the same general mechanism: bind ligand, bind DNA, and activate transcription.

He also demonstrated that most nuclear receptors contain two transcriptional activation functions, AF-1 and AF-2.

### Étienne-Émile Baulieu — mifepristone, DHEA-S, and neurosteroids

Étienne-Émile Baulieu is known as the father of mifepristone. Progesterone, through the progesterone receptor, affects the uterine endometrium, uterine growth, and gene expression. By searching for antagonists of that action, he identified RU-486, or mifepristone. This is an important drug for interruption of pregnancy through its antiprogestational effects.

Dr. Baulieu also identified dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S), an adrenal androgen, and demonstrated that DHEA-S levels decrease with age in men and women. He proposed that reintroduction or supplementation might improve some age-related effects. He performed a clinical trial of DHEA-S in men and women and reported physiological improvements in measures such as skin quality and bone density, with particularly pronounced effects in women over age 70.

By searching for non-adrenal mechanisms of DHEA-S synthesis, he also demonstrated the synthesis of neurosteroids such as allopregnanolone and pregnenolone sulfate, which can act on several receptors in the brain.

### David Mangelsdorf — reverse endocrinology and metabolic sensing

An entire session was dedicated to the contributions and legacy of Dr. David Mangelsdorf, who pioneered the concept of **reverse endocrinology**. Before his work, known hormones were used to hunt for their receptors. He and his colleagues used structural and sequence knowledge to clone orphan nuclear receptors—proteins with nuclear-receptor architecture but no known ligands—and then used those receptors to fish for ligands.

This established the paradigm that nuclear receptors can act as sensors for non-classical signaling molecules. Dr. Mangelsdorf and colleagues demonstrated that liver X receptor (LXR) is a receptor for oxysterols and that farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a receptor for bile acids, establishing bile acids as active signaling molecules. Before that, they were considered simply detergents that helped us get through a Chicago deep-dish pizza or a juicy steak.

He also demonstrated that pregnane X receptor (PXR) has a highly flexible ligand-binding pocket. That pocket can engage many ligands and environmental stimuli, leading to upregulation of cytochrome P450 enzymes—particularly CYP3A4—to support detoxification.

Most recently, his work demonstrated that a subclass of fibroblast growth factors, FGF19 and FGF21, can be activated by nuclear-receptor signaling in the liver, travel to distant sites, and exert endocrine effects.

This serves as a very nice introduction to nuclear-receptor signaling and to some of the mechanisms that interest us.

## 00:10:40–00:20:02 — Best Translational Feature: recent phase 3 trials

I now want to move into the awards portion of the presentation, starting with a new category: **Best Translational Feature**. In a group of basic scientists, we do not usually focus on clinical trials, but I want to review several recent, high-impact trials that illustrate the culmination of our work: dissecting basic mechanisms, identifying pathological targets, developing ligands that act on those targets, and achieving benefits for patients.

### FINEARTS-HF — finerenone and the mineralocorticoid receptor

The first nominee is finerenone and the FINEARTS-HF trial. The trial population consisted of patients with heart failure and mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction. Their hearts still pump, but they have heart failure associated with myocardial inflammation and fibrosis. This subgroup has lacked a good standard-of-care therapy, unlike patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, for whom there is a defined treatment protocol.

FINEARTS-HF addressed this unmet need by targeting the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR). MR is activated by aldosterone. When it translocates to the nucleus, it drives a gene-expression program that promotes fibrosis and inflammation, making it an important inhibitory target.

Spironolactone is used in heart-failure patients, but not for this specific context because it has not provided benefit. In the phase 3 FINEARTS-HF trial, finerenone reduced total worsening heart-failure events and cardiovascular death compared with placebo. This was a positive phase 3 trial from about a year and a half ago.

### MAESTRO-NASH — resmetirom and thyroid hormone receptor beta

Another nominee is resmetirom, developed for nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), now termed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Fatty deposits in the liver lead to hepatocyte injury, inflammation, and fibrotic scarring. NASH/MASH can progress to cirrhosis and liver failure and is a significant cause of death worldwide.

The nuclear-receptor target is thyroid hormone receptor beta (THR-β). In this case, we want to activate the receptor because THR-β activation induces lipid catabolism, improves mitochondrial function, promotes bile-acid metabolism, and is known to improve steatohepatitis.

In the phase 3 MAESTRO-NASH trial, low- and high-dose resmetirom resolved NASH without worsening fibrosis compared with placebo. In a subset of patients, it also improved fibrosis by at least one stage without worsening the NAFLD activity score. Resmetirom is now approved for use in patients with NASH/MASH.

### VERITAC-2 — vepdegestrant as an ER-targeting PROTAC

Vepdegestrant is a new proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) directed at the estrogen receptor (ER) and developed for advanced breast cancer. We had an entire session yesterday on the PROTAC revolution.

Vepdegestrant is a bifunctional molecule that engages cereblon as an E3 ligase and brings it into proximity with ER, targeting the ER protein for degradation. One problem in breast cancer is that resistance to standard endocrine therapies can occur through mutations in the estrogen receptor gene, **ESR1**, particularly **D538G** and **Y537S**. These mutations make ER constitutively active and resistant to ligands such as tamoxifen.

In the overall intention-to-treat population, vepdegestrant did not have a significant progression-free-survival benefit relative to fulvestrant, an ER degrader. In the population with ESR1 mutations, however, vepdegestrant provided the patient benefit. This is now an approved therapy for use in breast cancer.

### ROSELLA — relacorilant and glucocorticoid-receptor antagonism

The ROSELLA trial evaluated a glucocorticoid-receptor antagonist in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. Suzanne Conzen and colleagues established that upregulation of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) in ovarian-cancer cells protects them from chemotherapy-mediated apoptosis. You therefore want to reduce GR activity to overcome this problem.

Relacorilant is a GR antagonist developed for this purpose. In the platinum-resistant ovarian-cancer population, relacorilant plus nab-paclitaxel provided an overall-survival benefit compared with nab-paclitaxel alone. We now have a new GR antagonist for this setting.

### PROTEUS — perioperative apalutamide in high-risk prostate cancer

Most recently, the PROTEUS trial tested apalutamide, an androgen-receptor (AR) antagonist, as perioperative therapy for prostate cancer.

We have heard several talks at this meeting about prostate-cancer progression. The standard of care for localized prostate cancer is surgery or radiation. That is curative for many patients, but disease can recur, particularly in high-risk prostate cancer.

At recurrence, we begin targeting the androgen-receptor signaling axis by reducing ligand levels or using AR antagonists such as enzalutamide, darolutamide, and apalutamide. When these fail, patients move on to chemotherapy-based regimens.

PROTEUS tested whether adding apalutamide before surgery and for a short period after surgery—a neoadjuvant followed by adjuvant treatment approach—would improve outcomes in high-risk localized prostate cancer. Androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) alone had been tested previously without success, so the control arm was ADT plus placebo.

With ADT plus apalutamide, almost 10% of patients demonstrated a pathological complete response or minimal residual disease at surgery. There was also an improvement in metastasis-free survival in the combination arm. Metastasis-free survival in prostate cancer is a surrogate for overall survival. This is going to change practice in the prostate-cancer field, with patients undergoing surgery for high-risk disease beginning perioperative apalutamide treatment.

### And the winner is the field

When deciding on the winner in this category, I wanted to demonstrate the population-level impact of these trials. In FINEARTS-HF, the global population eligible for this therapy is about 4.5 million. The endpoint gain is modest, but multiplying that gain across the population produces many person-years of life gained.

The same applies to MAESTRO-NASH: about 6 million people are at risk for cirrhosis and liver failure, and a 1.2-month endpoint gain corresponds to approximately 600,000 person-years without liver decompensation.

For PROTEUS, the endpoint was metastasis-free survival, so we do not yet know the overall-survival benefit, but the estimated impact is about 350,000 person-years without metastasis. The VERITAC-2 ER-PROTAC trial corresponds to roughly 43,000 person-years without disease progression, and ROSELLA with the GR antagonist corresponds to more than 44,000 person-years of life.

This is all from approximately the past year and a half. I think it demonstrates that the winner is the endocrine nuclear-receptor field, because we can all take credit for these advances.

## 00:20:02–00:22:26 — Live Action Short Film: nuclear-receptor stoichiometry

The Oscars are usually about a four-hour show. I only have 45 minutes. Last night, after the presidential reception, we held another awards show. I do not know whether you were there, but every person in this room had their most recent papers highlighted. Everyone clapped. People were crying in the audience about the glory of your work. Trust me, your work was highlighted.

Today we only have time for a high-level overview of some category winners. We will start with **Live Action Short Film**.

I want to highlight work by Liza Dahal, Xavier Darzacq, and colleagues that used live-cell single-molecule imaging to ask an interesting question about nuclear-receptor stoichiometry. Several nuclear receptors heterodimerize with retinoid X receptor (RXR) on DNA to activate transcription. For regulation, does upregulation of the nuclear receptor or upregulation of RXR increase the number of chromatin-engaged transcription factors?

The live-action filming techniques were single-molecule tracking (SMT) and proximity-assisted photoactivation (PAPA). At baseline, the investigators established the bound and free fractions of RXR partners and RXR, using retinoic acid receptor alpha (RARα) and RXR as the model.

Increasing RXR relative to its partner RARα increased the free fraction of RXR but did not change the chromatin-bound fraction of active RARα–RXR heterodimers. Increasing RARα, in contrast, produced a significant increase in chromatin-bound RARα–RXR molecules.

This establishes that upregulating an individual RXR-partner nuclear receptor can increase the amount of chromatin-bound nuclear-receptor–RXR heterodimer.

## 00:22:26–00:25:18 — Animated Short Film: PPARγ conformational ensembles

For **Animated Short Film**, I appreciated a paper by Brian MacTavish, Douglas Kojetin, and colleagues that demonstrated conformational ensembles for repressive ligand-engaged peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) complexes.

PPARγ can adopt active and repressive conformations. Some PPARγ antagonists do not simply inhibit receptor activity; they promote an active transcriptional-repression complex. These are inverse agonists. When they bind PPARγ, they produce active gene repression. This differs from the neutral antagonists available for most nuclear receptors, which block receptor activity as competitive antagonists.

Structurally, we know little about the repressive poses of nuclear receptors. The goal of this study was to develop an ensemble of structures representing the repressed states of PPARγ.

The investigators used nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), labeling glycine 399 (G399). With G399 labeled, they could resolve active and repressive conformations. For some ligands, the receptor in solution moved between the two conformations. That was the proof of principle.

The tour de force was development of a series of ligands based on the same chemical scaffold that achieved exquisite control over PPARγ. Across the series, binding to nuclear receptor corepressor 1 (NCoR1) decreased as the molecules moved from the most repressive toward the most active, whereas binding to the coactivator MED1 increased. A transcriptional assay likewise showed a continuum from repression to activation.

In the NMR assay, receptors bound to the most strongly repressive inverse agonists spent most of their time in the repressive state. Across the ligand series, they spent progressively less time in that state and more time in the active state.

This provides a beautiful ensemble of PPARγ structures for understanding the structure–function basis of nuclear-receptor repression.

## 00:25:18–00:27:07 — Special Effects: AR monomers and dimers

Let us move on to **Special Effects**. I will not spend too much time on this because Dr. Donald McDonnell presented it during his plenary session on Sunday.

This is a new concept in prostate cancer in which the androgen receptor may exist in monomeric and dimeric forms. The dimeric form is in the nucleus, whereas the monomeric form is in the cytoplasm. This was discovered by studying the response of prostate-cancer cells to increasing androgen doses.

In LNCaP and VCaP cells, as in most prostate-cancer cell lines, there is a biphasic growth response. There is a “sweet spot” of synthetic androgen—R1881 in this case—at which cells achieve maximal proliferation. As androgen levels increase further, an antiproliferative effect emerges.

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is probably the best-characterized transcriptional target of AR, and it is induced at the highest androgen doses. Cell-cycle and growth-regulatory gene expression, however, is activated at the lower doses associated with maximal proliferation.

The model is that nuclear AR functions in its classical dimeric form, binding DNA and engaging coregulatory molecules. Many genes activated by AR in this context promote differentiation or inhibit tumors. A monomeric form of AR in the cytoplasm can activate the mTORC1 signaling pathway and promote proliferation.

This creates an opportunity to develop AR ligands that promote the nuclear dimeric complex while repressing the cytoplasmic monomeric complex, potentially improving outcomes in prostate cancer.

## 00:27:07–00:28:48 — Visual Effects: astrocyte GR and visual-system plasticity

For **Visual Effects**, I selected a study by Bruno Gegenhuber, Michael Greenberg, and colleagues investigating the role of the glucocorticoid receptor in neuronal plasticity.

The study used mice to examine development of the visual system. Mouse eyes open around postnatal day 14. After the eyes open, light signals drive development of the ocular system. The investigators reared some mice in darkness, so light did not reach the eyes, and reared others normally. They then performed multi-omic profiling to understand differences in gene expression.

Astrocytes showed extensive differential gene expression between dark-reared and normally reared animals. I found this fascinating: light reaching the eyes produced large-scale activation and chromatin engagement of the glucocorticoid receptor. In the dark-reared mice, this chromatin engagement was absent.

GR engagement was required for maturation of astrocytes and of the visual cortex. Even more strikingly, after development had occurred, deleting GR in mature astrocytes reversed the state and made the astrocytes plastic again. This is a reversible process controlled by the glucocorticoid receptor.

## 00:28:48–00:33:06 — Adapted Screenplay: AR amplification on ecDNA

When you make me do work like this, I am going to spend a little time presenting my own work. For **Adapted Screenplay**, I will discuss a new mechanism of **AR gene amplification** in prostate cancer.

Several years ago, we discovered that, in certain advanced prostate cancers with AR amplification, amplified AR does not reside on the chromosomes. It occurs on extrachromosomal circular DNA (ecDNA). We have 48 nuclear receptors, and I think AR is the only one known to do this. I am not aware of another nuclear receptor amplified on ecDNA.

This mechanism increases AR copy number, but ecDNA molecules are structurally unstable. Prostate-cancer cells can therefore accumulate not only more copies of AR but more AR gene rearrangements and greater diversity of AR proteins, including variants such as AR-V7.

We have collaborated with multiple groups to understand the importance of this process. Felix Feng is another giant in the field whom we lost this past year. Felix was a radiation oncologist and translational scientist in prostate cancer. He led the West Coast Stand Up To Cancer study, which performed extensive profiling of metastatic prostate-cancer biopsies.

In a collaborative study, Felix and colleagues examined 80 castration-resistant prostate cancers. Each row on the slide is a different tumor. They performed Hi-C multi-omic profiling and whole-genome sequencing.

We examined contact-frequency maps between an upstream AR enhancer and the AR transcription start site (TSS). Normally, the enhancer communicates with the TSS to upregulate AR. In approximately two-thirds of these advanced prostate cancers, that interaction occurred normally.

In approximately one-third of the castration-resistant tumors, however, there was essentially no contact between that enhancer and promoter because the chromatin architecture was completely different. When whole-genome sequencing data were analyzed with **AmpliconArchitect**, these tumors were predicted to have AR amplification on extrachromosomal circular DNA. This may occur in up to one-third of castration-resistant prostate cancers.

Tumors with normal regional contact frequency—those thought to lack AR ecDNA—showed an overall-survival difference when patients subsequently received an AR-signaling inhibitor such as abiraterone or enzalutamide, compared with patients who did not. This appears to be the population that responds to AR-targeted therapy.

Among patients with low regional contact frequency, predicted to have AR ecDNA, there was little difference in overall survival whether or not they received an AR-signaling inhibitor. AR ecDNA may therefore represent a mechanism of de novo resistance.

We have also studied this in cell-free DNA and liquid biopsy. We collaborated with investigators who conducted the phase 3 **Alliance A031201** trial of enzalutamide versus enzalutamide plus abiraterone. We profiled plasma at baseline and at progression.

The presence of an AR ecDNA signature at baseline was associated with an approximately twofold higher risk of disease progression and death. In a liquid-biopsy assay measuring about 70 genes important in advanced prostate cancer, the AR ecDNA signature was the largest change observed between baseline and progression.

Among 167 patients with detectable circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) at both baseline and progression, the frequency rose from approximately 10%–11% at baseline to almost 30% at progression. This is a newly recognized mechanism of AR plasticity.

## 00:33:06–00:37:42 — Red Carpet: fasting and weight loss

Now that the award show is over, I want to highlight scenes from the red carpet. This year's hot trend is fasting and weight loss. These mice have apparently gained access to the secret—or magic—sauce of fasting or Ozempic. I want to highlight two papers that bring nuclear receptors into this field.

### Quintuple agonism for obesity and diabetes

We have heard a great deal about glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonists, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor (GIPR) agonists, and dual agonism. A recently published paper demonstrated that quintuple agonism is the way to go: target five receptors.

The striking feature is that one molecule achieves all five activities. A GLP-1R/GIPR dual agonist was conjugated to lanifibranor, a PPARα/γ/δ pan-agonist.

Agonizing these PPAR receptors can have dramatic antidiabetic effects, but lanifibranor has toxicities. Conjugating it to a GLP-1/GIP peptide directs it preferentially to cells expressing GLP-1 receptors, allowing similar effects with much lower lanifibranor exposure.

In mice, compared with the GLP-1R/GIPR agonist alone, conjugation to lanifibranor produced greater weight loss, a larger reduction in fat mass, changes in lean mass, lower food intake, and improved blood glucose.

### Fasting, tamoxifen, and glucocorticoid activation

Wilbert Zwart was invited to speak at this meeting but unfortunately could not attend. I wanted to highlight work from his laboratory demonstrating a benefit of fasting in breast-cancer therapy.

The investigators grew MCF-7 cells as xenografts in mice and treated the mice with tamoxifen. A subset also underwent an intermittent-fasting protocol of 48 hours at a time.

Control tumors grew with their usual kinetics. Tamoxifen and fasting alone had similar antitumor effects on the MCF-7 xenografts, whereas tamoxifen plus fasting had a much greater effect.

Mechanistically, tamoxifen inhibits the estrogen receptor. Like AR, ER can engage genomic sites that are protumorigenic and sites that are tumor suppressive. Tamoxifen alone inhibits ER activity at both groups of sites. This produces antiproliferative and antitumor effects, but it also reduces expression of tumor-suppressor genes.

In mice undergoing intermittent fasting, one of the largest chromatin changes was opening of sites containing progesterone-receptor (PR) and glucocorticoid-receptor (GR) binding motifs. GR and PR then occupied genomic sites where ER would otherwise have tumor-suppressive effects.

By using tamoxifen to inhibit ER-driven cell proliferation and protumorigenic effects, while also using fasting or glucocorticoid activation, the investigators could prevent repression and maintain expression of these tumor-suppressive target genes.

This was demonstrated in a related experiment comparing control mice with mice treated with tamoxifen or dexamethasone alone. Tamoxifen plus dexamethasone produced effects similar to the tamoxifen-plus-fasting paradigm.
