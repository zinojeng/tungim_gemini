## Case 1: Acromegaly and postoperative polyuria

I will start with a case presentation of a 54-year-old woman who presented with newly diagnosed acromegaly. She had all the typical features that we usually see in patients with acromegaly: increased shoe size, rings that no longer fit, prognathism with an underbite, headaches, sweating, macroglossia, and carpal tunnel symptoms. There was a family history of colon cancer in her father.

Her IGF-1 level was 4.4 times the upper limit of normal, and her growth hormone level was elevated at 28. She had normal adrenal and thyroid function—presumed normal pituitary function—but we do not really know what her gonadotropins were.

On the MRI, I can see a very large lesion consistent with a tumor in the sella, filling and expanding the sella and extending into the suprasellar cistern. It is probably invading the right cavernous sinus and maybe the superior part of the left cavernous sinus. I can see normal pituitary tissue over on the left side, displaced laterally. That probably explains how she was able to have normal function despite this large tumor.

She underwent transsphenoidal surgery with medial cavernous sinus wall exploration, presumably on the right side, to try to remove as much tumor as possible.

Eight hours postoperatively, the nurses reported a marked increase in urine output. Over the next 2 hours, she produced 1,850 mL of dilute, colorless urine. She had intense thirst and was drinking water ad libitum. Her blood pressure was 108/68 mmHg, compared with a baseline of 122/76 mmHg, and her heart rate was 96 beats/min. She was mildly volume-depleted, with dry mucous membranes.

Her laboratory results were as follows:

- Serum sodium: 148 mEq/L
- Serum osmolality: 303 mOsm/kg
- Urine osmolality: 155 mOsm/kg
- Urine specific gravity: 1.001
- Urine sodium: 12 mEq/L
- Serum potassium: 3.9 mEq/L
- BUN: 22 mg/dL
- Creatinine: 0.82 mg/dL

The elevated serum sodium and serum osmolality, together with the low urine osmolality and urine specific gravity, support hypotonic polyuria rather than hyperglycemia as the explanation in this setting. Her serum potassium was normal. The BUN-to-creatinine ratio suggested that she was probably a little prerenal.

A number of questions—hopefully questions that your interns, residents, neurosurgery colleagues, and endocrine fellows would ask—are important here. How much fluid was administered during surgery? What dose of glucocorticoids did she receive in the operating room and postoperatively? How much had she been drinking since surgery? How awake was she, and what access did she have to fluids? We will discuss those points in a moment.

In this patient, desmopressin 0.5 µg was administered subcutaneously once.

## Diagnosing AVP-D after pituitary surgery

When evaluating patients who have polyuria, polydipsia, and thirst after surgery, we obviously think about arginine vasopressin deficiency, or AVP-D, as well as other possibilities that we will discuss in a moment.

Usually, postoperative AVP-D occurs within the first 24 to 48 hours after surgery. You may see it immediately in a patient with a large tumor or craniopharyngioma when the surgeons know that they sectioned the pituitary stalk.

Urine output is usually greater than 250–350 mL/h for 2 consecutive hours. It is unusual to see significant hypovolemia in an alert patient with a preserved thirst mechanism, because people drink in response to thirst and defend themselves against dehydration.

Typically, the serum sodium will be normal, although it may be in the 142–144 mEq/L range. Some people will have elevated serum sodium if they fall behind on fluid intake, are obtunded after surgery, do not have access to water, or lack the motivation to drink. Plasma osmolality may be normal or elevated, whereas urine is usually quite dilute. Urine osmolality may be less than 100 mOsm/kg or lower than the plasma osmolality.

It is important to observe patients carefully over a short period once this scenario presents itself. You need to consider the differential diagnosis and determine whether this truly looks like acute AVP-D before initiating treatment.

We are often asked whether copeptin should be checked postoperatively. The guideline group found no convincing evidence that copeptin levels should be routinely evaluated or used to guide clinical decision-making in this setting. There is a lot of research in this area, and you will find many papers, but generally you must make a clinical decision about treatment or observation before the laboratory result would return.

## Differential diagnosis of postoperative polyuria

Growth hormone and IGF-1 increase renal salt and water retention. You all know about the soft-tissue swelling seen in acromegaly. Successful surgery and the resulting reduction in growth hormone and IGF-1 usually produce natriuresis, followed by diuresis.

It is not unusual for a person with acromegaly to lose 7–10 pounds within the first 24 hours; that is usually all volume. Many patients have a normal ring size within the first 2 days of surgery, or at least a decrease by two ring sizes, in my experience. This is an important differential diagnosis consideration. These patients usually have a normal serum sodium; it will not be near the thirst threshold.

Anesthesiologists also may administer more intravenous fluid than is physiologically required. Excess intravenous fluid suppresses AVP secretion, which causes an aquaresis and physiologic polyuria. These patients also usually have a normal serum sodium.

Stress-dose glucocorticoids raise two additional considerations. First, they may cause hyperglycemia, leading to glucosuria and osmotic polyuria. Second, in someone with reduced AVP reserve who is still producing enough AVP to osmoregulate nearly normally, excess glucocorticoids can suppress AVP gene transcription and AVP production. This may unmask partial AVP-D immediately after surgery.

## Initial recommendation and audience response

The recommendation is that acute postoperative or post-traumatic AVP-D be treated with sufficient fluid intake and/or a single dose of desmopressin to maintain serum sodium within the normal range. If serum sodium is greater than 145 mmol/L, or if it is clearly rising even while still within the reference range—in the context of polyuria and polydipsia—desmopressin is indicated.

Let us look at this audience-response question. After one dose of desmopressin 0.5 µg subcutaneously, the serum sodium was 140 mEq/L and urine osmolality was 620 mOsm/kg. The patient was alert, drinking fluids comfortably, and had no nausea. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

**A.** Continue subcutaneous desmopressin every 6 hours on a fixed schedule, regardless of urine output or serum sodium, to prevent recurrence of polyuria overnight.

**B.** Monitor serum sodium every 6 hours and redose only when urine output is greater than 250–350 mL/h for 2 hours or the serum sodium rises.

**C.** Discontinue desmopressin and observe for 24 hours with no therapy, presuming this is transient AVP-D.

**D.** Switch to a continuous intravenous vasopressin infusion at 0.01 units/min for a predictable antidiuretic effect and then proceed to intermittent desmopressin dosing.

Eighty-five percent chose the correct response, option B. That is really good and impressive. Your clinical judgment is aligned with what we recommend.

## Treatment of AVP-D after pituitary surgery

For the treatment of AVP-D after pituitary surgery, we generally consider subcutaneous or intravenous desmopressin at a starting dose of 0.25–1.0 µg to be a reasonable first approach. I usually administer 0.5 µg subcutaneously. If a patient is hypotensive or in the ICU with decreased cutaneous blood flow, you might choose intravenous administration.

Repeat the dose if urine output is greater than 250–350 mL/h for at least 2 hours, with low urine osmolality or a urine specific gravity below 1.005. I usually tell residents to use the number 300 for 2 consecutive hours, or 600 mL in any 2-hour period. I do not want them splitting hairs if the output is 250 mL in one hour and 450 mL in the next.

You should also begin treatment if the patient cannot maintain the oral fluid intake you have advised, if urine output exceeds fluid intake and the patient is moving into a negative fluid balance, or if there is absolute hypernatremia.

We recommend that patients drink according to thirst. Confirm with the nursing staff that the patient has not inadvertently been placed on a fluid restriction. Make it clear that you do not want fluid restriction in this phase.

We recommend against an upfront standing prescription of continuous desmopressin unless persistent AVP-D is likely. In other words, we do not want the house staff to write a scheduled daily prescription. It should be a one-time order, followed by reassessment before another dose is prescribed. Do not write a PRN order, because that can be misinterpreted by people who do not fully understand the individual clinical course.

If a patient has a craniopharyngioma with known pituitary stalk transection, scheduling therapy may be reasonable. Even then, in the immediate postoperative period you should define the patient's actual needs and determine the dose based on several consecutive days of assessment.

## The triphasic response

One special pattern after surgery involving the hypothalamic-pituitary unit is the triphasic response, which almost all of you have probably encountered.

The first phase is AVP-D. It generally begins 12–48 hours after pituitary surgery or head trauma and may extend from the first day into the first week or two. It produces classic hypotonic polyuria and polydipsia, with serum sodium and plasma osmolality near or above the upper limit of normal. Patients often require desmopressin during this phase because the polyuria and polydipsia can be severe. This usually results from injury to the posterior pituitary, pituitary stalk, or axons; the neurons are in a state of shock and do not release AVP.

Neurons can degenerate retrogradely from the axon toward the cell body. As the injured axons degenerate, AVP may be released into the circulation in an uncontrolled fashion, without regulation by the osmoreceptor system. The patient then develops the second, antidiuretic phase—syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis, or SIAD. This may continue for 7–14 days and sometimes up to 3 weeks.

One important reason to avoid scheduling desmopressin early after pituitary surgery is that you do not know who will enter this second phase. You must reassess the need for each subsequent dose. The patient may no longer need therapy because the AVP-D was transient, or may be entering the SIAD phase, in which additional desmopressin could cause more severe and rapidly developing hyponatremia.

After the antidiuretic phase resolves, a third phase may occur. It is depicted here as recurrent AVP-D, with a return of diabetes insipidus as hypothalamic neuronal bodies degenerate. I have also seen a return to normal osmoregulation when enough neurons survive the insult. Once the non-osmotically released AVP has cleared and its effect is gone, those patients return to normal. Most patients who develop the complete triphasic response, however, enter a recurrent phase of AVP-D and require reassessment and retitration to find a dose that maintains good quality of life.

## Transient AVP-D and patient education

Keep in mind that postoperative AVP-D may be transient. Subcutaneous or intravenous desmopressin is usually preferred for initial hospital treatment. Intranasal desmopressin is not preferred immediately after transsphenoidal surgery because postoperative inflammation in the sinonasal passages may limit absorption.

Monitor fluid intake and urine output closely after the first dose. Establish a dose effect, then confirm a return of hypotonic polyuria and review the serum sodium before deciding whether a subsequent dose is required. Do not simply schedule repeated doses; prove that the patient still needs treatment.

By discharge, oral desmopressin is preferred. I have seen patients who need desmopressin for only 1 week, others for 3 months, and others for 6 months. A large proportion recover during the first few weeks, so remain alert to that possibility.

All patients should be instructed to incorporate regular **desmopressin escape**, or breakthrough aquaresis, into their treatment regimen to reduce the risk of hyponatremia. You do not want to stack doses and their clinical effects day after day without a breakthrough period. Otherwise, water accumulates because the patient has no opportunity to aquarese, increasing the risk of hyponatremia. This is particularly important after surgery, when AVP-D is likely to be transient.

## Case 2: Craniopharyngioma with adipsic AVP-D

The second case is a 24-year-old woman with a craniopharyngioma. The imaging shows a classic, very large, partially calcified sellar and suprasellar mass measuring 2.1 × 2.5 × 2.7 cm, with severe visual loss.

She had adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism and was started preoperatively on hydrocortisone and levothyroxine. She then developed increased thirst and urination, with a serum sodium of 142 mEq/L. She was not started on desmopressin preoperatively, with plans to start it postoperatively.

This is likely the situation in which steroid replacement suppresses the small amount of AVP the patient was producing and unmasks AVP-D. I have even seen patients present with hyponatremia due to hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency; after treatment with glucocorticoids, AVP-D is unmasked and the serum sodium rises rapidly. You need to be careful in that setting.

Urgent surgery was planned. On postoperative day 1, the serum sodium rose to 153 mEq/L. Serum osmolality was 309 mOsm/kg, while urine osmolality was only 68 mOsm/kg. Urine output over the preceding 2 hours was 780 mL/h. The highest output I have seen in my career is 1 L/h. For reference, plasma flow through the kidneys is approximately 35 L/h, so very large urine outputs can occur in complete AVP-D.

Her BUN was 34 mg/dL, suggesting prerenal volume depletion; hematocrit was 51%, consistent with hemoconcentration. Her weight had fallen by 3.2 kg, and her urine specific gravity was 1.001.

The nurses noted a striking finding: despite the marked rise in serum sodium, the patient had no thirst whatsoever. When offered water, she took only a few sips and stopped because she did not feel thirsty and had no motivation to drink. She had no nausea or altered consciousness, was neurologically intact, and felt well.

This patient has severe AVP-D together with adipsia. This is more likely when damage is more rostral—or anterior—involving the hypothalamus, pituitary stalk, lamina terminalis, and organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis. I see this as a spectrum: adipsic hypernatremia with mild, moderate, or severe AVP-D, depending on the patient and the disease process. A similar situation can occur after an anterior communicating artery aneurysm.

The slide-based treatment in this acute case was desmopressin 2 µg intravenously once.

## Management of adipsic AVP-D

We recommend that patients with adipsic AVP-D receive desmopressin together with a fixed, prescribed fluid intake. You must balance the water prescription against urine output and the desired target urine output.

For me, the prescription is usually about 30 mL/kg/day of water for most patients, adjusted for insensible losses and activity. Patients should weigh themselves daily because day-to-day weight changes usually reflect fluid balance. Track urine output and fluid intake, monitor body weight, and measure serum sodium regularly.

These patients are very difficult to manage, but it can be done.
