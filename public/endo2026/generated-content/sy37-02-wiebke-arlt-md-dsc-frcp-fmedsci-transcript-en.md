# Steroid Metabolomics for Detection and Classification of Adrenal Tumors

**Speaker:** Wiebke Arlt, MD, DSc, FRCP, FMedSci  
**Conference:** ENDO 2026  
**Session:** Adrenal Tumors: Assessing the Risk  
**Reviewed source interval:** 31:10–59:10

## Adrenal masses and the malignant steroid fingerprint

**Moderator:** It is now our honor to invite Dr. Wiebke Arlt to speak about steroid metabolomics for detection and classification of adrenal tumors.

**Wiebke Arlt:** Many thanks for having me. I will discuss targeted steroid metabolomics, rather than an untargeted global metabolome. Regarding disclosures, I am Chief Scientific Officer of the spinout company Intelligent Mass Spec Solutions Ltd.

Adrenal masses are mostly detected incidentally. There are currently 7.2 million CT scans performed annually in the United Kingdom and 93 million in the United States—93 million scans in 68 million unique patients. One in 20 scans detects an adrenal mass, and about one in 20 adrenal masses is cancerous. In large series, approximately 5% of patients are diagnosed with adrenocortical carcinoma, or ACC. This is a significant diagnostic problem. The key question is whether a mass is benign or malignant.

Imaging shows that the larger a tumor is, the more likely it is to be malignant. In the prospective EURINE-ACT study, most patients with adrenocortical adenoma, or ACA, had tumors below 4 cm, while most patients with ACC had tumors above 4 cm. A 4-cm cutoff achieved 98% sensitivity and almost 80% specificity. The problem is prevalence: ACCs are rare, while adenomas are very common. Consequently, there are more large adenomas than carcinomas, so size alone is insufficient.

Unenhanced CT attenuation also has limitations. EURINE-ACT showed that 20 Hounsfield units, or HU, is a useful cutoff; lowering it to 10 or 15 HU includes a very large number of benign tumors. Tumor size, unenhanced CT density, and routine biochemical parameters all have limited specificity and therefore a high false-positive rate.

We have systematically examined urine testing as the basis of a diagnostic-test strategy. Urine testing has a long history; when I trained in internal medicine, my trainer told me he had still been required to taste patients’ urine. We do not taste it. We apply mass spectrometry to profile the urinary steroid metabolome.

In healthy people, the most abundant urinary steroids are androgen and glucocorticoid metabolites. Androgen precursors, mineralocorticoid precursors, and glucocorticoid precursors are present in relatively low concentrations. Cedric Shackleton developed steroid profiling by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, or GC-MS, and kindly trained me in the technique.

Our 2011 proof-of-principle study included 102 patients with histologically proven benign adrenal adenoma and 45 patients with histologically proven ACC. Each patient collected 24-hour urine. We profiled 32 steroids by GC-MS and applied machine learning in collaboration with Michael Biehl, using generalized matrix relevance learning vector quantization, or GMLVQ. ACC had a distinct steroid profile. Rather than an abundance of steroid end products, ACC produced an accumulation of precursors that are not routinely measured in blood.

That fit our biologic hypothesis: a malignant adrenocortical cell is relatively immature, and its steroidogenesis may be arrested at precursor stages rather than producing fully processed end products. The model achieved an AUROC of 0.97, with approximately 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity.

A useful clinical test needs high throughput. GC-MS could process only about 20 samples per week. We therefore transferred the assay to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, LC-MS/MS, reduced the panel from 32 steroids to 15, and maintained similar diagnostic performance: an AUROC of 0.95 with approximately 90% sensitivity and specificity.

## Prospective validation in EURINE-ACT

The next question was how to validate the test prospectively in clinical practice. Jonathan Deeks, a world-leading diagnostic-test statistician, calculated that we needed to recruit 2,000 adrenal tumors prospectively, with ACC comprising approximately 5% of the cohort. At that time, the largest adrenal-incidentaloma study, by Franco Mantero and colleagues, had recruited approximately 1,000 patients retrospectively over 15 years.

We therefore began EURINE-ACT: Evaluation of Urine Steroid Metabolomics in the Differential Diagnosis of Adrenocortical Tumors. This study would not have succeeded without my excellent postdoctoral fellow Irina Bancos, who was sent to me by William Young at Mayo Clinic. When Irina returned to Mayo, she continued as one of the principal investigators.

Fourteen ENSAT centers recruited 2,017 patients from 2011 through 2016. ACC was present in 98 patients, or 4.9%, close enough to the prespecified 5% for the study to be fully powered. Eighty-four percent of the masses were adrenal incidentalomas. Patients undergoing imaging specifically for screening or staging of an extra-adrenal malignancy were excluded.

The final distribution was 1,767 benign adrenocortical adenomas, 87 other benign adrenal tumors, 65 other malignant adrenal tumors, and 98 ACCs. The size of this unselected prospective cohort also showed how limited imaging can be. With the conventional threshold of greater than 10 HU, 32% of 1,328 benign adenomas were classified as potentially malignant. Raising the threshold to greater than 20 HU reduced false positives from 423 to 200 while retaining 99% of ACCs as true positives.

Among 568 patients who underwent adrenalectomy, 214—38%—had a benign, nonfunctioning adrenal mass and would not have required surgery.

We analyzed the 2,017 24-hour urine samples by steroid mass spectrometry and machine learning. Urine steroid metabolomics, or USM, classified patients by ACC risk. Thresholds were informed by a Delphi consensus among 24 experienced investigators from the participating centers.

As a single test, USM produced far fewer false positives than tumor diameter or unenhanced CT attenuation. Combinations that included USM performed best. Combining tumor diameter, unenhanced CT attenuation, and a high-risk USM result markedly reduced false positives and potentially unnecessary surgery while maintaining excellent identification of true-positive ACCs. The resulting Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology article was accompanied by an editorial describing a multitest strategy for adrenal tumors.

The prospective study achieved a negative predictive value of 99.7% and a positive predictive value of 76.4% for USM, compared with a much lower positive predictive value for imaging alone.

The joint interpretation of size and density was important. In 1,803 participants with both measurements, no ACC occurred among the 1,131 masses that were either below 10 HU at any size or measured less than 40 mm with attenuation of 10–20 HU. Three ACCs occurred among 422 intermediate-risk masses, and 95 ACCs occurred among 247 masses larger than 40 mm with attenuation above 20 HU.

This leads to a proposed pathway. A homogeneous mass with attenuation below 10 HU, regardless of size, or a mass smaller than 40 mm with attenuation of 10–20 HU requires no further imaging. A mass larger than 40 mm at 10–20 HU, or a mass smaller than 40 mm above 20 HU, can undergo interval imaging after 6–12 months. A mass larger than 40 mm and above 20 HU should undergo USM promptly. A high-risk USM result supports surgery. If USM is negative, I would obtain FDG-PET/CT. A positive FDG-PET/CT supports surgery. When both USM and FDG-PET/CT are negative, the pathway suggests interval imaging after 3–6 months and consideration of biopsy.

Biopsy is not useful for distinguishing ACA from ACC, but it can diagnose many of the other benign and malignant tumors that accumulate in this high-risk imaging group.

We are now optimizing the assay for implementation. A National Institute for Health and Care Research Invention for Innovation product-development award supports an eight-steroid, high-throughput LC-MS/MS assay intended for clinical use in the United Kingdom. We hope subsequently to bring it to other countries.

USM may also help detect ACC recurrence. In the proof-of-principle postoperative study led by Vasileios Chortis, serial steroid profiles often recapitulated the steroidogenic fingerprint of the original tumor. Across all recurrence samples, machine-learning classification achieved an AUROC of 0.89; for first recurrence samples alone, the AUROC was 0.80. Vasileios is preparing the prospective ACC-PROTECT follow-up study.

## Why urine outperforms serum

People often ask why we do not simply measure steroids in blood. We anticipated this question: EURINE-ACT participants provided both 24-hour urine and blood. The paired cohort comprised 471 patients—384 with ACA and 87 with ACC. We quantified 14 urinary steroid metabolites and 14 corresponding serum steroids by tandem mass spectrometry.

The machine-learning training cohort included 117 ACA and 40 ACC samples. Repeated internal train-test splits were used to develop the model, after which the algorithm was locked. Prospective validation used the larger independent cohort of 267 ACA and 47 ACC samples.

The locked urine-14 model achieved an AUROC of 0.90, compared with 0.77 for the serum-14 model; the difference was significant at P = .0045. That is a large difference in clinical practice: urine won, and serum lost.

For routine implementation, clinical biochemists asked us to reduce the panel to fewer than 10 steroids. We selected the eight most informative urinary metabolites and eight serum steroids. Urine-8 achieved an AUROC of 0.91, compared with 0.80 for serum-8, P = .028. Urine-8 and urine-14 performed essentially identically, with no meaningful loss of information. Thus, urine steroid metabolomics is superior to serum steroid metabolomics for detecting ACC, and reducing the urinary panel from 14 metabolites to eight preserves performance.

## Steroidogenic ACC subtypes and nighttime urine

Vasileios Chortis and our jointly supervised PhD student Roland Veen, working with Michael Biehl, are also investigating whether ACC contains steroidogenically distinct subgroups. Unsupervised clustering of 89 tumors identified three patterns: one dominated by androgen precursors, one by central and glucocorticoid precursors, and one by glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids.

The preliminary cohort suggested differences in ENSAT stage. Ki-67 was highest in the central- and glucocorticoid-precursor cluster. Overall survival also differed, with the glucocorticoid- and mineralocorticoid-excess cluster showing the best survival in this preliminary analysis. We are taking these findings forward in a larger cohort.

I am a great fan of 24-hour urine because it captures the entire day’s output. But daytime includes activity, meals, and stress, while nighttime—hopefully spent in bed intending to sleep—has less physiologic noise. We hypothesized that a small abnormal steroid signal, such as that from a single metastasis, might be hidden in daytime variation but revealed at night.

For timed collections, participants emptied the bladder when going to bed. The next morning, their first urine was added to the nighttime collection. They recorded bedtime and rising time. A separate daytime collection was performed in the same way. This allowed calculation of steroid-excretion rates in micrograms per hour for day and night.

The proof-of-principle cohort comprised 13 patients with active Cushing syndrome and 39 healthy controls. For total cortisol metabolites, cortisone metabolites, and total glucocorticoid metabolites, respectively, the 24-hour AUROCs were 0.93, 0.86, and 0.92. Daytime performance was lower, at 0.87, 0.85, and 0.72. Nighttime performance was superior, at 0.97, 0.93, and 0.97, and individual steroid measures reached an AUROC of approximately 0.99.

Nighttime collection may therefore improve detection not only of Cushing syndrome but also of ACC or postoperative recurrence. I leave you with that thought and acknowledge the students, postdoctoral fellows, clinical investigators, analytical scientists, and machine-learning collaborators who made this work possible—in particular Alessandro Prete for the Cushing study, Vasileios Chortis for the recurrence study, James Hawley for the serum-versus-urine study, and our longstanding collaborators Michael Biehl and Roland Veen. We look forward to implementing the assay in clinical practice.

## Questions and answers

**Bill Rainey, University of Michigan:** Beautiful work. This may be an adrenal-tumorigenesis question, but at some point in its life an ACC is only 1 cm. At 1 cm, has genetic instability not progressed far enough to produce the steroids you are seeing? Is this a progression, or do you think the tumors produce these steroids when they are still 1 cm?

**Wiebke Arlt:** My opinion is that they produce the steroids from the beginning; that is how they are programmed. When ACC recurs, it recurs with the same steroidogenic pattern it had at diagnosis.

I also asked Jonathan Deeks why nearly all ACCs in the prospective study were large, apart from about two cases. He said this was strong evidence that the overwhelming majority of ACCs grow rapidly. They do not remain small for long; their size rises quickly, so the statistical probability is that they are detected only after becoming larger.

**Hans Ghayee, University of Florida:** Are you considering saliva for some of these precursor steroids? Most patients would prefer something simple to collecting a 24-hour or overnight urine.

**Wiebke Arlt:** Saliva provides a very limited steroid profile. We collaborate with Brian Keevil, who is an expert in this area. We may be able to measure six or seven steroids, but they do not map adequately onto the diagnostic precursor profile. Timed urine is actually quite easy: patients collect it at home and can send it by post without traveling to the hospital. Saliva is also limited because the salivary gland expresses considerable 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2, which causes dominant local steroid conversion and restricts what can be measured.

**Audience member, Liverpool:** In the EURINE-ACT pathway, consider a mass larger than 4 cm with attenuation above 20 HU, a negative USM result, and a negative FDG-PET/CT. If interval imaging or biopsy is chosen, how long should follow-up continue? In our multidisciplinary team, surgeons would be very worried about leaving a mass larger than 4 cm and above 20 HU in place.

**Wiebke Arlt:** I would actively pursue a diagnosis. The pathway is: perform USM; if positive, remove the tumor. If USM is negative, obtain FDG-PET/CT. If FDG-PET/CT is positive, remove the tumor, while recognizing that FDG uptake is not very specific and a positive result can still occur in a benign tumor.

If both USM and FDG-PET/CT are negative, the remaining group contains fewer ACCs but still includes other malignant and benign non-ACC tumors. I personally argue for biopsy to establish the diagnosis. Others may remove all such tumors, but that creates many false-positive operations. Others may repeat imaging after three months, but I would not choose a long follow-up interval. My preference is FDG-PET/CT followed by biopsy when it is negative, to force the diagnosis.

**Troy Puar, Singapore:** The timed overnight urine is attractive. What about patients with obstructive sleep apnea? Might their steroids also rise overnight?

**Wiebke Arlt:** That is an interesting question, and we will have to test it. We currently have approximately 83 patients with timed samples, but no patients with obstructive sleep apnea in the present analysis. In a broader mild-autonomous-cortisol-secretion population, especially one with higher BMI, obstructive sleep apnea may be common. I cannot yet say whether it makes a significant difference. My expectation is that obstructive sleep apnea will still change the steroid profile less than daytime activity, eating, and stress.
