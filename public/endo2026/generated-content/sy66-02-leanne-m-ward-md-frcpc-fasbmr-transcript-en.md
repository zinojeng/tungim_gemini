## Presentation

**Moderator:** I would like to welcome Dr. Leanne Ward from the University of Ottawa in Ontario. She'll be presenting on skeletal fragility in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, closing the gap between risk and intervention.

**Dr. Leanne Ward:** Thank you so very much for hanging in here to Tuesday, and also for your interest in what I think is a very important topic. Osteoporosis in Duchenne muscular dystrophy is essentially unparalleled in terms of the magnitude of bone fragility, and I appreciate your interest in this important topic. These are my disclosures with funds to my institution, and my objectives are to review the mechanisms of bone fragility in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, to discuss the importance of longitudinal skeletal health phenotyping and the way that that links to early anticipatory bone protection therapy, and to highlight the evolving muscle-targeted therapeutic landscape and the way that that impacts skeletal health.

### Duchenne muscular dystrophy and fracture burden

So Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked recessive disorder. It's a rare condition, and most individuals in contemporary times will be diagnosed between four and six years of age. The condition arises from usually deletion mutations, which are loss of function in the DMD gene, and untreated there is progressive muscle strength deterioration with loss of ambulation by about 10 years of age and mean age of death in early adulthood. So this is a devastating diagnosis for families, as you can imagine. 

Glucocorticoids given at supraphysiological doses have shown consistent benefits in clinical trials, and these are the most widely used therapies the world over. They prolong ambulation by about three years. They dramatically decrease the frequency of scoliosis if given on a daily basis. They improve cardiorespiratory function, which provides rationale for their use beyond loss of ambulation, and they extend life expectancy into the mid to late twenties on average, with reports of individuals living into their thirties and even rarely reports of living into one's forties. Steroids decrease inflammation, which is part of the pathobiology of this condition. There is an inflammatory reaction to the death of myofibers in this condition, and they do so through the NF-κB system. But harsh side effects limit their use. So, for example, there's double the risk of fractures if you start steroids before five years of age. So this is notionally imperfect in that sense. 

There are a number of approved therapies for Duchenne in the United States beyond classic steroids like deflazacort. There's a dissociative steroid which attempts to separate the good from the bad, and I'll talk about that. There's exon skipping therapies, gene therapy, and an HDAC inhibitor. What you need to know as an endocrinologist is that all of these therapies are on a backbone of glucocorticoid regimens. And so we have not yet been able to relinquish the patient from steroid therapy, and even higher doses are used peri–gene therapy. So glucocorticoid therapy is here for the foreseeable future, and it's from that perspective that I speak today.

### Mechanisms and clinical burden of bone fragility

We know that the long-bone fracture frequency is driven by the myopathy, and this is a very nice example of a four-year-old boy recently diagnosed with Duchenne, and he has a femur fracture prior to glucocorticoid initiation. 

Indeed, there is an unfavorable myokine-osteokine relationship in Duchenne muscular dystrophy. There are myokines that are upregulated that have a deleterious effect on bone health, like interleukin-6, and there's osteokines that are upregulated that also have a deleterious effect, like RANK ligand (RANKL). So that drives the myopathic phenotype in addition to the lack of weight bearing. And then the glucocorticoid doses are given in the spirit of long-term use. They're supraphysiological, and there's essentially no bone strength pathway that's preserved on glucocorticoid therapy. So it's no surprise that the particularly spine phenotype is significantly exacerbated by steroids. 

This is a serious osteoporosis. So children with, and adults with femur fractures or tibia fractures will have premature loss of ambulation if you expect to walk until you're 14 and you stop walking at 8, that is devastating. And there's been unexpected premature death due to fat embolism syndrome after a fracture that results in respiratory distress. And then vertebral fractures, if left unattended, can be profound. This is an example in the middle. This is an individual after five years of unmonitored deflazacort from a spine perspective, and this is associated with pain and early loss of ambulation and also potential for respiratory compromise. 

We know that the lifetime risk of fractures in Duchenne is very high, with modeling statistics showing that up to 100% of patients on daily classic steroids will have at least one vertebral fracture, and the non-vertebral fracture frequency is 30 to 60%, whether patients are treated with steroids or not. Plus, there's lack of potential for spontaneous, that is medication-unassisted recovery, and that's in contrast to a condition like leukemia of childhood, where most children will recover from even profound osteoporosis. 

We did a systematic review in order to map out the bone health trajectory in Duchenne, benchmarked to the literature, and I just want to draw your attention to the bottom, where it says that the first long bone fracture occurs on average at 7 to 9 years of age, and the first vertebral fracture, just above it, occurs around the same time at 8 to 9 years of age, and on average two years after starting steroid therapy. So not only is this a substantial osteoporotic condition, but it's a precocious one. And then the phenotype just amplifies from there. 

There's been a lot of interest in the extent to which the different steroid regimens help with muscle versus negatively impact bone. And what we know is that daily deflazacort is associated with the highest risk of fracture, followed by daily prednisone, and then the intermittent regimens like prednisone 10 days on/10 days off are associated with a lower fracture rate, but at a cost to muscle strength. And even in the absence of steroids, there's still an increase in the likelihood of a fracture. So all individuals, whether steroid treated or not, whether on daily or intermittent regimens or not, require bone health monitoring.

### Longitudinal skeletal health phenotyping

Indeed, longitudinal skeletal health phenotyping unveils the progressive and substantial nature of this osteoporosis. So I want to take you through that. This is a lovely study looking at the distal tibia by peripheral quantitative computed tomography (pQCT) in individuals with Duchenne compared to a healthy cohort. And you can see after two years of monitoring that the boy with Duchenne who remained mobile during that time had a certain volumetric BMD at the distal tibia, but the boy who lost ambulation had a profound loss of vBMD. So we know from a bone protection perspective that we need to be getting to the patients before they lose ambulation. 

For longitudinal spine fracture phenotyping, we use the Genant semiquantitative (Genant SQ) method, and then we sum the Genant grades to derive the spinal deformity index. As you well know, Genant SQ defines a fracture critically as more than 20% loss in vertebral height ratio, and then there are degrees of deformity from there. And we can sum the Genant grades in order to develop the SDI and follow that longitudinally to see if patients are getting worse or getting better. This is an example of longitudinal spine phenotyping in a child. At baseline, T9 is normal, and then a year later, T9 has a grade 1 fracture. If left untreated, that progresses to grade 2, and then T10 at 11 years of age is now also fractured. So this is a train accelerating out of the station. 

Now, in a relentlessly progressive osteoporotic condition like Duchenne muscular dystrophy, even a pre-20% vertebral height ratio loss is clinically significant because of the lack of potential for recovery, and that is provided the progressive height ratio loss is evident relative to prior images. So let me give you an example. T11 looks lovely at baseline in this 9-year-old, and then 3 years later, there's depression of the superior endplate that has not yet reached more than 20% loss in vertebral height ratio, but this is clinically significant because we know that it will just progress, and it's associated with declines in BMD Z-scores. Now, it's interesting because the spine BMD Z-score holds on longer than the hip BMD Z-score, so it's important to do multimodal BMD assessments in these patients. And if you do so, you'll see that the hip is the first BMD to decline in this context. 

We've done a number of studies, and so have other colleagues, looking at the risk factors for imminent fractures in individuals with Duchenne in childhood. We've talked about the myopathy. We've talked about glucocorticoid dose that links also to duration. Glucocorticoid-induced endocrinopathy also exacerbates that risk, and I'll talk about that on the next slide. Prior history of fracture, lumbar spine vBMD and total hip BMD. A delay in bone age also heralds imminent fractures, and something called a reduction in the bone health index, which is a surrogate for bone strength measured on the metacarpals of the left hand on a bone age X-ray by an AI program called BoneXpert. So this is a paper where we looked at the risk factors for an increase in the spinal deformity index in a pediatric cohort of individuals with Duchenne so that we could see the risk factors benchmarked to each other. And we showed that a lower lumbar spine areal BMD Z-score, higher weight, a lower BHI Z-score by BoneXpert, a reduction in height Z-score, although small stature itself is not associated with an increased risk of fractures, it is a marker of significant systemic steroid exposure, and a higher average daily steroid dose all were associated with an increase in the likelihood of a higher spinal deformity index. 

In terms of the way in which endocrinopathies interact with fracture risk, we know that being heavier is a risk factor for fractures. It's harder to maintain strength and prevent fracture if you're heavy, falling on an outstretched arm in this context, and also delayed puberty is a risk factor for fractures. When we talk to boys about their delayed puberty, it's a nuanced discussion. Some of the boys will say that they want puberty because they want to have a lower voice and they want to have masculinization. But others will say, Well, I'm 16, but I have the height age of an eight-year-old, and if you give me a beard, that's just one more difference compared to my healthy peers. So this is a very nuanced discussion with individuals indeed. And we know, though, that giving testosterone prevents vertebral fracture progression. So there is biological rationale for doing it even beyond the aesthetics of the testosterone supplementation.

### Early anticipatory bone protection

It is very important to operationalize bone protection in a timely fashion, given the aggressive and precocious nature of this condition. 

In 2018, we published guidelines that at the time, based on the evidence, stated that bone protection should be initiated in the presence of at least one low-trauma vertebral or non-vertebral fracture. We now appreciate that that's too late. And so now the recommendation, and this is a manuscript in preparation for submission to the Journal of Neuromuscular Diseases, is that we start before first fractures to keep individuals on their feet and prevent that vertebral fracture cascade. And we do so in the presence of evident vertebral height ratio loss, even if it precedes the critical threshold of more than 20%, or a clinically significant decline in lumbar spine or hip BMD Z-scores defined as a half a standard deviation decline or more in terms of a Z-score. 

And so with that bone health monitoring guidance, then this is the current monitoring and treatment paradigm. So we recommend doing a spine and a hip BMD. Some also like to do total body and lateral distal femur BMDs annually around the time of diagnosis or steroid start. If an individual is on steroids, no matter what the dose or regimen, we recommend spine imaging annually. If not on steroids, spine imaging every two to three years by conventional radiography or a DXA-based vertebral fracture assessment (VFA), and spine imaging more often if there's back pain or there's BMD declines of a half a standard deviation or more. And then we recommend starting IV bisphosphonate therapy if there's evidence of vertebral height ratio loss, even if it precedes 20%, or if there's significant declines in BMD Z-scores, as I mentioned, and to continue bone protection as long as an individual is on steroid therapy and potentially even longer if there's unmet needs and it's safe to do so.

### Bisphosphonates and denosumab

So how do we prevent and treat bone fragility once criteria for intervention are met? So here I think of the skeletally immature, and then I think of the skeletally mature when I think about this question. For the skeletally immature, drawing on all the evidence we have from Duchenne as well as pediatric osteoporosis in general, we recommend intravenous bisphosphonate therapy first line because this is an aggressive osteoporosis that needs to be met by an agent with a high bioavailability. And then second line is oral bisphosphonate therapy if IV is not available or feasible. 

This is a systematic review that we did looking at the oral versus IV agents across studies, and just make the point that zoledronic acid gives rise to a greater mean annual change in areal BMD Z-scores than oral agents as expected. 

So what are the short-term effects of bisphosphonate therapy in Duchenne muscular dystrophy? Well, we have two randomized trials. One is zoledronic acid versus IV placebo in pediatric steroid-induced osteoporosis. A third of the patients on the left had Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and on the right, this was a Duchenne-specific cohort, a randomized trial of IV zoledronic acid versus calcium and vitamin D supplementation. And they both showed significant increases in lumbar spine BMD Z-scores with the maximum gain in the first six months, and then a plateau but a maintenance of that gain over six to 12 months and out to 24 months in the trial on the right. In the Duchenne-specific trial on the right, incident new vertebral fractures occurred in 24% of the control group and 15% of the zoledronic acid group. The trial was not powered to look at the differences statistically, but that is a clinically significant difference. And more importantly, there were patients in the control group with new grade 3 fractures that needed to go on zoledronic acid and come off the trial. 

What about the long-term effects of bisphosphonate therapy in Duchenne? Well, there's essentially no data, and this is an unmet need in the field. We have a manuscript in preparation for submission done by one of my colleagues working with my team, and this is a study of 32 children all steroid treated with Duchenne on daily steroids, with a mean age pre-bisphosphonate therapy of 11 years and an average duration of IV pamidronate or zoledronic acid of almost five years, ranging from two to nine years. This is a pre-post study with no control group. Compared to baseline, there were statistically significant increases in lumbar spine total hip and total body less head BMD Z-scores, and you can very clearly see here how much lower the hip BMD is relative to the spine in this cohort, which is what we see clinically. So that was very encouraging to see. 

And then this is the spine fracture data. Eighty-eight percent of patients had vertebral fractures pre-treatment, 78% had at least one post-treatment. The mean number of prevalent vertebral fractures per patient was 3.4 at baseline and the same at follow-up, and the average SDI per patient at baseline was 5 and 5.5 at follow-up. So despite a high prevalence of vertebral fractures at baseline, the spine phenotype largely held fast in most patients after five years of IV bisphosphonate therapy. Thirty-four percent of patients had incident non-vertebral fractures, and that is not surprising because bisphosphonates do not correct the small cross-sectional diameter of long bones. And so we see here that the fractures occurred mostly at the lower extremities, and that is also very typical of Duchenne muscular dystrophy. 

So we know that bisphosphonates work best at trabecular-rich sites like the spine and the hip, that the long bones are more resistant because the bone is compact and there's a lower surface area. And as I said, bisphosphonates do not alter the cross-sectional diameter of bones, which are reduced in Duchenne muscular dystrophy because of the myopathy. So this opens the door to thinking about other therapies beyond antiresorptives. 

Now, what about denosumab in Duchenne muscular dystrophy? This is notionally attractive because RANK ligand (RANKL) is upregulated both in bone and in muscle in Duchenne. And so we asked the question: Is the low bone turnover of Duchenne combined with chronic glucocorticoid therapy protective against the hypercalcemia rebound in Duchenne muscular dystrophy? And the answer to that is a resounding no. 

So this is what happens when you give denosumab to a patient who is growing. So first of all, in A, you see a very dense block of bone because this is a powerful antiresorptive. And then at B, this sets up a stress riser with potential for fracture, as you see here. And then at C, we have a fracture of the lower extremities as well that goes through the dense metaphyseal band. 

We found in this paper that hypercalcemia rebound preceded these dense metaphyseal band fractures, and that the hypercalcemia rebound was associated with a stark decline in the BMD gains that we had previously realized. So this was very concerning indeed and, of course, not unexpected given what we know about denosumab. And that was while on active therapy, not post-discontinuation. But I'd like to share with you that the rebound hypercalcemia is more consequential in Duchenne than in non-myopathic individuals because the hypercalcemia causes muscle catabolism, as measured by hypercreatininuria. And so really denosumab is not a viable option in Duchenne, in my opinion.

### Skeletally mature adults with DMD

So what about prevention and treatment of bone fragility in skeletally mature individuals with DMD? There are no studies. If you want to hook into a research area, this one is ripe for the taking. I'd like to just think this through a little bit for you because these patients still have an unmet need despite the lack of Duchenne-specific evidence for how to treat the adults. 

First of all, I just want to sensitize you to the variability in the adult phenotype. This is a study that we're doing and unpublished images of high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) of the distal radius in adult men with Duchenne compared to a beautiful example of a healthy control. So all of these individuals have variable antecedent medical histories and antecedent glucocorticoid and bisphosphonate exposures. So in the middle, this is a 25-year-old. He's now not walking. He has never had steroid therapy, and he's never had bone protection, and his trab vBMD Z-score is minus 2.2. And then a 19-year-old is walking. He's had prolonged steroid exposure and zoledronic acid for seven years, and his trabecular vBMD Z-score is minus 3.3. And then this is a 32-year-old with Duchenne. He's non-ambulatory now. He's had prolonged glucocorticoid therapy, and he had denosumab for 13 years, and his trab vBMD Z-score is minus 5.5. And then a 24-year-old non-ambulatory individual had prolonged steroid therapy and also had zoledronic acid, but he stopped when he was 19, and he was still growing, and his trab vBMD Z-score is minus 5.7. And we do not recommend stopping bisphosphonate therapy when an individual is still growing. 

So how are we going to think about these adults with Duchenne and how to treat their osteoporosis? So we can think about the adult glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIO) guidelines with special considerations for Duchenne, and the adult GIO guidelines, of course, come from the American College of Rheumatology 2022 guideline, published in 2023. 

So oral bisphosphonates, these individuals can lose the ability to safely swallow tablets, and we can't crush them, and they're already at increased risk for esophagitis because of steroid therapy. I've already given you my view on denosumab. 

There's limited evidence for the use of teriparatide in Duchenne, but we know they're less effective after IV bisphosphonates, and so we anticipate that this may not be an option for individuals who have had bisphosphonates already in childhood. 

There's no cases of sclerostin-antibody use in Duchenne, and I think the concern here is that one year's duration of sclerostin-antibody is unlikely to rescue this phenotype plus cardiovascular concerns, given that these individuals pass away from cardiorespiratory failure. 

And so I think in adults at the present time, in the absence of other evidence, we are limited to IV bisphosphonate therapy. It is the most frequently used agent in Duchenne, and once an individual is skeletally mature, we can give annual dosing. But certainly more data is needed to inform the treatment of adults with Duchenne.

### Vamorolone and future directions

And finally, I'll just finish up by talking about the fact that the first principle of chronic-disease osteoporosis management is effective treatment of the underlying disease, ideally with a steroid-sparing agent, as you know. So let's talk about that. 

There is a new steroid called vamorolone, which is a dissociative steroid, a partially dissociative steroid that attempts to separate the good things we like about steroids from the side effects. This is a drug that was approved for the treatment of Duchenne in the U.S. in October 2023. It retains anti-inflammatory benefits and shows similar efficacy relative to prednisone and deflazacort up to three years of use for muscle strength outcomes. It has some improvement in endocrine side effects. Growth is improved and bone strength is improved at the spine, specifically at the spine. 

So let's unpackage this. Vamorolone is distinct from classic corticosteroids. It lacks an 11β oxygen, and it's not a substrate for 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase enzymes, which are what mediate the side effects of steroids. It is a mineralocorticoid antagonist, and that has implications for adrenal insufficiency management, and I'll talk about that in a moment. So what's going on here is there has been medicinal chemistry or conformational chemistry by introducing the 9,11 double bond and removing the 11β oxygen, there is a change in the shape of the steroid and it interacts in its receptor differently. 

We know through clinical trials, pivotal phase 3 clinical trials and extension studies that vamorolone improves bone turnover markers, which theoretically should lead to better bone adaptation to mechanical loads and improved fracture healing, although fracture healing is not a particular problem in Duchenne, in my experience. We see fewer but not absent spine fractures, and we see normal growth at least out to three years based on what we have so far. 

This is a study that we did looking at the vamorolone-treated patients after three years of steroid exposure compared to daily prednisone and daily deflazacort, and we found that the fracture prevalence was about 13% compared to more than double on daily prednisone and daily deflazacort, and we did not find fractures of the spine on intermittent prednisone after three years. 

We still see femur fractures, and I remind individuals that we're going to see femur fractures that is pathological ones until we rescue the dystrophinopathy. These are myopathy-driven. 

So we know vamorolone normalizes linear growth and improves bone turnover markers, and it reduces but does not eliminate spine fractures. It does not prevent excess weight gain. It does not prevent adrenal suppression. It cannot be used for steroid stress dosing because of the antagonism to the mineralocorticoid receptor. And we don't know if there's a similar effect on muscle strength, scoliosis, and cardiorespiratory health beyond three years of use, and we don't know if it normalizes pubertal development. 

So going forward, we have a lot of work to do in this space. This is a rare disease. It is a difficult disease to study from an endocrine and bone perspective because the families are so focused on the myopathy and being in trials specifically for that, which we understand. 

A few years ago, we decided to come together and form the OPTIMIZE DMD Consortium, which you can find at optimizedmd.org, and this is an international group of researchers and advocates and educators moving together forward in order to optimize the endocrine and bone health management of this condition. And we formed working groups to address the various facets of DMD care from an endocrine and bone perspective. And with that, I thank you for your attention and your interest in this important topic, and I look forward to your questions.
