## Introduction and clinical case

This presentation will address the question: How do we diagnose AVP deficiency in adults? Thank you, Cihan.

I would like to begin with a case. This is a 57-year-old patient who reported slowly increasing thirst over 6 months and now drinks 5–6 L/day. The patient also described nocturia several times per night and drinking twice during the night. There were no other complaints, comorbidities, or medications.

This case leads to the first recommendations: Whom should we test for AVP deficiency?

## Whom to test for AVP deficiency

We recommend ruling out other known causes of polyuria and polydipsia, including hyperglycemia and hypercalcemia, before considering AVP deficiency.

We recommend evaluating patients for AVP deficiency when they present with persistent hypotonic polyuria—typically defined as more than 40 mL/kg per 24 hours or more than 3 L/24 hours, with relatively dilute urine having an osmolality below 800 mOsm/kg—and concomitant polydipsia.

Returning to our patient, glucose was within the normal range, as was calcium, so we could exclude those other causes of polyuria and polydipsia. A 24-hour urine collection showed 5.3 L of relatively dilute urine, with a urine osmolality of 330 mOsm/kg and an approximate fluid intake of 5 L/day. Clearly, this patient should be evaluated for AVP deficiency.

## Baseline laboratory evaluation

As the first step, we recommend a baseline evaluation. In patients with unexplained hypotonic polyuria and polydipsia, we recommend measuring a random plasma or serum sodium level, plasma osmolality, and spot urine osmolality as the initial diagnostic laboratory evaluation.

We also suggest calculating plasma osmolality using the following formula:

**Calculated plasma osmolality = (2 × plasma sodium) + glucose + urea.**

Calculated osmolality is less variable, provided that glucose and urea are within their normal ranges.

How should we interpret these baseline values? We recommend that AVP deficiency or AVP resistance is highly likely when random plasma sodium is above 146 mmol/L. Conversely, we recommend that primary polydipsia is highly likely when random plasma sodium is below 135 mmol/L and/or spot urine osmolality is above 650 mOsm/kg.

These recommendations are based in part on the data shown here. AVP deficiency is shown in blue and primary polydipsia in red. The threshold with the highest specificity for AVP deficiency was plasma sodium above 146 mmol/L, whereas plasma sodium below 135 mmol/L supported primary polydipsia.

For calculated plasma osmolality, the corresponding thresholds were above 300 mOsm/L for AVP deficiency and below 275 mOsm/L for primary polydipsia. The same thresholds applied to measured plasma osmolality, although its diagnostic accuracy was slightly lower. The urine-osmolality threshold with high specificity for primary polydipsia was above 650 mOsm/kg.

Returning to our patient, random plasma sodium was 142 mmol/L and measured plasma osmolality was 292 mOsm/kg. These values were in the indeterminate range, so we could not establish the diagnosis from them. The same was true of the calculated plasma osmolality of 290 mOsm/L and urine osmolality of 260 mOsm/kg. All values were in the gray zone, so further evaluation was needed.

## Unstimulated copeptin at baseline

What about copeptin in the baseline evaluation? If plasma copeptin measurement is available, we suggest measuring unstimulated, or random, plasma copeptin in patients with hypotonic polyuria and polydipsia in addition to the baseline tests.

We recommend that a baseline copeptin level above 5.6 pmol/L rules out AVP deficiency and makes primary polydipsia highly likely. We also recommend that a markedly elevated baseline copeptin level of at least 21 pmol/L is diagnostic of AVP resistance.

The data show that the threshold above 5.6 pmol/L has very high specificity for primary polydipsia. For AVP resistance, the available data showed copeptin levels above approximately 21 pmol/L.

Our patient's random copeptin concentration was 1.9 pmol/L. This was again within the gray zone and did not establish a conclusive diagnosis.

## Baseline diagnostic algorithm

To summarize the first part of the algorithm, we first confirm hypotonic polyuria—urine osmolality below 800 mOsm/kg, with urine volume above 40 mL/kg per 24 hours and/or above 3 L/24 hours—and polydipsia above 3 L/24 hours.

We then obtain random plasma sodium, plasma osmolality, plasma copeptin if available, and spot urine osmolality.

Primary polydipsia is supported by plasma sodium below 135 mmol/L, copeptin above 5.6 pmol/L, plasma osmolality below 275 mOsm/kg, or urine osmolality above 650 mOsm/kg. Conversely, plasma sodium above 146 mmol/L or plasma osmolality above 300 mOsm/kg supports AVP deficiency or AVP resistance. A baseline copeptin concentration of at least 21 pmol/L supports AVP resistance. Values between these thresholds remain inconclusive.

## Overnight fluid-deprivation test

Unless it is contraindicated, we recommend an overnight fluid-deprivation test when either copeptin measurement is unavailable or unstimulated copeptin is below 5.6 pmol/L.

The data shown on the left represent baseline thresholds, while the right side shows the results after 8 hours of overnight fluid deprivation. The thresholds are the same as those used for the baseline evaluation: plasma sodium above 146 mmol/L or osmolality above 300 mOsm/kg for AVP deficiency, plasma sodium below 135 mmol/L or osmolality below 275 mOsm/kg for primary polydipsia, urine osmolality above 650 mOsm/kg for primary polydipsia, and copeptin above 5.6 pmol/L for primary polydipsia.

The important difference is that the overnight test allows us to rule in or rule out a diagnosis in more patients. With baseline measurements, 71% of patients remained in the inconclusive range. After overnight fluid deprivation, this proportion fell to 35%.

Returning to our patient, after overnight fluid deprivation from midnight to 8 a.m., plasma sodium increased to 145 mmol/L. This was a clear increase, but it remained below the threshold of 146 mmol/L. Measured plasma osmolality increased to 298 mOsm/kg and calculated osmolality to 296 mOsm/L, but both remained below 300. Urine osmolality increased to 590 mOsm/kg, showing that the patient could concentrate urine, but it remained below 650. Copeptin increased to 2.8 pmol/L. All of these results remained within the gray zone, so the cause of the polyuria and polydipsia required further evaluation.

## Dynamic testing

In patients who remain in the indeterminate range after the baseline evaluation and overnight fluid-deprivation test, we recommend performing a copeptin stimulation test. If copeptin testing is unavailable or not feasible, we recommend a formal water-deprivation test.

Two copeptin stimulation tests have been validated.

### Hypertonic saline stimulation test

The hypertonic saline stimulation test takes approximately 2–4 hours. A stimulated copeptin concentration of 4.9 pmol/L or less supports AVP deficiency, whereas a value above 4.9 pmol/L supports primary polydipsia. This test has a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 95%–97%.

Close monitoring with rapid sodium measurements is essential. Sodium must be measured every 30 minutes. The test is contraindicated in patients with epilepsy, uncontrolled hypertension, or heart failure.

### Arginine stimulation test

The arginine stimulation test is shorter, taking approximately 1.5 hours. A copeptin concentration of 3.0 pmol/L or less supports AVP deficiency, while a concentration above 5.2 pmol/L supports primary polydipsia. Values from above 3.0 through 5.2 pmol/L constitute a nondiagnostic zone, in which another test is required to establish the diagnosis.

The arginine test must be interpreted carefully in the presence of severe nausea and/or vomiting.

This completes the diagnostic algorithm. When dynamic testing is needed, we recommend copeptin-based testing with either arginine or hypertonic saline stimulation. If copeptin testing is unavailable, we recommend the indirect water-deprivation test. I will not discuss those interpretation thresholds in detail; they derive from the classic criteria published by Miller and colleagues.

## Hypertonic saline test in the case patient

In this patient, we performed a hypertonic saline stimulation test. The patient had no fluid intake from 6 a.m., and the test began at 8 a.m. with an initial 250 mL bolus of 3% saline over 15 minutes, followed by a continuous infusion at 0.15 mL/kg/min.

Sodium was monitored every 30 minutes until it reached at least 150 mmol/L, at which point plasma sodium and copeptin were measured. Afterward, the patient drank water and received 500 mL of 5% glucose intravenously over 40–60 minutes until serum sodium normalized.

The patient's baseline plasma sodium was 139 mmol/L. Sodium measured by venous blood gas analysis was 142 mmol/L at 30 minutes, 146 mmol/L at 60 minutes, and 151 mmol/L at 90 minutes. At 90 minutes, laboratory plasma sodium was 150 mmol/L and the corresponding copeptin concentration was 14.5 pmol/L.

This stimulated copeptin value supported the final diagnosis of primary polydipsia.

## Further evaluation after confirmed AVP deficiency

Finally, in patients whose testing confirms AVP deficiency, we recommend a complete anatomical evaluation of the suprasellar and pituitary regions and a biochemical evaluation of pituitary function.
