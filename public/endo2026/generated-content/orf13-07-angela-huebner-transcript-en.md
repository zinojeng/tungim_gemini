## Background: congenital adrenal hyperplasia and the need for in vivo models

Thank you very much for the invitation to give this talk today. I am very happy to share with you the results of our novel humanized mouse models for congenital adrenal hyperplasia.

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a group of rare genetic disorders of adrenal steroid biosynthesis. The most common form is 21-hydroxylase deficiency, which leads to glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid deficiency and massive adrenal androgen excess. This androgen excess causes virilization of the genitalia in female newborns and virilization later in women whose disease is poorly controlled.

21-hydroxylase deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the *CYP21A2* gene. Controlling the androgen excess usually requires very high, supraphysiological glucocorticoid doses. This is associated with many undesirable side effects, such as growth failure, obesity, hypertension, and osteoporosis, and has paved the way for the development of new therapies for CAH.

What has been lacking in the field is an effective in vivo model for preclinical testing of novel substances. Decades ago, a *Cyp21a1* knockout mouse model was developed. However, these mice survive only with massive and continuous dexamethasone treatment and are very difficult to handle.

## Generation of the humanized knock-in models

We therefore decided to develop two knock-in mouse models carrying mutations that are common in human patients and have approximately 1%–2% residual enzyme activity: I173N and R484Q. The I173N mutation manifests as either the simple virilizing form or, in some patients, a salt-wasting form.

To generate these mice, we first replaced the mouse *Cyp21a1* gene with the human *CYP21A2* gene and then used CRISPR–Cas9 to insert the mutations. Today I will focus on characterization of the homozygous I173N mice because the other mouse strain, R484Q, was recently published by our group.

When we first attempted to breed homozygous I173N mice, we could not obtain any homozygous offspring. We therefore treated the pregnant dams with dexamethasone beginning around embryonic day 19 and continued treatment through weaning on day 21. After weaning, the pups survived without any further glucocorticoid treatment. The data shown today come from organs and blood collected when the mice were approximately 19–20 weeks old.

## Body weight, adrenal enlargement, and histology

Body weight was slightly lower in the female homozygous mice. In contrast, adrenal weights were markedly elevated in both male and female homozygous mice compared with wild-type mice.

This enlargement was evident macroscopically and histologically. The adrenal glands were very large in both sexes. Histologically, we observed adrenocortical hyperplasia typical of CAH.

This hyperplasia can also be seen in human newborn adrenal glands. I showed sonographic images of an adrenal gland from a healthy newborn and adrenal hyperplasia in a newborn with CAH; the appearance was very similar.

## Adrenal gene expression, steroid profile, and ACTH

In the adrenal gene-expression analysis, we observed very high expression of the human *CYP21A2* gene, as expected, and increased expression of *Cyp11a1*, *Cyp11b1*, and, somewhat surprisingly, *Cyp11b2*. This is probably due to strong overall stimulation of the adrenal glands by ACTH.

We therefore examined pituitary *Pomc* expression and observed increased *Pomc* expression. These gene-expression results were also reflected in steroid and ACTH measurements in mouse plasma obtained by LC–MS/MS.

The homozygous mice had very low 11-deoxycorticosterone and corticosterone levels in the presence of high progesterone levels, a situation very similar to that in human patients. ACTH was also increased.

## Urinary catecholamine metabolites and adrenal-medullary function

We also examined urinary catecholamine metabolites. Normetanephrine and metanephrine were decreased only in female mice, whereas 3-methoxytyramine was decreased in both male and female mice.

This may point to depressed adrenal-medullary function, which has also been described in human patients. This finding and the observed sexual dimorphism require further investigation.

## Blood pressure and aldosterone

Interestingly, we found extremely low systolic and diastolic blood pressure in the I173N mutant mice.

We then measured aldosterone levels. Aldosterone was unchanged in males but was elevated in females. Whether the low blood pressure is related to depressed adrenal-medullary function or to the very low glucocorticoid levels remains to be elucidated.

## Gonadal phenotype

When we focused on the gonads, we found that the female mice were infertile and remained in the diestrous phase. Histologically, the ovaries of homozygous females lacked corpora lutea and showed impaired follicular development, compatible with the observed ovulatory failure.

The males were fertile. Their testes did not differ between wild-type and homozygous mice, either macroscopically or histologically.

## Adrenal transcriptomics

We then performed bulk RNA sequencing of the adrenal glands from female I173N mice. We observed upregulation of steroidogenic and ACTH-response pathways and downregulation of certain circadian, developmental, and metabolic pathways.

This indicates possible adrenal overactivation together with disrupted adrenal development and rhythmicity.

## Potential use for preclinical therapy development

Overall, we think these humanized 21-hydroxylase-deficient knock-in mouse models may provide an excellent system for investigating novel therapies being developed for CAH, such as chaperones, peptidomimetic or small molecules, and even stem-cell treatment.

In summary, *CYP21A2*-p.I173N and *CYP21A2*-p.R484Q mice are the first viable knock-in mouse models that partly mimic the human CAH phenotype and are available for testing future therapies relevant to CAH.

The I173N mice exhibit a stronger phenotype than the recently published R484Q mice. Steroid hormones such as progesterone, 11-deoxycorticosterone, and corticosterone in serum, as well as tetrahydroprogesterone in urine, are well suited for measuring the success of potential therapies.

Mice carrying the I173N mutation show various features of sexual dimorphism in adrenal and gonadal function in CAH that also require further investigation. We believe these mouse models will support the transition from basic research into clinical application.

With this, I thank you for your attention. I am also thankful to my group in Dresden and to all our collaborators who contributed to this work. Thank you very much for your attention. We have time for questions.

## Question and answer session

### Possible alternative pathway for aldosterone formation

**Audience member 1:** Interestingly, you found that aldosterone levels were normal. There is some work indicating that 21-deoxyaldosterone can be converted to aldosterone by a different cytochrome, including in peripheral tissues. I do not remember the exact cytochrome; I read this work more than 20 years ago.

At that time, I also had a fellow who demonstrated in lymphocytes that 21-deoxyaldosterone could be converted to aldosterone, although the paper was never finished. Maria New also described a patient many years ago with classic 21-hydroxylase deficiency caused by a deletion, so the enzyme activity was zero. The patient was lost to follow-up for more than a year without medication but appeared normal when she returned. We confirmed the 21-hydroxylase deficiency, yet aldosterone was completely normal. The conversion of 21-deoxyaldosterone was probably the explanation. This is very interesting, and you should look for that older reference.

**Angela Huebner:** Thank you very much for this comment. We had a similar thought. In the other mouse strain, we found elevated aldosterone levels, and we thought this might represent some form of compensatory mechanism within the adrenal gland. We will look into what you mentioned. Thank you; that is very interesting.

### Prenatal dexamethasone and survival of homozygous pups

**Audience member 2:** You mentioned that you had to treat the pregnant mice with dexamethasone because otherwise the animals did not survive. Can you explain why you needed to do that? My adrenal physiology is a little rusty.

**Angela Huebner:** First, it was an observation that we could not obtain any homozygous pups. The homozygous pups must have died either in utero or perinatally. We knew from other models that we could try treating the mothers in order to treat the pups and perhaps allow them to grow larger and stronger.

This is what happened in our mice. After prenatal treatment, we obtained viable pups. After 20 or 21 days, we could stop dexamethasone, and they remained viable without supplementation.

**Audience member 2:** Can you speculate about the mechanism of action? In humans, we do not see intrauterine mortality in patients with these mutations.

**Angela Huebner:** It is difficult to say. I cannot really answer that question.
