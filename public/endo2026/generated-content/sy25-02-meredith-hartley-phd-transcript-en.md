### Introduction: thyroid hormone and myelin

**Moderator:** It is a pleasure to introduce our second speaker, Meredith Hartley from the University of Kansas, who will share data on thyroid-hormone regulation of microglia during myelin damage and repair.

**Meredith Hartley:** I am glad to be here to tell you about our work in this area. My disclosures relate to licensed thyromimetics that I will mention later.

I will begin with the connection between myelin and thyroid hormone and describe how thyroid hormone regulates myelin during development and disease. I will then discuss how central nervous system, or CNS, lipids are modulated by thyroid hormone in the context of myelin disease. That brings us to our more recent interest in microglia as another thyroid-hormone-regulated cell type.

Myelin is formed by oligodendrocytes. An oligodendrocyte extends membranes in many directions, and the double lipid bilayer wraps repeatedly around an axon and compacts to form a myelin sheath. A single oligodendrocyte can form up to 50 separate wraps, making this an extraordinary cell type with an important connection to thyroid hormone.

Oligodendrocytes arise from neural stem cells through an oligodendrocyte progenitor cell, or OPC, stage and then mature into myelinating oligodendrocytes. T3 is a key developmental signal that prompts OPCs to differentiate. In humans, thyroid-hormone levels rise dramatically around birth as the brain begins myelinating, and myelination continues into the twenties.

This developmental role prompted interest in whether thyroid hormone could also promote myelin repair in disease. In multiple sclerosis and other demyelinating disorders, could T3 improve remyelination? Several groups have shown this in preclinical models. However, administering T3 to a euthyroid person would cause hyperthyroidism, so researchers have pursued thyroid-hormone receptor agonists that might preserve remyelinating activity while reducing systemic toxicity.

### Sobetirome and the CNS-selective prodrug Sob-AM2

As a postdoctoral fellow in Tom Scanlan's laboratory, I worked with sobetirome, also known as GC-1. Sobetirome is a well-studied thyromimetic. Through thyroid-hormone receptor beta selectivity and selective tissue distribution, it avoids much of the cardiac, bone, and skeletal-muscle toxicity associated with systemic thyroid hormone; for example, it has limited distribution to the heart.

The Scanlan laboratory subsequently developed Sob-AM2. In Sob-AM2, sobetirome's carboxylic acid is masked as a methyl amide, creating a neutral prodrug that crosses the blood-brain barrier more readily. In the brain, fatty acid amide hydrolase, or FAAH, cleaves the prodrug back to sobetirome. This is a brain drug-delivery strategy for a thyroid-hormone receptor agonist.

We tested whether these compounds could promote myelin repair and demonstrated that sobetirome and Sob-AM2 improved myelin restoration in mouse models. When I started my own laboratory, I wanted to understand how these agonists worked and how thyroid-hormone regulation of lipid metabolism contributed to myelin repair.

Myelin is an exceptionally lipid-rich membrane: approximately 70% to 80% of its composition is lipid, compared with roughly 40% in a typical cell membrane. It is also highly enriched in cholesterol. We therefore asked how thyroid-hormone-driven lipid metabolism changes during demyelination and remyelination.

### The Plp1-iCKO-Myrf demyelination-remyelination model

We used a genetic demyelination model, the Plp1-iCKO-Myrf mouse. MYRF is a transcription factor required for healthy myelin. In this model, inducible deletion of Myrf occurs specifically in mature myelinating oligodendrocytes. When mature oligodendrocytes lose MYRF, they do not survive. The OPCs retain Myrf, however, so they can proliferate, differentiate, and remyelinate. This produces distinct phases of demyelination and remyelination.

We induce Myrf deletion at eight weeks of age, when the mice are adults. Approximately 10 weeks after deletion, there is widespread demyelination throughout the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerve. By week 24, myelin is returning. These discrete phases let us align biochemical measurements with tissue damage and repair rather than treating demyelination as a single static state. They also correlate with motor ability on the rotarod. Near peak demyelination at 10 to 12 weeks, mice fall from the rotating rod almost immediately; as remyelination proceeds, motor performance recovers.

In the earlier work from the Scanlan laboratory, we induced demyelination at eight weeks of age and began treatment two weeks later with sobetirome or Sob-AM2. Both drugs improved motor function, accelerated remyelination, and increased the extent of myelin recovery. Black-Gold staining, which labels myelin lipids, showed more myelin with treatment.

We then used this model to examine lipid changes. We induced demyelination, started Sob-AM2 treatment, and collected tissue for lipidomic analysis at week 12, near peak demyelination, and week 18, during remyelination. This work was led by my graduate student Nishama De Silva Mohotti, who extracted brain lipids for analysis. The extracted brain lipids formed the small yellow film shown at the bottom of the tube, and we measured a broad panel of lipid classes rather than limiting the analysis to one membrane component.

In the graphs, healthy mice were shown in blue and mice undergoing demyelination and remyelination in orange; darker shades represented drug treatment. This design allowed us to compare disease effects, treatment effects, and their interaction at different biological phases of the model.

At week 12, demyelinated mice had reduced levels across most measured lipid classes. Phosphatidylethanolamine is one example. Similar patterns occurred for phosphatidylinositol, ether-linked phosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidylserine. These included lipids considered primarily structural as well as lipids with signaling functions. At this demyelination time point, there was little drug effect, which was important because it showed that Sob-AM2 did not simply normalize every lipid immediately after damage.

At week 18, during remyelination, Sob-AM2 increased many of these lipids. The drug did not necessarily restore every lipid class completely, but it moved levels upward. Thus, treatment improved both the amount of myelin and aspects of its lipid composition, across lipids with structural and signaling roles.

### Cholesterol esters and microglia

We became particularly interested in cholesterol esters. Approximately 99% of cholesterol in the healthy brain is free, unesterified cholesterol, so cholesterol esters are normally scarce. In the healthy groups, the cholesterol-ester measurements were only small lines near the bottom of the graph. Demyelination caused a striking rise—approximately 30-fold in the example shown. Sob-AM2 reduced that accumulation. This was intriguing because it linked a disease-specific lipid change with thyroid-hormone agonism at the same time that the drug promoted remyelination. It also suggested that cholesterol esters might report how myelin-derived cholesterol is being handled rather than simply how much myelin is present.

During demyelination, cholesterol-rich myelin debris is released into the brain parenchyma and is phagocytosed, initially in large part by microglia. Microglia metabolize lipids and form neutral lipid droplets containing cholesterol esters, diacylglycerols, and triacylglycerols. We began to ask whether thyroid hormone acted directly on microglia to accelerate cholesterol-ester handling.

At the same time, Tom Scanlan and Skylar Ferrara published evidence that thyroid hormone upregulates triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2, or TREM2. TREM2 is a microglial receptor that senses disease-associated debris and helps activate a response. Their work showed that thyromimetics could increase TREM2 expression in lesion-associated myeloid cells in a mouse model of multiple sclerosis. That provided another reason to investigate direct thyroid-hormone action on microglia.

### A demyelination-only model to isolate non-OPC drug effects

In the Plp1-iCKO-Myrf model, Myrf is deleted in mature oligodendrocytes while OPCs drive remyelination. Sob-AM2 is known to act on OPCs, and we suspected that it might also act on microglia, making the two effects difficult to separate.

We therefore developed a model using a Sox10-driven inducible deletion of Myrf across the oligodendrocyte lineage. Myrf is deleted in OPCs as well as differentiating and mature oligodendrocytes. Because the OPCs cannot proceed through normal MYRF-dependent differentiation, these mice cannot mount the same remyelination response. This is essentially a demyelination-only model, allowing us to examine Sob-AM2 effects that are not explained by conventional OPC-driven remyelination. It does not make the model microglia-specific, but it removes the dominant OPC-mediated repair pathway that complicated interpretation in the original model.

After inducing the knockout at eight weeks of age, motor disability developed rapidly. The clinical score worsened and rotarod performance fell nearly to zero. These mice were generally euthanized at the eight-week study time point because they became severely affected. Sob-AM2 treatment began two weeks after induction, with tissues collected at week eight.

The drug did not improve the clinical score or rotarod performance. The clinical score was based on observing whether each mouse could perform defined movements, while the rotarod provided a separate quantitative motor readout. That neither measure improved was not surprising because demyelination was driven by lineage-wide Myrf deletion and a conventional remyelination recovery was not expected. However, Black-Gold staining showed more myelin in Sob-AM2-treated mice, including a visible difference in the central corpus callosum, and the effect was quantifiable. The dissociation between histologic myelin and motor function is one reason we are cautious about calling the effect remyelination.

We do not yet know whether this reflects protection of existing myelin, slowing of demyelination, or an unexpected source of new myelin. Neural stem cells are not targeted by the Sox10 strategy and can generate oligodendrocytes, but their niche is relatively small and the apparent protection is distributed broadly across the brain. We therefore think a protective effect is more likely. Electron microscopy is underway to examine myelin thickness. Remyelinated sheaths are typically thinner, which may help distinguish new from preserved myelin.

### Opposite cholesterol-ester response without remyelination

In the demyelination-remyelination model, Sob-AM2 reduced cholesterol-ester accumulation. In the demyelination-only model, Sob-AM2 instead increased cholesterol esters. Although this was initially surprising—and we were not sure what direction to expect—it suggests a coherent working model. The same treatment may enhance the early uptake and processing of myelin-derived lipid, while the later tissue-level cholesterol-ester measurement depends on whether those lipids can be reused during repair.

Microglia phagocytose cholesterol-rich myelin debris. If thyroid-hormone agonism acts on microglia, it may enhance phagocytosis, lipid metabolism, and formation of cholesterol-ester-rich lipid droplets. In a model with remyelination, those lipids may subsequently be mobilized back toward myelin synthesis. In the demyelination-only model, the remyelination pathway is blocked, so the processed lipid may remain trapped in microglial lipid droplets. This remains our hypothesis rather than a completed mechanistic conclusion.

Whole-brain cholesterol-ester measurements cannot prove that the esters reside in microglia. We therefore used fluorescence-activated cell sorting, or FACS, to isolate microglia from drug-treated and control brains and are using high-resolution mass spectrometry to measure cholesterol esters. The analysis is not complete, so I am not presenting a finalized quantitative treatment comparison. We can already detect cholesterol esters in microglia from diseased mice and not from healthy controls, which is encouraging evidence that the disease-associated signal is present in the cell population of interest. We still need to determine whether there is a reproducible drug effect in the isolated cells.

### Microglial activation and inflammatory state

We also stained for microglial activation, phagocytosis, and inflammatory markers. IBA1, a general microglial marker used here to assess activation, did not differ between drug-treated and untreated mice, suggesting that microglia respond to the disease model regardless of treatment.

We examined two phagocytosis-associated markers, including TREM2, and did not see a terminal-time-point difference. This negative result does not necessarily conflict with a transient effect. Myelin injury may have peaked approximately four weeks earlier, when phagocytic activation would be expected to be greatest. By the terminal collection, both drug-treated and untreated microglia may already have responded strongly. We therefore plan to study earlier time points to look for different marker dynamics.

We also examined CD68, arginase 1, or Arg1, and inducible nitric oxide synthase, or iNOS. Arg1 and iNOS are often used as anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory markers, respectively. The Arg1-to-iNOS balance within CD68-positive cells shifted substantially with Sob-AM2 toward a more pro-inflammatory state.

This was somewhat surprising. One interpretation is that microglia are phagocytosing debris and storing additional cholesterol esters, pushing them into an unhealthy, disease-associated state. We are continuing to investigate this with lipid-droplet staining and other approaches.

### Conclusions

Our data show that thyroid-hormone agonism modulates lipid metabolism during myelin damage and repair. Sob-AM2 changes cholesterol-ester metabolism in a manner that depends on whether remyelination is available, and it alters the microglial inflammatory state. The mechanistic links among phagocytosis, lipid-droplet storage, myelin protection, and remyelination remain active areas of investigation.

I particularly want to acknowledge the work of Matt Zupan and Nishama De Silva Mohotti, along with the Hartley laboratory, collaborators, core facilities, and funders listed in the acknowledgments.

### Q&A

**Moderator:** Thank you for a fantastic and beautifully organized talk. On electron microscopy, will you be able to see lipid droplets in microglia?

**Meredith Hartley:** Lipid-droplet accumulation can be seen by electron microscopy. The difficult part is confidently identifying which cells are microglia. Doing that rigorously may require immunoelectron microscopy with immunogold labeling to identify microglia before looking for lipid deposits.

**Moderator:** Is this drug being used clinically in any human demyelinating disease?

**Meredith Hartley:** Not this particular compound. A related drug is in clinical development for psychiatric disorders as an augmentation therapy to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or SSRIs, and Tom Scanlan is involved in that work. It is not currently being developed for a demyelinating disorder. Multiple sclerosis is difficult to target with remyelination therapies because remyelination outcomes are challenging to identify and measure in a clinical trial. That difficulty in selecting a sensitive outcome is one of the barriers even when the preclinical biology is promising.

**Audience member:** Given the disadvantages of administering triiodothyronine itself, have you compared the receptor agonist directly with T3 to determine whether the effects are the same?

**Meredith Hartley:** We originally included a T3/T4 group, but the dose was too high and caused thyrotoxicity, which illustrates the reason for pursuing selective agonists. More recently, we have been trying to identify a lower, tolerated T3 dose at which an effect might still be detectable. We have identified a tolerated dose, but I do not think we are seeing the same efficacy that we see with the agonist. The comparison is not yet complete, so I would not draw a final equivalence conclusion.
