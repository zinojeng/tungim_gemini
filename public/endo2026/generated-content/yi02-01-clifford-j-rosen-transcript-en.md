## Fecal microbiota and bone material properties

The first study is work involving Christopher J. Hernandez and colleagues, published in *JBMR Plus*. This has been an issue in the bone field for several years, although we have not resolved exactly how the fecal microbiome affects bone. The investigators wanted to establish why conventional DXA or bone-mass measurements show only minimal changes when the fecal microbiome is altered. They asked whether changing the fecal microbiome could alter bone mechanical strength and material properties.

We know that the bones of mice treated with antibiotics to disrupt the gut microbiome differ from those of untreated mice. We also know that co-housing can affect mice because of fecal ingestion across groups, and that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can affect bone. The central questions are how this occurs and what mechanisms are involved.

The proposed mechanisms include circulating hormones, changes in nutrient absorption, alterations in the immune system and bone marrow, and gut microbial dysbiosis. Several factors released after changes in the microbiome could affect the skeleton. Bile acids, for example, have been shown to stimulate osteoblast differentiation and inhibit osteoclastogenesis, principally through suppression of IL-6 and TNF-alpha. Changes in FGF15/19 signaling may also have a role. Fatty acids can signal to the skeleton and may stimulate bone formation, increase osteocalcin and bone remodeling, and suppress bone resorption through several mechanisms. Our laboratory is examining GPR120, a fatty-acid receptor, among other targets.

The study used C57BL/6 mice assigned to microbiome-manipulation groups. Ampicillin and neomycin were provided in drinking water either continuously or during defined periods; another group underwent microbiome reconstitution with FMT, and a delayed-antibiotic group was also included. The investigators assessed fracture toughness, conventional micro-CT measures, Raman spectroscopy, serum biomarkers, and microbiome composition.

Reconstitution by FMT improved bone toughness while shifting bacterial composition toward *Lactobacillus* and away from *Bacteroides*. The change in microbiome composition was striking. TNF-alpha was the serum marker most positively correlated with impaired mineralization and negatively correlated with bone toughness. Thus, a circulating cytokine appeared to have a profound relationship with biomechanical properties and the bone matrix.

The experimental summary was that reconstituting the gut microbiome restored a more favorable microbial profile, reduced TNF-alpha, decreased the mineral-to-matrix ratio, improved mineral crystallinity, and made bones more resistant to fracture by increasing toughness.

The take-home messages were that fecal microbiota can affect the skeleton negatively by increasing bone resorption; the changes may be subtle by DXA, while impairment of mineralization is significant and drives changes in skeletal toughness; and TNF-alpha is one of the key cytokines associated with altered bone microarchitecture during fecal microbial dysbiosis.

## Piezo1-mediated mechanotransduction in osteocytes

The second paper is more complicated. It is elegant work from Yingzi Yang and colleagues, published in *Nature Communications* in November 2025. The investigators asked what controls mechanotransduction, osteocyte maturation, and dendrite development.

I have always wondered how a neonate or infant can mineralize and strengthen the skeleton when there is relatively little movement. Developmental changes in osteocytes and bone nevertheless consolidate the skeleton so that when the infant is ready to walk, the bones do not simply break. This is a question with which the field has struggled for some time.

Mechanical forces are everywhere. Newton described them centuries ago, and Wolff described the law of bone remodeling in the 1890s: the forces placed on the skeleton help determine what the skeleton looks like. Mechanical loading produces a biological response, and bone adapts its size and shape to meet functional needs, although the mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

The work focused on the osteocyte, an area of intense recent investigation. Osteocytes are terminally differentiated from osteoblasts. They possess dendritic processes, communicate with the vasculature and bone marrow, and act as sensors for mechanical loading. Wolff's law therefore places the osteocyte at the center of skeletal mechanosensing.

When mechanical forces are removed, prolonged bed rest and spinal cord injury increase bone resorption. Spaceflight has similar effects. Weight-loss-associated bone loss is also accompanied by skeletal changes that may be related to gravitational unloading. Work from our laboratory shows osteocytic osteolysis with weight-loss-induced bone loss, probably reflecting mechanical unloading. This is of particular interest for research on long-duration space travel, including Mars missions.

Piezo1 is a force-gated ion channel in the osteocyte cell membrane. It enables calcium entry, electrical signaling, and activation of downstream chemical signals in response to force. Human *PIEZO1* variants have been associated with height and osteoporosis, again raising the question of how skeletal sensing operates in utero and during early infancy.

The investigators used a Dmp1-Cre; *Piezo1* conditional-knockout mouse model. Loss of Piezo1 in late osteoblasts and osteocytes reduced bone mass and disrupted osteocyte maturation, with fewer and shorter dendrites and impaired lacunar-canalicular networks. These abnormalities appeared early after birth, suggesting that Piezo1 is critical to developmental osteocyte maturation and matrix stiffening.

RNA-sequencing and cell-model studies, including work in OCY454 cells, showed reduced YAP activity and altered Wnt/beta-catenin signaling in the absence of Piezo1. Intermittent activation of YAP with a Tet-On system largely rescued dendritic and skeletal defects in Piezo1-deficient mice. The data indicate that Piezo1 activates YAP in the Hippo pathway while inhibiting Wnt signaling to promote dendrite formation, matrix stiffening, and osteocyte maturation.

Downstream, CCN1 and CCN2 are secreted YAP targets important to extracellular-matrix biology. Overexpression of CCN1/2 rescued osteocyte dendritic and cortical-bone defects in Piezo1-deficient mice. The proposed pathway is a Piezo1-YAP-CCN1/2-Src signaling axis: mechanical stimulation activates Piezo1 and YAP, increases CCN1/2, activates integrin-Src signaling and actin polymerization, and supports dendrite formation and osteocyte maturation through a positive feed-forward loop. CCN1 enhances matrix formation; the presentation also described CCN2 in relation to regression of fibrosis.

The take-home messages were that mechanotransduction stimulates new bone formation through osteocytes; Piezo1 is especially important for the developmental response to mechanical stimulation during the neonatal period; Piezo1 signals through YAP in the Hippo pathway and modulates Wnt signaling; and CCN1/2 are important mediators of Piezo1-dependent mechanotransduction and possible future therapeutic targets.

## CCN3: a maternal brain hormone that builds bone

The third paper, led by Muriel E. Babey and colleagues and published in *Nature*, changed how the field thinks about factors regulating bone. There have long been clues that the central nervous system can regulate bone; for example, work from Gerard Karsenty and others established sympathetic tone as a driver of bone resorption. This study addressed a distinctive physiological question: why do women who are lactating, with low estrogen and high PTHrP, not sustain permanent bone loss?

Many investigators have searched for circulating or local factors that could explain central control of bone mass. Earlier work identified KISS1 neurons in the arcuate nucleus. When estrogen receptor-alpha was deleted conditionally in these neurons, a striking high-bone-mass phenotype appeared in females. In the presentation's example, bone volume increased from approximately 11% to 64%. This suggested that estrogen signaling in arcuate KISS1 neurons normally restrains a powerful bone-building pathway.

Parabiosis experiments supported the presence of a circulating factor. Femur-transplantation experiments also showed that control femurs from either male or female donors gained bone mass when implanted into mutant female recipients. Thus, although the phenotype originated from female hypothalamic estrogen-receptor signaling, the circulating factor could act on bone from either sex.

Transplantation experiments with osteochondral skeletal stem cells from mutant mice further supported a direct effect on skeletal stem-cell recruitment and osteoblast differentiation. The investigators localized the source to the arcuate nucleus and used single-cell expression studies to identify candidate factors.

The circulating factor was identified as cellular communication network factor 3 (CCN3), a brain-derived hormone expressed by KISS1-positive neurons in the arcuate nucleus. CCN3 stimulated mouse and human skeletal stem-cell activity, increased bone remodeling, and accelerated fracture repair in young and old mice of both sexes. The high-fat-diet experiments helped narrow the candidate list and linked CCN3 expression to the high-bone-mass phenotype.

Recombinant and circulating CCN3 increased bone mass principally through increased bone formation and improved fracture healing. Exogenous CCN3 delivered through systemic or hypothalamic approaches produced marked increases in trabecular bone. During lactation, reducing CCN3 in arcuate KISS1 neurons caused mothers to lose bone and, under low-calcium conditions, fail to sustain their progeny.

The take-home messages were that KISS1 neurons, which are gatekeepers of female reproduction, participate in a brain-bone axis; localized loss of estrogen receptor-alpha in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus markedly enhances bone mass; CCN3 enhances bone formation in a low-estrogen state; and during lactation, CCN3 protects the maternal skeleton from dramatic bone loss while helping sustain the offspring. Low doses may be therapeutically useful, whereas higher doses may produce counter-regulatory or anti-osteogenic effects, so the therapeutic window will be important.

With that, I will conclude. Thank you very much.
