# Glucocorticoid Replacement Therapy in Adrenal Insufficiency: Are There Any Objective Biomarkers?

**Speaker:** Irina Chifu, MD  
**Conference:** ENDO 2026  
**Session:** From Bench to Bedside: Tools for Personalized Care in Adrenal Disorders  
**Reviewed source interval:** 00:00–30:52

## Introduction: the unresolved problem of physiologic replacement

**Moderator:** Good afternoon, everyone. Welcome to the symposium, *From Bench to Bedside: Tools for Personalized Care in Adrenal Disorders*. It is a pleasure to invite the first speaker, Dr. Irina Chifu from University Hospital Würzburg, to present “Glucocorticoid Replacement Therapy in Adrenal Insufficiency: Are There Any Objective Biomarkers?”

**Irina Chifu:** Good afternoon, everyone, and thank you for inviting me to talk about this important but very challenging topic. I am not sure that I will be able to answer the question, but I hope to provide some insight into possible biomarkers and new research ideas. There have also been very interesting sessions on adrenal insufficiency today, before and after lunch, so I hope I will not bore you with slides that may be a little redundant.

My conflicts of interest mostly arise from my desire to improve the management of these patients. Let me take you back a couple of decades. In the past, adrenal insufficiency was more or less a death sentence until the discovery of hydrocortisone, work that was recognized with the Nobel Prize. Survival then improved, but it began to decline again at some point. Even in more recent studies, mortality is significantly increased compared with control cohorts. This is driven mainly by cardiovascular events, malignancy, infections, and adrenal crisis. It makes us think that we are probably not replacing cortisol—and perhaps also fludrocortisone—in a physiologic way.

Patients switch between supraphysiologic cortisol levels and subphysiologic levels. This significantly affects quality of life across physical and mental-health dimensions. It also has socioeconomic consequences through impaired work ability. In our German adrenal-insufficiency registry, a high proportion of patients have a reduced workload, disability pension, part-time employment, or substantial absenteeism during the preceding 12 months.

In an ideal world, we would find an equilibrium between quality of life and the long-term outcomes that replacement therapy can offer. Unfortunately, the balance is shifted, for many reasons. One major reason is that we cannot reproduce the beautiful circadian and ultradian cortisol secretion of healthy adrenal glands. There are fine pulsatile secretions—not only of cortisol but also other adrenal steroids—in serum and plasma and at the tissue level, as Steven Lightman’s group has shown. There is also considerable interindividual and intraindividual variability: the pattern is not identical from day to day or from one person to another. It is not easy to replace.

In our patients, these circadian and ultradian rhythms sometimes look more like a wheel of fortune. Standard hydrocortisone replacement three times daily produces supraphysiologic peaks between subphysiologic troughs, and it cannot reproduce the early-morning cortisol rise that is important to physiology.

Especially in Europe, we now have two modified-release preparations: Plenadren and Chronocort, marketed as Efmody. Plenadren cannot restore the early-morning rise, but it provides a better daytime cortisol curve. Efmody is the first oral preparation that can restore the increase in early-morning cortisol. Neither preparation provides finely tuned pulsatile cortisol secretion. Pump therapy may be able to do so, and has been shown to improve mental and physical health, but this is a double off-label use, is not approved or generally available, and studies in different cohorts have produced inconsistent results. It will take time before we have it. Even if we had it, we would still need biomarkers to adjust the dose, determine how to deliver it, and decide when pulses should be given.

The regulatory system governing glucocorticoid secretion and action is complicated. We probably will not find one biomarker or bioparameter that lets us fine-tune physiologic replacement. Regulation occurs at the hypothalamic and tissue levels and is influenced by other pituitary hormones, other replacement therapies, sex, BMI, and age.

My vision is that we need something like the diabetes model for therapy management and surveillance: a parameter that assesses long-term cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, analogous to glycated hemoglobin, together with parameters that help us adjust circadian exposure, analogous to continuous glucose monitoring. These measures must be evaluated against outcomes of both excessive and insufficient cortisol exposure: cardiometabolic risk, adrenal crisis, hospitalization, and quality of life. It is a difficult task.

## Clinical, blood-pressure, saliva, urine, and hair measures

What can we use now? I will begin with the easiest routine clinical parameters. We all assess them, but we know that they are not very specific.

A 2006 study by Wiebke Arlt and colleagues examined serum cortisol levels in patients receiving hydrocortisone. The investigators obtained three timed samples after the morning dose on two or three separate occasions, then compared them with a clinical score based on the signs of under- and over-replacement that we routinely evaluate. The score included weight changes, glucose, electrolytes, blood pressure, and subjective signs and symptoms. Patients with a score above +2 were classified as over-replaced, those below −2 as under-replaced, and all others as adequately replaced. Their cortisol measurements showed a high degree of overlap among the under-, over-, and adequately replaced groups. The cortisol levels and clinical score might identify those who are markedly over- or under-replaced when they walk into the clinic, but they will not reliably identify subtle over- or under-replacement.

We also examined these clinical scores in our registry cohort. We compared patients who had never experienced adrenal crisis, patients with a crisis frequency close to the mean reported in the literature, and patients with frequent crises. Again, symptoms overlapped considerably and did not identify the vulnerable group.

Perhaps we are looking for some parameters in the wrong compartment. Sodium is present not only in plasma or serum but also in tissues such as skin, muscle, and bone. Tissue sodium correlates with cardiometabolic outcomes such as elevated blood pressure. With the help of sodium MRI, which is fortunately available in our clinic, we evaluated relative tissue-sodium signal intensity in patients at first diagnosis. Tissue sodium was significantly lower at diagnosis than at the first follow-up after replacement therapy had begun. It rose toward the level of healthy controls, which was good. In the longer term, however, patients with chronic adrenal insufficiency had significantly higher tissue sodium than matched controls, and tissue sodium correlated with replacement therapy, suggesting over-replacement. Sodium MRI is not feasible for routine dose assessment, but its findings might correlate with blood pressure.

In a registry analysis now underway, patients receiving standard replacement have a high prevalence of nighttime hypertension, including those who take no antihypertensive medication. Patients with elevated ambulatory blood pressure also have higher glucocorticoid exposure within recommended dose ranges; this is reflected in urinary cortisol and free plasma metanephrine measurements. Twenty-four-hour ambulatory blood-pressure monitoring may therefore be a practical way to assess disruption of circadian physiology and help adjust therapy.

Salivary profiles offer another way to evaluate circadian rhythms. A small prospective study of 20 patients with Addison disease compared salivary cortisol day curves with healthy-control profiles. Physicians could change twice-daily therapy to three-times-daily treatment and then adjust the dose to bring the salivary profile closer to the physiologic range. This produced more physiologic salivary cortisol profiles and was associated with improvement in sleep disturbance and fatigue.

The CHAMPAIN study, discussed in earlier sessions today, was a double-blind, double-dummy, randomized crossover study comparing Plenadren with Chronocort. Its primary outcome was restoration of early-morning serum cortisol, which was achieved with Chronocort. Chronocort also produced a more physiologic cortisol profile and improved fatigue and several measures of health-related quality of life. Salivary profiling may therefore be a useful biomarker for treatment assessment.

Urinary cortisol cannot characterize the diurnal pattern, but it can assess cumulative daily glucocorticoid exposure. In a randomized 12-week crossover study, patients were switched from conventional three-times-daily hydrocortisone to dual-release hydrocortisone, Plenadren, while urinary steroids were compared with healthy controls. The initially elevated urinary steroid profile fell substantially and moved toward the healthy-control range. It correlated well with total glucocorticoid exposure, including plasma exposure later in the day. The elevated activity of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 also fell significantly, although not fully to the healthy-control level. This was not perfect, but it was better.

Hair cortisol is currently used more often in patients with Cushing syndrome, but it could also measure long-term glucocorticoid exposure in adrenal insufficiency. Hair grows approximately one centimeter per month, so hair cortisol could be analogous to glycated hemoglobin. In a study of 64 adults with adrenal insufficiency and 64 matched controls, hair cortisol was significantly higher in patients. Within the patient group, higher values were associated with glucocorticoid exposure and metabolic or cardiovascular strain, including BMI, waist circumference, and blood pressure; lower values were associated with more severe fatigue and pain, which may indicate under-replacement.

We also measured hair cortisol in two special groups: patients with high adrenal-crisis frequency and those with low crisis frequency from a prospective study of adrenal crisis. We were looking for markers of crisis vulnerability. Patients with frequent crises were receiving significantly higher glucocorticoid doses, still had recurrent crises, and had higher hair cortisol than patients with low crisis frequency. In this vulnerable group, hair cortisol probably will not be sufficient to guide cumulative glucocorticoid exposure. We need measures of circadian exposure and dynamics.

These patients came from a prospective cohort followed for two years. The only predictor of recurrent adrenal crisis was a crisis that had already occurred. This was a well-educated cohort; patients adjusted their doses according to recommendations and began the study on higher glucocorticoid doses, so they were not underdosed. When we reassessed this cohort after 16 years, we were astonished that the strongest predictor of recurrent crisis remained a crisis during the prospective study. People who have crises are the people who will have more crises.

After 16 years, however, we saw hints that a more circadian replacement pattern, though still imperfect, may reduce risk. A multiple-dose regimen appeared favorable compared with a single daily dose. When crises were divided into hospitalized, more severe events and those treated in an ambulatory setting, the risk of hospitalized crisis also appeared lower among patients receiving long-acting glucocorticoids in multiple doses. In this vulnerable cohort, moving toward a more circadian treatment pattern may have an advantage.

## FKBP5, multi-omics, and digital biomarkers

Ideally, one parameter would reflect both short-term and long-term glucocorticoid exposure. FKBP5 is interesting because it is among the most strongly glucocorticoid-induced genes. FKBP5 is a co-chaperone of the glucocorticoid receptor and participates in short-loop negative feedback that protects against prolonged glucocorticoid exposure. Glucocorticoids increase FKBP5 expression and reduce methylation at glucocorticoid-responsive regions, thereby increasing its activity.

Several regions across FKBP5 contain glucocorticoid-response elements, and multiple studies show that glucocorticoid exposure changes their methylation. FKBP5 mRNA also changes in a dose-dependent manner in adrenal insufficiency. In one study, 10 patients with autoimmune Addison disease received 100 mg intravenous hydrocortisone. FKBP5 expression rose markedly within two hours and then declined over the next several hours. It may therefore be useful for short-term changes in exposure, although we still lack data under more physiologic concentrations and daily replacement. FKBP5 mRNA expression also normalizes toward healthy-control levels after curative surgery for Cushing syndrome.

FKBP5 methylation may be a marker of longer-term exposure. A study of healthy participants who collected awakening and bedtime salivary cortisol for more than 30 days found significant negative correlations with methylation at CpG sites in two intronic glucocorticoid-response regions. The correlations were strongest for cortisol exposure during the first three weeks and weakest for the most recent week, suggesting that methylation reflects chronic rather than immediate exposure.

We performed an extensive literature review of glucocorticoid-responsive FKBP5 regions shown to change methylation after glucocorticoid exposure. Much of the evidence came from supraphysiologic dexamethasone exposure, animal models, or cell cultures rather than physiologic human replacement. We evaluated these regions in registry patients, looked for polymorphisms that might influence methylation, and correlated methylation with replacement dose and cortisol profiles in saliva, urine, and hair. We also compared patients with adrenal insufficiency with patients who had cortisol-producing adrenal adenomas.

We observed significant negative correlations between FKBP5 methylation and glucocorticoid replacement dose, urinary cortisol, and salivary cortisol. The published analysis included 120 patients with chronic adrenal insufficiency—72 primary and 48 secondary—and 64 patients with cortisol-producing adrenal adenomas. I am showing only two regions because otherwise I could fill 20 slides with correlations.

I found the dose-pattern comparison particularly interesting. Only 10 patients in our cohort took the entire dose once daily, so the result was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, patients taking the same total glucocorticoid dose all at once in the morning—a less physiologic regimen—showed lower methylation across several regions than patients who divided that dose throughout the day. Methylation may distinguish patients receiving replacement in a more circadian pattern, but this needs confirmation in a larger cohort.

We were surprised by the comparison with cortisol-producing adenomas. We expected patients with adrenal insufficiency to fall somewhere between cortisol-producing and nonfunctioning adrenal adenomas. Instead, patients with adrenal insufficiency had similar or even lower FKBP5 methylation than those with cortisol-producing adenomas. This suggests substantial biologic exposure to glucocorticoid peaks even when replacement doses are within recommended ranges. We are examining a larger cohort now.

Most studies I have presented, including ours, evaluated patients receiving replacement regimens that are known not to be physiologic. It is difficult to identify valid biomarkers of treatment quality under a nonphysiologic treatment regimen.

One randomized crossover study examined glucocorticoid-response markers under controlled exposure in 10 patients with adrenal insufficiency. The patients underwent a 24-hour saline-withdrawal phase and a 24-hour hydrocortisone-infusion phase, with the order crossed over after two to five weeks. Investigators performed multi-omic analyses: peripheral-blood-mononuclear-cell transcriptomics, adipose-tissue transcriptomics, plasma microRNAs, and serum metabolites. Nine genes changed in the same direction in blood and adipose tissue. DCK was especially interesting because it is related to glucocorticoid sensitivity. A hypernetwork analysis identified miR-122-5p as a promising marker of exposure; it correlated with cortisol exposure and FKBP5 expression.

To assess dynamic changes in glucocorticoid demand, we may also need digital biomarkers that show, in real time, whether a patient should increase the dose. An annual or semiannual clinic visit with one blood draw does not capture stress vulnerability throughout the day. We are planning an app-based ecological momentary assessment in our registry patients. We want to measure daily performance, stress dosing, symptoms, emergencies or adrenal crises, and especially fatigue over a longer period, then incorporate these data into clinical decision-support systems.

Mayo Clinic is also running an observational biomarker study using an Oura Ring and longitudinal symptom, quality-of-life, sleep, cognition, glucocorticoid-dose, and medical-event data to predict periods of illness. It is not easy to send patients across the ocean to Würzburg, but they can go to Irina Bancos at Mayo Clinic. This is important work. The ability to predict crisis vulnerability in the short term could be life-changing.

## Conclusions

We have much work to do in biomarkers, digital health, prevention, and clinical outcomes. We need a physiologic setting in which to discover good biomarkers, but we also need biomarkers in order to make treatment physiologic. It is not easy.

Some parameters can be used now, although they vary in specificity and sensitivity. We will always use routine clinical and biochemical measures. They can identify a patient who is markedly over- or under-replaced, but the patients in between are much more difficult. Urinary steroid profiles may help assess treatment exposure in some situations. Hair cortisol has limitations: it is easier if you have long hair like I do, and hair coloring or the absence of sufficient hair can make it unavailable or difficult to interpret. Twenty-four-hour ambulatory blood pressure and salivary steroid profiles may be more appropriate for assessing circadian glucocorticoid exposure.

For the other promising parameters, we need collaborative, interventional, long-term studies in large cohorts with strong clinical outcomes. These studies must be conducted in more physiologic treatment settings. Thank you very much for your attention, and thank you to my wonderful supporting team in Würzburg and around the world.

## Questions and answers

**Moderator:** Thank you for this lovely talk. We have six minutes for questions. I see people lining up; otherwise, I have a burning question of my own.

**Audience member:** Thank you, Irina. We recently completed a survey of adrenal crisis in Norway. Over the last 24 years, we did not see an increase in severe crises, but we saw an increase in incipient or milder crises without hemodynamic instability. I think that is probably a good thing because we educate patients and want them to come to the hospital. When we examine crisis frequency, it may not be bad that patients seek care early. Crises may be inevitable. The learning point is that if you reach the hospital soon enough, you survive; the danger is when you are not at the hospital.

**Irina Chifu:** Exactly. Not all ambulatory crises in our study were treated in the hospital; we also recorded those treated at home. In the study we hope to publish soon, after implementation of a national education program for adrenal-insufficiency crisis prevention, the prevalence of hospitalized crises stayed more or less the same, while ambulatory-treated crises—where patients could return home—went up. I did not show this because of time. The main message is that patients should seek care. That is what we teach them. They may be discharged the same day, rather than needing to remain overnight.

**Wenyu Huang, Northwestern University:** Excellent presentation. Most of us treat primary hypothyroidism, and fortunately we have TSH, so we do not give everyone a full replacement dose. Yet, when treating secondary adrenal insufficiency, some patients still have endogenous cortisol production if cortisol is measured in the morning before the hydrocortisone dose. Many of us nevertheless give a full replacement dose and then adjust it according to symptoms. I wish there were a marker. Are studies evaluating endogenous glucocorticoid production so that treatment could be graded, as it is in thyroid disease? If someone has endogenous cortisol, why do we give a full replacement dose?

**Irina Chifu:** There is no really good prospective interventional study. We examined this in our cohort. Patients with higher replacement doses have lower levels of endogenous steroid precursors, probably because of the replacement dose. We did not see correlations with crisis frequency. But this is a very good point: residual adrenal function definitely plays a role.

**Moderator:** You showed data associating higher glucocorticoid doses with more adrenal crises. We found the same in our survey. Why does this occur? Are patients anxious because they have had a previous crisis and therefore think they need a higher dose, even though that has nothing to do with future crisis? Or does it reflect reduced glucocorticoid sensitivity?

**Irina Chifu:** Is the patient anxious? Is the doctor anxious? Do we increase the dose prophylactically? That is what we used to think. It is not easy to determine. In Stefanie Hahner’s prospective study and in the cohort we have now followed for the long term, patients who experience crises start on higher doses. We do not know everything that occurred beforehand. They stay at a higher dose or increase it further and still experience crises. They adjust their doses during stress and follow the rules.

In the registry, patients with frequent crises also have more gastrointestinal disease and other comorbidities that may impair absorption. We see differences in polymorphisms of the glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptors that may influence sensitivity and blood pressure. There may be several explanations; it will not be just one.

**Gordon Cutler:** I am thinking of unfortunate cases colleagues have told me about. One is a reminder that adrenal insufficiency is not necessarily all-or-none at onset; an autoimmune attack develops over time. In one woman, the clinician started cortisone replacement alone. I have seen a similar problem in oncology consultations when adrenal tissue was destroyed by cancer and clinicians treated only the cortisol deficiency. In one case, the glucocorticoid dose reached something like 60 mg without recognition that aldosterone deficiency also required treatment. Catecholamine deficiency may play a role as well.

Physicians focus so much on cortisol that we may not adjust fludrocortisone carefully enough or make sure renin is in the appropriate range. The woman treated only with glucocorticoid did well for several years. As aldosterone deficiency worsened, she developed salt craving. Near the end she was eating almost nothing but potato chips to obtain enough salt. She then developed an intercurrent illness and died. The clinician called me afterward, understandably distressed, because he had never reconsidered the need to add Florinef. It is not only a matter of adding mineralocorticoid; the dose also needs to be correct, guided in part by renin. Do you have a short comment?

**Irina Chifu:** I can only agree. We focus mainly on cortisol. Data from the European Adrenal Insufficiency Registry, EU-AIR, show that patients with recurrent crises have findings consistent with less adequate mineralocorticoid replacement, including higher renin. It is a very good point.
