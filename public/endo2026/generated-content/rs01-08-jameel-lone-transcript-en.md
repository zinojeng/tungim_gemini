## Discovery of BRP3

**Jameel Barkat Lone:** Hello and welcome. My name is Jameel Lone, and I am from Katrin Svensson's lab at Stanford. Today I will share the discovery of a novel peptide that regulates appetite—before we all go to lunch.

Peptide hormones have historically been discovered through classical biochemical purification. However, many peptides are small, present at low abundance, and have short half-lives, so many likely remain hidden. To uncover these signals, we adopted a strategy guided by human genetics and identified BRINP3—bone morphogenetic protein/retinoic acid-inducible neural-specific protein 3—as an interesting candidate associated with human body mass index (BMI). BRINP3 contains prohormone-like features, but its metabolic function was unexplored.

We tested peptides derived from this precursor protein in mice and found one that significantly reduced caloric intake. We named it BRP3. To evaluate its therapeutic potential in obesity, we chronically treated diet-induced obese mice for 14 days and observed significant weight loss. Combining BRP3 with glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) therapy produced an additive effect on weight loss. Most importantly, we did not observe nausea-like or aversive responses of the type associated with many approved weight-loss drugs.

## Hypothalamic mechanism

The brain is the control center for feeding behavior. We therefore examined which areas were activated by BRP3 and found striking activation of key satiety-regulating hypothalamic regions, particularly the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVH).

Using FosTRAP-guided chemogenetic inhibition, we inhibited the hypothalamic neurons activated by BRP3 and observed rescue of BRP3's anorectic effect. This suggests that hypothalamic circuits are essential to its function. We also found that BRP3 suppresses appetite independently of the GLP-1 receptor.

On that note, I will end with the hope that our BRP3 remains silent for a few days so that we can enjoy the food of Chicago. Thank you.

## Audience Q&A

**Audience member:** How do you test nausea in a mouse?

**Jameel Barkat Lone:** We performed approximately 12 behavioral tests, including conditioned taste aversion (CTA) and sucrose-preference testing. The results were negative. We were particularly convinced by the conditioned-aversion test, in which lithium chloride (LiCl) served as the positive control.

**Audience member:** Do you have evidence that the peptide crosses the blood-brain barrier?

**Jameel Barkat Lone:** We have not labeled the peptide in a way that would allow us to track it in the brain. We do, however, have evidence for a central effect: when we administered the peptide directly into the brain by stereotaxic intracerebroventricular delivery, it suppressed appetite.

**Audience member:** Do you know where the receptors are located in the body or which organ systems should be examined?

**Jameel Barkat Lone:** We are working on that. This peptide has only recently been identified, and we do not yet know its receptor.

**Audience member:** Do you know the mechanism of action—for example, whether it activates pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons?

**Jameel Barkat Lone:** We do not yet know the specific neuronal population. We know that hypothalamic neurons are involved because, in FosTRAP mice, inhibiting the hypothalamic neurons activated by BRP3 rescued the anorectic effect.
