**Dr. Jain:** Yeah, if you are wondering why this talk is part of this symposium, it was not supposed to be. So I do apologize that the topic is slightly off from the rest of the symposium, but I hope you guys enjoy it. So I titled this Race and Ethnicity in Osteoporosis: Time to Reconsider. 

These are my disclosures, and my objectives today are to talk about racial and ethnic differences in fragility fractures, talk about some of the possible causal mechanisms of those differences, and then talk about FRAX at the end. 

### Clinical case and race-specific FRAX estimates

So I wanted to start with a case. This is a 70-year-old woman who presents to your clinic for follow-up. She has type 2 diabetes for the last 20 years, history of a stroke three years ago, and hypertension. Her medications include insulin glargine, insulin aspart, olmesartan, chlorthalidone, and aspirin. She had a routine bone density test ordered by her primary care doctor, and she reports one fall but no fractures, and she's never been treated for osteoporosis. You can see her BMD T-scores listed there. And if you're a clinician, you might think about calculating this patient's risk of having a fracture. In the U.S. and in many parts of the world, FRAX is the gold standard in calculating fracture risk. The output is a 10-year risk of a major osteoporotic fracture or a hip fracture. You can see all the risk factors in the tool there. Inherent to the tool in the United States is race. So the tool actually requires the input of White, Hispanic, Black, or Asian into the tool. And depending on which race this patient is, you actually get four very different outputs. And so you see for the White patient, the risk is going to be 16% for a major osteoporotic fracture and 3.9% for a hip fracture. You can see for all of the other racial groups, the risk is about half, and that difference can change your treatment decision-making. So if you follow most osteoporosis guidelines, they recommend treating women if they have a hip fracture probability of greater than 3%. So if you go back to this first case and if you follow those guidelines to a T, you would treat the White woman in this case, but not if she were Black, Hispanic, or Asian. And that discrepancy has brought on scrutiny, rightfully so. 

Many of you may be familiar with this New England Journal article in 2020 called Hidden in Plain Sight. This article identified many different tools throughout medicine that utilize race. In particular, the authors of this article were concerned about the use of race when the outcome is diverting resources away from racial and ethnic minorities. So this included FRAX in the article, and you can see there, they say the lower risk reported for non-White women may delay intervention with osteoporosis therapy. The authors go on to say it's not prohibited to use race or ethnicity in clinical algorithms, but that you should ask three questions. So the first question is, is the need for race correction based on robust evidence? The second question is, is there a plausible causal mechanism for the difference by race? And would implementing this race correction relieve or exacerbate health inequities? So I wanted to use those three questions. I think they're good questions to try to think this through for FRAX. And then at the end, I was going to talk about some of the studies looking at performance of FRAX in racial and ethnic minorities. 

### Evidence for racial and ethnic differences in fracture rates

So I'll start with this first question. 

To take a quick step back, what is race? So race is a social construct. It serves as a proxy for national origin, cultural practices, and a wide variety of social determinants of health. There is known to be more genetic diversity within racial groups than between racial groups. 

And then just a little bit about FRAX. So many of you are familiar with FRAX. It was originally developed in 46,000 men and women, mostly European. There was one U.S. site, which was Rochester. And then it was validated in a very large cohort of over 230,000 people. And the validation did include more U.S. sites, including the Women's Health Initiative. It's a proprietary algorithm, meaning we don't know the weights of each risk factor in the tool, but it is free to calculate for an individual, and it's been available since 2008. It is country specific, and as many of you know, it's been widely adopted into medical society guidelines. So to go back to this question, what is the evidence that fracture rates differ in Black, Hispanic, and Asian people in the U.S.? So the evidence is pretty good. So this is from the National Osteoporosis Risk Assessment Study. This was a study throughout the country of postmenopausal women. You can see very clearly the Black and the Asian women in this study had much lower fracture rates than the White women in this study. So that's very clear, and it's kind of regardless of age, of decade, as you can see there. In this study, Hispanic women actually did not fracture at substantially different rates than the White women. However, that has not been a uniform finding in the Women's Health Initiative, for example. Hispanic women fractured at about half the rate of White women. So already you can see some discrepancies in the evidence. So these are two of the largest prospective studies we have looking at fracture rates, and they have very different results in Hispanic women. If you look at some observational work, this is from Nicole Wright's group looking at Medicare data, you see that the truth may be somewhere in the middle. In this study of Medicare patients, hip fracture rates in Hispanic women were between the rate of White women and Asian and Black women. Why the intra-ethnicity variation? I suspect it's related to the fact that fracture rates differ widely throughout the world. If you look at countries like Mexico and Brazil and Chile, et cetera, you can see very different fracture rates throughout the world, and that may explain the intra-ethnicity variation. So to answer this question more briefly, is the need for race correction based on robust evidence? I think we have pretty robust evidence in Black women and Asian women, but maybe not so for Hispanic or for men. 

### Bone density, microarchitecture, and possible mechanisms

About the next question: Is there a plausible causal mechanism for this difference? Asked more simply, why do fracture rates vary so much by race? Some of it's bone density. Black women are known to have higher bone density. You can see them here at the top. They have higher bone density than other racial and ethnic groups. However, Asian and Hispanic women, not so. And so clearly it's more than just bone density. This is another way of looking at the same thing, where this is broken down by BMD category, and you can see across racial groups, even within the same BMD category, there are very different rates of fracture. So I wanted to just go through a little bit and try to explain why. So the short answer is we think it's bone structure or bone quality. So I just wanted to show some studies looking at bone structure. I wanted to start with Black women. This is a study using HR-pQCT, high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography. This is an advanced imaging modality allowing for very detailed information about cortical and trabecular bone. This is from SWAN, the Study of Women at the Menopausal Transition. And I love this picture because you have these two nice cross sections representative of Black and White women. And you don't have to be an expert in this modality to look at these two bones and say there's very big differences, particularly in the cortical bone right here. You can see the Black women have far superior and thicker cortical bone than the White women. In this study, they also found lower cortical porosity, higher estimated bone stiffness and failure load. There's also a thought that Black patients may have more favorable hip geometry that protects against hip fractures. Black patients have higher bone density, superior bone structure, and lower fracture risk. 

What about Asian women? So I mentioned earlier, Asian women have lower bone density than White women. This is actually a bit of an artifact on DXA. DXA is a 2D imaging modality. It incorporates bone size as part of its measurement, and so smaller bones measure lower on a bone density test. So Asians have smaller bone size, and that is why they have lower bone density measured. In this HR-pQCT study, they found Asian women had higher cortical thickness and cortical density, but fewer and thicker trabeculae. So that is thought to lead to superior bone structure and lower fracture risk. I also just wanted to mention a very important clinical point, is that Asian patients have a higher rate of medication-related atypical femur fractures. These are dreaded side effects from our antiresorptive drugs. Despite the name of atypical, they have a very prototypical appearance that you can see here. We know the risk increases with long-term bisphosphonate use, and some patients develop a prodrome. And we have found, and this is one of the largest AFF studies that there are, found a much higher rate of AFFs in Asian women as compared to other racial and ethnic groups, so six- to seven-fold higher rates. So just to kind of highlight some of the complexity here, Asian patients have lower measured bone density than White patients, but lower fracture rates and higher rates of adverse effects from bisphosphonates. Just to highlight some of the complexity. 

What about Hispanic women? So we have the least information about Hispanic women. This data comes from NHANES, looking at in the blue bars here is the rate of osteoporosis. You can see that Mexican women had higher rates of osteoporosis than other groups. But again, there is thought to be a lot of intra-ethnicity variation. This is a study looking at bone density across different countries. The top country in the diamonds is the U.S. You can see Cuba is at the top, and then all the other countries vary quite a lot. In terms of bone structure, bone quality, again, we have fewer studies. This was one study looking at Caribbean-origin Hispanic women, and they actually found inferior bone structure in this study with lower trabecular density and higher trabecular separation. So definitely the least information about the Hispanic women, but we know that Mexican women have lower bone density and there are higher rates of osteoporosis, but there is a lot unknown. So again, the second question, I think, again, the answer is yes, we have some mechanisms that could explain the differences by race. 

### Equity implications of race correction in FRAX

And let's go to this third question. I think this is the most difficult question: Does implementing race correction in FRAX relieve or exacerbate health disparities? 

And just to give some numbers to this, so FRAX adjusts by race-specific fracture rates in the U.S. These are fairly sizable adjustments. So as an example, a White woman, if her risk was 10%, a Black woman with otherwise equivalent risk factors would have a risk of 4.3%, so less than half of what's calculated for a White woman. And that's in the context of real disparities in osteoporosis care. We know Black women are less likely to get a bone density screening test. We know that treatment rates are very low in Black patients. This is a population-based study in the U.S. looking at treatment rates, and if you look at the patients deemed high risk, White women were treated at rates anywhere from 17 to 26%, but Black women were treated anywhere from 2 to 9%. So I think we can all agree that treatment rates are generally very low for osteoporosis, but they were particularly low in the Black patients in this study. 

And then after fracture, there are also gaps in care. So studies have shown that Black and Asian women had higher risks of delayed surgery after hip fracture. After hip fracture, Black and Hispanic patients had lower functional outcomes and were more likely to be discharged to home. And in Medicare patients with osteoporosis, after fracture, Black patients had worse outcomes, including worse mortality. So, by having a race correction in FRAX, how might this affect disparities? So I tried to look at this by looking at treatment eligibility using NHANES, the population-based U.S. sample. I actually used the Endocrine Society osteoporosis guidelines. So the guidelines suggest treatment for those with T-scores of minus 2.5 or lower, those with high FRAX, and those with a prior hip or spine fracture. Because there are three different criteria, changing FRAX actually didn't have as much of an impact as you might suspect. So in this, for example, just focusing here on the women, with the race-based FRAX, 9.5% of Black women qualified for therapy. With race—this is called race-neutral FRAX—trying to take the race component out of FRAX, 11.5% qualified for therapy. So a little bit less than you might expect. And it's also unclear how much providers use FRAX versus BMD T-score. Nonetheless, you're reducing treatment eligibility. I think there's a very good chance you are decreasing treatment rates in Black, Hispanic, and Asian women and men, and that could exacerbate these health disparities. 

### Discrimination and calibration of FRAX

So finally, I wanted to talk about the performance of FRAX. So FRAX is out there. It's been out there for over 15 years. What is the performance of FRAX in racial and ethnic minorities? Are there differences? 

So before I get to that, I just wanted to mention these two aspects of tool performance. One is discrimination, and the other is calibration. So discrimination is the idea of sorting your patients from highest to lowest risk. So if you have a risk tool, you sort the patients by their risk score from highest to lowest risk. Is your tool identifying those highest-risk patients who go on to have a fracture? Many of you may be familiar with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. This is often depicted as an AUC, or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. An AUC of 0.5 is a coin flip. That's along this dotted line here. 1.0 is perfect. That hugs the y-axis and the x-axis. The idea here is that if, for example, you were to take a case patient and a control patient out of your cohort, what is the chance that your case patient has a higher risk score than your control? So you want that to be true. 0.7 is often used as a reasonable target of discrimination. 

Calibration is very different. It's actually looking at how many events occur compared to the predicted rates of the event from your tool. So this is often expressed as an observed-to-predicted ratio and as a calibration curve. So as an example, if you have 100 patients and your tool predicts a 10% risk of a fracture, 10 fractures, but there are actually 20 fractures, your observed-to-predicted would be 2, so twice as many fractures as predicted. In these calibration curves, the dotted line here represents perfect calibration. Observed here is on the y-axis. So if you're above this dotted line, your tool is underestimating the number of fractures that are occurring. If you're below the dotted line, you're overestimating the number of fractures. 

So with that background, I just wanted to show you what the evidence is in racial and ethnic minorities. Wanted to start with the Women's Health Initiative. This is really the first study that looked at this. You can see this is discrimination, and I mentioned to you earlier 0.7 is a reasonable target. These were younger postmenopausal women, ages 50 to 64, and somewhat lower fracture risk. In this study, in all of the racial groups, including the White women, FRAX performed somewhat abysmally, which is very inconsistent with some prior data. Looking at calibration, though, calibration was okay. Most groups had close to a 1.0 observed-to-predicted. There was a little bit of underestimation in the Black women, but overall the calibration was okay in this study. The problem is, you know, the Women's Health Initiative included interventional components. There were some exclusions in WHI for contraindications to estrogen, and it had been really the only cohort that had looked at FRAX in minority patients for a long time. So we've been trying to—and there were no studies in men—so we have been trying to look at other cohorts to try to improve the amount of data in this area. 

### FRAX performance in diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis

I want to just highlight two studies. The first study is actually a disease-specific study in diabetes. So no previously published study has looked at racial subgroups in the U.S. with diabetes. We collaborated with Kaiser Permanente Northern California to look at FRAX in about 97,000 patients with well-characterized diabetes, and these were the patients. So very different than the WHI study I showed you, in particular age. So these patients were over 65, so they had much higher risk of fracture. So these were the patients. The first column is the full cohort and then broken down by fracture status. You can see they were about age 74 on average, about 50-50 men and women, and very good racial diversity. You can see 20,000 Asian women and men, 14,000 Hispanic women and men, and then almost 10,000 Black patients. Diabetes duration also spanned the gamut. You can see some patients had very short duration diabetes and some had very long. So what about, how did FRAX do? So in terms of discriminating, again, this is sorting of highest to lowest risk patients, FRAX did fine. So FRAX for major osteoporotic fracture, it was around 0.7 for all racial groups. For hip fracture, around 0.8. So again, very different than WHI, but this is good news, I think, that FRAX performed well in terms of discrimination. We found problems in calibration, so observed-to-predicted. So this is for the White women and men here at the top. You can see observed-to-predicted was about 20% higher than predicted for White women and men. We felt that that was a diabetes effect. But look at the Hispanic women. So there were 80% more fractures in Hispanic women than predicted, Hispanic men 40% higher fractures than predicted. These are Black women and Black men. For Black women, calibration was close to expected even with diabetes; the observed-to-predicted ratio was 1.1. But the Black men, 50% more fractures than predicted. So we did look at hip fractures. I'm going to save that just for time's sake. So FRAX demonstrated good discrimination in all racial groups, but substantially underpredicted fractures in Hispanic women, Hispanic men, and Black men with diabetes. It's interesting that the direction of the errors were the opposite of the actual adjustments made in FRAX for race. 

Another study that came out earlier this year was a disease-specific study in rheumatoid arthritis, and again, they found relatively similar things. So they looked at calibration slightly differently, but you can see this is for Black women. This is a dotted line representing perfect calibration, and you can see that observed rates in Black women were above the line of perfect calibration. The same was true for Hispanic women. So I think a very reasonable question is, is this a disease-specific phenomenon? I showed you diabetes and RA. 

### Race-neutral FRAX

To try to better understand this, we looked at FRAX at the University of Chicago in patients with diabetes, and we included a control group with hypertension. Hypertension is the most common comorbidity in the United States, using electronic health record data, and we hypothesized that a race-neutral FRAX could perform better than the actual FRAX. 

Race-neutral FRAX, I don't want to get into the weeds too much here, but the idea is that you're trying to take the race component out. So it's a weighted sum based on what portion of the population they are. So just as an example, this patient that I showed you, this is a 70-year-old Black woman. If you look at U.S. Census data, 75.4% of women in this age range are White. So basically, you would take 75.4% and multiply it by the White FRAX, then use 10.3% Black, 5.1% Asian, and 9.2% Hispanic weights, and so on. And that's what we called race-neutral FRAX. For this particular patient, her risk was 14.2% when calculated this way, as opposed to 7.4% with Black FRAX. So this is a busy slide. I just want to orient you here. So at the top, we have number of patients observed fracture rate, predicted fracture rate adjusted for time, and then observed-to-predicted ratio. You're shooting for a ratio around one. And then this is broken down by diabetes and hypertension status and race and sex. I did want to point out that this was a predominantly Black cohort. You can see by far the largest numbers in the Black patients. How did FRAX do? So in the White patients, the ratios here are very close to one, indicating good performance regardless of diabetes or hypertension. However, in the Black women and the Black men, we saw much higher rates than predicted. So as much as 30% up to 130% higher observed than predicted rates. And Hispanic, again, small numbers here, but relatively high observed-to-predicted ratios. When we looked at race-neutral FRAX, there were some improvements. So for Black women, it went from underpredicting substantially to mild overprediction. Black men, race-neutral FRAX performed quite well. But then in the Hispanics, even with this race-neutral FRAX, you still have pretty substantial underprediction. So this does not solve all problems. 

As another point, we found that comorbidity played a really big role. This is low versus high comorbidity scores. We saw with low comorbidity scores, FRAX performed a bit better, although still some problems in Black men and Hispanic people, versus high comorbidity really led to some problems in the performance of FRAX. So race-neutral FRAX, again, did a little bit better in some areas, but far from perfect. 

### Clinical implications and conclusions

So just to summarize these two studies, you know, we found relatively similar things. In a larger diabetes population, we found FRAX substantially underpredicted fractures in Hispanic women and Black men, and moderate underprediction in Hispanic men. And then in an urban Chicago population with diabetes or hypertension, we found FRAX substantially underpredicted fractures in Black and Hispanic women and men. A race-neutral FRAX performed somewhat better. So I think there's a lot of unanswered questions in this area. Is FRAX properly calibrated? Could race adjustment actually worsen disparities that already exist? And I also think a missing component is provider and patient perspectives. If we include race in the risk tool, is that impacting the trust we have with our patients? 

So just to kind of leave you with how should you treat your patient, this comes from a review we published in JCEM, and we tried to incorporate race into how you think about osteoporosis therapy decision-making. So some patients get everything, but for those at the highest risk, I think it's very important to say those with a prior fracture, regardless of bone density, or those with a T-score of minus 2.5, regardless of race, should really be thought about for therapy. For those with osteopenia, we suggested using lower treatment thresholds than the traditional 10-year thresholds of 20% for major osteoporotic fracture and 3% for hip fracture in osteoporosis guidelines. That is kind of based on both what I showed you for calibration problems, as well as the disparities. So we found that Black, Hispanic, and Asian people have worse outcomes after fracture. Perhaps we should be a little bit more aggressive in treating them. 

So just to go back to this case, so this was actually a Black woman, and you can see what the Black FRAX calculated 7.4% for major osteoporotic fracture and 1.7% for hip fracture. Race-neutral FRAX showed 14.2% for major osteoporotic fracture and 3.1% for hip fracture. But I kind of wanted to point out, you know, this patient has a ton of risk factors: diabetes, stroke, insulin use, and falls. These are all risk factors. So you can hopefully look at this Black FRAX and understand and appreciate that it is not an accurate reflection of this patient's risk. 

So just to summarize, there are substantial differences in bone structure in racial and ethnic minorities in the U.S., likely leading to lower fracture rates. Race correction in FRAX is evidence-based in Black women, but could exacerbate these disparities. The current FRAX appears to underestimate fractures in Hispanic women, Hispanic men, and Black men. Consider using lower treatment thresholds in patients who you would worry about having a poor outcome after a fracture. And I just wanted to thank some of my collaborators, mentors, and funding. I also wanted to thank my son. This is an old picture of him. He let me come here despite it being his sixth birthday, and I'm happy to take any questions.
