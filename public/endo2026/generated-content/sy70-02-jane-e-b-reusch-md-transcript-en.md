### Introduction: cardiorespiratory fitness as an integrated systems measure

**Moderator:** We'll collect questions toward the end if we have more time. Our next speaker—and again, it is my honor to introduce a long-term friend and collaborator—is Dr. Jane Reusch at the University of Colorado. She is going to talk about insulin resistance and cardiorespiratory fitness.

**Dr. Jane Reusch:** First, I want to thank Zhenqi, because I know he was behind putting together this session and also giving me the chance to speak about my favorite topic. I want to thank Steve for introducing all the concepts, so I don't have to spend extra time on some of the methodology and other things.

I am going to talk about insulin resistance and cardiorespiratory fitness as a supply-and-demand issue. One of the things I want to highlight, because they already put up a disclosure slide, is that I am a physician-scientist, and I think it is absolutely the world's best job. I will weave that into this talk.

As Steve introduced, today's topic is physical activity, with a nod to lifestyle as medicine as the ultimate combination therapy for decreasing mortality. Why would you want to focus on exercise? The reason that I, as a clinician, would want to focus on exercise is that, no matter what disease situation you may have in the background, if you have lower cardiorespiratory fitness, you have about a twofold increase in cardiovascular mortality.

Why would that be? There is a lot of multi-omics being presented here, but I want to talk about the systems biology of the integrated system that is the human body. When you present an exercise stress to the human body, you first need the lungs to work and transport oxygen to hemoglobin, then pump that oxygen-loaded hemoglobin to the rest of the body and out to peripheral systems, including skeletal muscle; get those mitochondria to work; and then repeat that all over again. It is not really surprising that this complex metabolic response to a workload would represent something that dictates how long you might live.

### Exercise impairment in type 1 and type 2 diabetes

How does this system work in the setting of diabetes? The work of our lab, in collaboration with Dr. Judy Regensteiner and Dr. Kristen Nadeau over the last couple of decades, has made the statement that it does not work so well in diabetes.

When you take sedentary people with type 2 diabetes—these were premenopausal women—you see early data from a period when A1Cs were not as well controlled. As we have gone through this process, our participants have become better and better controlled. That is very relevant to something Steve said earlier about where glucose fits into this whole equation.

What we see is that sedentary, premenopausal women have a peak oxygen uptake, or VO₂peak, of 17. This is different from a maximum because the test is done on a bicycle. We are having a lot of conversations right now about frailty. A VO₂peak of 17 in an uncomplicated 42-year-old woman is in the frailty range, so that is alarming.

When these same participants undergo supervised exercise training, they have a very good response. Even after the intervention, however, the people with diabetes look similar to the sedentary controls and much worse than the lean individuals. This defect is also more potent in women than in men. Women, because they are smaller, will always have a lower VO₂peak, but the defect seen with uncomplicated diabetes is greater in women than in men.

We wondered whether this was also starting early in life. Dr. Kristen Nadeau, a pediatric endocrinologist, did similar studies demonstrating that children with obesity and type 2 diabetes had a phenomenon similar to what we had seen in premenopausal women, and that it was also seen in type 1 diabetes.

Now the physician-scientist in me says: I want to answer an important question. Decreased functional exercise capacity is seen in newly diagnosed, uncomplicated, sedentary adults and youth with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. This is an important research question, and research is challenging—I would say even more so in this environment. Please write your comments to OMB between now and July 13, because we do not want political assessment of research. We want peer-reviewed, excellent research, and we do not want to lose research leadership here or across the world.

What is our big question? Moving back from my brief segue: What physiological mechanisms lead to decreased exercise capacity in diabetes, and how might we overcome this exercise defect by understanding them?

Studies over the last couple of decades—I do not want to say maybe three decades, because that would be embarrassing—have identified insulin resistance and endothelial dysfunction as major drivers of this exercise intolerance. As a scientist, can we use the knowledge that endothelial dysfunction and insulin resistance drive this phenomenon to understand and hopefully remediate the exercise defect?

### Insulin sensitization, eNOS signaling, and GLP-1

As proof of concept, with a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp we identified a robust—this is crazy data in human subjects—relationship between insulin sensitivity and exercise capacity, measured as VO₂peak.

I am a clinician, so don't we have a drug for that? We have insulin sensitizers. Back before rosiglitazone was, in my view, indicted incorrectly, it was an insulin sensitizer, much like pioglitazone, a peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) ligand that we still have. We said that if this relationship was so strong, then an insulin sensitizer might augment functional exercise capacity.

In a similar group of people with diabetes treated in a placebo-controlled fashion, four months of half-dose rosiglitazone at 4 mg per day improved VO₂peak. It also improved the kinetics at the onset of exercise. We will give a nod to that later. Exercise kinetics refer to moving from not moving at all to beginning to move: your heart rate and oxygen uptake have to increase. That is the kinetic response at the beginning of exercise.

How might insulin action tie together this complex cardiometabolic challenge? I spent the first 10 years of my scientific life as a cell signaler studying insulin action. I wanted to know where there might be an intersection between the perturbation from exercise and insulin action. We focused on nitric oxide synthase. I used to study a transcription factor named cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (CREB), so we were a little excited because it is an upstream regulator of PGC-1, which has already been introduced as a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis.

As Steve told you, insulin action—or insulin resistance—in the vasculature disrupts the ability of AKT to signal to endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). We did a set of experiments that I do not have time to show today in which we either knocked out nitric oxide synthase or inhibited it pharmacologically. We developed a phenotype much like what we see in diabetes: decreased exercise capacity at baseline and after exercise training. We think of nitric oxide synthase in relation to vasomotion, but it is also an upstream regulator of PGC-1α. It therefore regulates mitochondrial function and dynamics, as well as vascular reactivity, which could contribute to exercise capacity.

Once again, the clinician in me, a decade later, asked whether there was a way to target nitric oxide synthase pharmacologically and jump over the barrier that insulin resistance poses to nitric oxide regulation. We used the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) pathway.

In the next set of experiments, we were not using a GLP-1 receptor agonist. We used saxagliptin, a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitor that blocks the degradation of GLP-1, so we were looking only at endogenous GLP-1 levels. We used the Goto-Kakizaki (GK) rat, a lean, insulin-resistant model of type 2 diabetes, to see whether GLP-1 manipulation could overcome the impact of insulin resistance.

As was mentioned in an earlier talk, rodents are a little obnoxious in that they can exercise-train in seven days; you get the physiological adaptation. In the GK rat, we saw no stimulation of vascular AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), nitric oxide synthase, or PGC-1α in response to exercise training, as shown in black. When the animals were treated with saxagliptin for seven days plus exercise training, we saw stimulation of this entire mitochondrial-biogenesis pathway. We also saw induction of subunits of mitochondrial oxidative proteins that were not seen in the untreated state. Importantly, when we did this for three weeks, we saw an augmentation in the exercise-training response.

To ask whether GLP-1 was actually responsible, we then blocked GLP-1. Amy Keller in the lab did this experiment, and Rebecca Scalzo blocked GLP-1 with a GLP-1 antagonist and blunted the exercise-training response. As with all science, some things are not new or are rediscovered, but I always like somebody else to find the same thing that I found. I was thrilled to see a paper in which others found a similar observation that GLP-1 was essential for the skeletal-muscle response to exercise training.

As a translational lab, we take whatever we find in animal models straight to humans. Unlike the insulin sensitizer rosiglitazone, when we used a first-generation GLP-1 receptor agonist—exenatide—we did not see an improvement in VO₂peak, but we did see changes in cardiac and vascular function. I will return to this as an ultimate goal of combination therapy using drugs and exercise training.

### Cardiac perfusion and central vascular stiffness

We had a new tactic. We were still trying to tie together insulin action, endothelial dysfunction, and the exercise defect. One thing my primary mentor did not tell me, but I learned later, was that humans might be a great model of human disease.

We studied men and women with type 2 diabetes and reasoned that the heart and skeletal muscle were essential contributors to decreased exercise capacity. We asked what was specific about diabetes. Diabetes specifically attacks the microvasculature, so we conducted human studies of the heart and skeletal muscle.

Working with Dr. Judy Regensteiner—and Zhenqi and I are now working with her—is a great thing. You think of an experiment, and she thinks, “We can do this.” In the next experiment, we invited people with and without diabetes to have a right-heart catheter placed—that is an invasive procedure—and then to come over to the Clinical Research Center. I do not say that erroneously; this was long enough ago that it was still called the Clinical Research Center. We had them get on a bicycle and exercise while we measured their pulmonary capillary wedge pressures.

We saw the wedge pressure rise during exercise at a relatively low workload. That reflects a degree of cardiac stiffness, which we have now identified by echocardiography and cardiac MRI, but this was what we had available at the time. These were not women with an A1C of 9%; they had an A1C of 6.5% or 7% and had only recently been diagnosed with diabetes. I did not believe this was simply a problem with the cardiac myocytes.

As a clinician, when I wonder whether perfusion is abnormal in the heart, I use sestamibi. We performed myocardial perfusion analysis before and after exercise stress. Unlike some of Zhenqi's data, in these women with obesity we saw a lovely improvement in the people without diabetes. This was exercise stress, not insulin stress. The women with diabetes did not change at all, even though they were not sick. The absent adaptive perfusion response was compelling.

Some of Dr. Linda Peterson's subsequent work showed that women with diabetes may actually have increased basal cardiac perfusion, and some of that was also shown in Steve's work. Perhaps they simply do not get the adaptive response. Another reason we used the right-heart catheter was to obtain a detailed assessment of cardiac output. We did not see a difference in cardiac output between the two groups.

To assess these issues in more detail, we have begun incredible collaborations with colleagues in cardiovascular imaging. They are using cardiac MRI to measure blood-flow waveforms in the heart and perform detailed assessments of central arterial stiffness. The main finding so far is that the heart is somewhat stiff, as we showed previously, but central arterial stiffness is specifically different.

If central arterial stiffness is increased in people with diabetes, then the usual decrease in pressure between the heart, aorta, and capillary beds of end organs is disrupted. That may feed both backward and forward to skeletal muscle. If we have learned anything from pulmonary hypertension, it is that capillary beds do not like to see high pressures; those pressures can disrupt their function.

What have we learned so far from imaging the heart? We have increased aortic stiffness and a 31% reduction in end-diastolic volume, but we do not have decreased cardiac output. That does not fully explain the exercise defect, so we moved to skeletal muscle.

### The skeletal-muscle mitochondrial paradox

In skeletal muscle, similar to the studies you saw earlier, we examined bulk blood flow, tissue-level capillary perfusion, and mitochondrial function. We assessed mitochondrial function in two ways. One was phosphorus magnetic resonance spectroscopy (³¹P-MRS), which allowed us to measure oxidative flux; the second was a muscle biopsy.

We saw what had previously been reported by the groups of Dr. Mary-Elizabeth Patti and Dr. Gerald Shulman: impaired phosphocreatine recovery, a longer time to regenerate ATP, and an overall decrease in oxidative flux. We then asked whether the problem was in the mitochondria themselves. We performed muscle biopsies and measured mitochondrial respiration using Oroboros high-resolution respirometry. Much like the cardiac-output finding, there was very little change in mitochondrial respiratory capacity, and there was no change in muscle-fiber type.

This told us that in vivo we saw decreased mitochondrial function, but in vitro—or in the muscle biopsy—we did not see much decrease in capacity. We therefore wanted to examine skeletal-muscle perfusion. We first did this using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS).

### NIRS, supplemental oxygen, and the metaboreflex

Near-infrared spectroscopy examines oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin, and with our system we can also assess the hemoglobin response. At the onset of exercise, oxyhemoglobin decreases and deoxyhemoglobin increases. Exercise physiologists have demonstrated a strong relationship between deoxyhemoglobin and VO₂peak.

When we examined people with diabetes, however, we saw a scatterplot. That suggested there might be heterogeneous perfusion, with areas of low oxygen tension and areas of high oxygen tension. I live in Colorado, so I thought: Maybe I will just put oxygen in these people's noses and see whether they get better. I would not be telling you this if it did not work.

We gave supplemental oxygen and increased oxygen tension. To my great surprise, we also increased muscle perfusion. That means the environment must have been hypoxic, because giving oxygen usually produces vasoconstriction if the oxygen is not needed. This suggested tissue hypoxia.

That got me reading, because I am not an exercise physiologist and I am always learning, about something called the metaboreflex. When you start exercising, the metaboreflex should increase sympathetic tone. You should vasoconstrict the parts of the body that do not need blood flow and augment flow to those that do. The augmented blood flow to working muscles is locally modulated by signals such as endothelin and other markers of local demand. If you have high sympathetic tone at baseline, this does not work.

Even in our people with uncomplicated diabetes, markers of autonomic function suggested mild autonomic neuropathy; others subsequently demonstrated the same. During exercise, I should have vasoconstriction in tissues that are not working, but the working muscle should signal that it needs increased blood flow. I think that signal is disrupted in diabetes, but perhaps you can jump the train again by giving oxygen. We found that five minutes of oxygen improved mitochondrial oxidative function.

We began to ask whether this was a perfusion problem, which is why I always reach out to Zhenqi Liu when I am thinking about perfusion. We met at our first American Diabetes Association poster sessions together a few short decades ago.

Diabetes can disrupt blood flow in many ways, including capillary rarefaction, blood-flow heterogeneity, and glycocalyx degradation. There is also the myogenic response, which we may be able to measure, but we cannot measure it clinically as well without better tools. In collaboration with Dr. Aaron England, we created a multiparametric MRI method called Velocity and Perfusion, Intravascular Venous Oxygen saturation and T2* (vPIVOT). With it, we can measure arterial and venous waveforms, muscle-bed-specific perfusion, and oxygen extraction.

### Skeletal-muscle extracellular matrix

I will add one more twist to the story: What might be happening between blood flow and skeletal muscle itself that alters functional status? Could it be the architecture of skeletal muscle?

Skeletal muscle contains muscle cells and capillaries, but it also contains extracellular matrix (ECM). The late Dr. David Wasserman's group, with Dr. Lee Kang, previously showed that extracellular matrix in skeletal muscle can disrupt insulin action in skeletal-muscle cells. In other work, Dr. Sarah Lessard's group showed that collagen accumulation in skeletal muscle can disrupt VO₂peak in an animal model.

We took the same muscles we had studied earlier and examined the extracellular matrix. Dr. Lessard's work had focused largely on collagen, and we saw a slight increase in collagen in people with diabetes. This was a single-leg, 10-day exercise-training intervention, and we did not see much response in collagen.

This was the work of my graduate student Nicholas Hulett, who really wanted to finish his thesis. I said: What else can we do, and how can we relate these findings to clinical observations? We had glucose infusion rate, and we saw the relationship Dr. Lessard previously reported: greater collagen was associated with a lower glucose infusion rate. We also saw a new relationship between glucose infusion rate and elastin in the gastrocnemius muscle, which was a little surprising. We saw a similar relationship between VO₂peak and elastin. Phosphocreatine turnover velocity was also better when there was less dystrophin—less overall matrix.

Where are we now? We are saying that there is vascular inflexibility: the vasculature does not respond appropriately to exercise in either the heart or skeletal muscle. We also have a mitochondrial paradox. The real question is which of these abnormalities will respond to intervention.

### Heterogeneous responses to exercise training and ongoing work

We performed all the assays I just described and then conducted an exercise-training intervention. Much like others have reported, we saw a waterfall plot: some people responded to exercise training, and some did not. We wondered whether this would correlate with insulin sensitivity. Again, we saw what I call the diabetes-related scatterplot.

What if we looked only at those who responded with improved insulin action? Would all of them have a fabulous glucose response? Once again, we saw a scatterplot. We then examined mitochondrial function in muscle biopsy, and everyone had a wonderful mitochondrial response. Responder status is therefore dictated by the endpoint you are examining. One responder may have a beautiful increase in insulin sensitivity and mitochondrial biogenesis, whereas another may have changes in VO₂peak or endothelial function.

Right now, we are working with our fearless leader here, Zhenqi Liu, to use the microbubbles you saw in the previous talk to ask whether insulin, through eNOS, can improve nutritive blood flow and exercise capacity.

Sometimes, when you are doing one thing, you can do something unexpected. Because the design includes muscle biopsies before and after insulin infusion, we can examine mitochondrial flexibility in response to insulin. We will have pre- and post-infusion data, but of course I only have the pre-infusion data now. In response to insulin infusion, there are changes in the ability to metabolize—or in sensitivity to ADP—in both lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. In the people with diabetes, you can see that these responses are quite [unclear in audio] here.

To summarize, diabetes, through decreased eNOS activity and impaired insulin action, decreases exercise capacity. Exercise training can move this in the other direction. We had to coin a new phrase: metabolic or vascular inflexibility.

It takes a village to do any work like this, and I thank everyone who worked with me, including Zhenqi. Where we are going in the future is the nervous system.
