## Introduction and overview

**Moderator:** Our next speaker is Professor Matthias Kroiss from the University of Würzburg. He will discuss biomarkers of survival and treatment response in adrenocortical carcinoma.

**Matthias Kroiss:** Thank you for being here. It is a great honor to speak before such an exquisite audience. I will give you my view of biomarkers of survival and treatment response in adrenocortical carcinoma, or ACC.

I will begin with some basic facts about ACC, then discuss clinical prognostic markers, molecular pathogenesis, and molecular markers of prognosis. I will show unpublished multilayer analyses that we performed in refractory ACC. Finally, I would like to convince you that standard mitotane treatment needs to be questioned if we want to make progress in ACC treatment.

## ACC and the limitations of standard therapy

ACC is an orphan malignancy. It frequently secretes steroid hormones, including glucocorticoids and androgens. The clinical diagnosis is generally prompted by signs of hormone excess or tumor mass effect and its complications, and sometimes by an incidentaloma.

Treatment is surgical in localized stages and systemic in advanced disease. The only approved therapy is mitotane. It is used as adjuvant therapy in high-risk cases after surgery and, in advanced disease, either alone or with chemotherapy. It has many common adverse effects, induces hepatic enzymes and therefore causes drug-drug interactions, and has a very long half-life—approximately six months. Once it is started, its effects are difficult to eliminate.

The standard regimen for advanced ACC is mitotane plus etoposide, doxorubicin, and cisplatin, or EDP-M. More than a decade has passed without much change. In the FIRM-ACT trial, median progression-free survival with EDP-M was approximately five months, and median overall survival was approximately 15 months. Outcomes are heterogeneous: many patients die early, whereas others die much later or, fortunately, not at all. We need to understand what distinguishes these patients.

In my opinion, biomarkers may serve two purposes. At diagnosis in localized disease, they may identify patients at risk of an aggressive course who could be candidates for adjuvant treatment. In advanced disease, they may identify responders to current therapy or innovative treatments.

## Clinical and histopathologic prognostic markers

How can we estimate prognosis in a person with ACC? Martin Fassnacht and colleagues showed that the 2004 UICC classification did not distinguish prognosis sharply enough. Together with the European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumors, or ENSAT, they developed a new classification that has subsequently been validated many times. Clinical and pathologic factors already provide important clues to an individual patient's prognosis.

A very simple biomarker also required formal validation. Felix Beuschlein and colleagues showed that the proliferation marker Ki-67 distinguishes good, intermediate, and poor prognosis in localized ACC. Tumor stage and Ki-67 therefore provide two useful clinical or histopathologic markers, and this approach is reflected in the 2022 WHO classification.

Hormonal secretion is another prognostic biomarker. Alfredo Berruti and colleagues showed that, in localized ACC, relapse-free survival differed between patients without cortisol secretion and those with overt cortisol secretion. Overall survival was also less favorable with overt Cushing syndrome. This may reflect tumor biology in addition to the detrimental systemic effects of glucocorticoid excess.

Attempts have combined ENSAT stage, Ki-67, resection status, age, and symptoms such as glucocorticoid excess into the S-GRAS score. The score improves on stage alone, but the difference is still moderate. The key point is that clinical and histopathologic markers in ACC already provide substantial outcome information. Any molecular marker must outperform them, which is difficult.

## Molecular pathogenesis and prognostic classification

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway—particularly CTNNB1—and TP53 are among the most frequently altered drivers in ACC. The IGF2/IGF-1 receptor axis is also important. In many ACCs it is altered by epigenetic events; IGF2 is highly expressed and has been studied as a therapeutic target.

Guillaume Assié and colleagues used genome-wide transcriptomic, chromosomal, and methylome analyses to stratify patients by molecular class. The resulting groups differed in disease-free and overall survival. They then developed more clinically applicable targeted approaches: one classifier combined gene expression, methylation, and chromosomal alterations; a DNA-based classifier combined methylation, chromosomal alterations, and mutations.

In localized disease, these classifiers improved discrimination among intermediate- and poor-prognosis patients. In stage IV disease, however, the molecular markers had no significant association with outcome. These classifiers have not entered routine clinical practice.

## Targeted sequencing in advanced ACC

Can molecular markers inform prognosis and guide treatment? Pozdeyev and colleagues analyzed 364 unique ACC samples tested by FoundationOne across 553 genes. The median number of alterations was seven per tumor, with a range of 1 to 56. They identified 46 potentially druggable targets in 213 of the 364 samples. At first glance, this makes molecular testing appear promising.

One finding I want to emphasize is MSH6, which encodes a DNA mismatch-repair protein. Tumors with alterations in DNA-repair genes had a significantly higher tumor mutational burden. These patients may therefore be candidates for immune-checkpoint inhibition. However, few clinical data were available, most target variants outside mismatch-repair genes were not characterized further, and there was little information about whether treatment recommendations were implemented or whether patients benefited.

Another study analyzed metastatic tissue from 14 patients. Samples from the same individual clustered together in principal-component analysis even when they came from different metastatic sites, such as lung and liver. Mutations were found not only in familiar cancer targets but also in ion channels, G-protein-coupled receptors, and nuclear receptors. Some proposed treatments were surprising—for example, using the antihypertensive drug amlodipine for an aggressive malignancy. Such approaches might work in theory, but clinical implementation and outcome data were absent.

Tito Fojo and colleagues analyzed 43 tumor samples from metastatic or locally advanced disease using exome sequencing and Affymetrix gene-expression profiling. They compared their findings with the COMETE and TCGA cohorts and with a pooled all-cancer dataset. Despite the multiplicity of data, TP53 and CTNNB1 again dominated. Mutational-signature analysis identified signatures associated with aging and mismatch-repair deficiency, but the authors estimated that 82% of genes likely had no role in ACC etiology or biology. They concluded that the totality of the data argued strongly against mutation analysis reliably identifying critical, medically actionable targets. ACC remained, in their words, a mystery.

## NCT MASTER multilayer analysis of refractory ACC

We did not give up. We performed an unpublished genome-wide multilayer analysis of refractory ACC within the National Center for Tumor Diseases MASTER program—Molecularly Aided Stratification for Tumor Eradication. Please do not photograph these unpublished data.

We studied 101 advanced ACC specimens after failure of standard therapy. Patients had received a median of two treatment lines. We performed genomic and transcriptomic profiling on 56 metastatic biopsies and 45 primary tumors; 17 samples were from untreated tumors because metastatic tissue could not be obtained for clinical reasons. Every result went to a multidisciplinary molecular tumor board. Outcomes included the number and type of evidence-based treatment recommendations, the proportion implemented, and clinical response.

We performed extensive analyses, including immune-cell deconvolution, germline testing, and evaluation of steroid excess. CTNNB1 and TP53 remained the most important alterations.

Mutational-signature analysis identified SBS4, associated with tobacco exposure, even though most patients were young women who had never smoked or had smoked little. SBS18 indicated oxidative stress. Other groups showed DNA-repair deficiency, mismatch-repair deficiency, and aging-related signatures.

We found 31 likely pathogenic germline variants in 29 patients. Sixteen were heterozygous variants with autosomal-dominant inheritance: nine in Lynch-syndrome genes—MLH1 in three, MSH2 in two, and MSH6 in four—three in TP53 associated with Li-Fraumeni syndrome, two in BRCA2, one in RAD51D, and one in AIP. This supports genetic counseling in advanced ACC.

We also examined genome-wide RNA expression, focusing on tyrosine kinases because they are important drug targets. We did not find recurrent fusion events or critical mutations that could readily be targeted. We therefore asked whether an overexpressed kinase could be identified by comparing ACC with other rare tumors in the MASTER program.

MERTK stood out. RNA sequencing was available for 84 tumors, and high expression was present in a large proportion of ACCs. MERTK expression was much higher in ACC than in the other rare advanced tumors in the program. Importantly, high MERTK expression was associated with adverse overall survival, with P = .007. MERTK may therefore be both a therapeutic target and a prognostic marker in advanced ACC. These data remain unvalidated and require confirmation in an independent metastatic ACC tissue series.

The molecular tumor board produced treatment recommendations for almost every patient, often several recommendations targeting different pathways. Only a few were supported by evidence from a similar tumor entity; most relied on analogy rather than ACC-specific data. RNA-based markers—primarily tyrosine-kinase overexpression—contributed to 41% of recommendations.

What were the clinical outcomes? We examined tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy using a swimmer plot that compared progression-free survival on the previous treatment with progression-free survival on the molecularly informed treatment. Most patients received cabozantinib through a trial that I presented yesterday. Outcomes were similarly disappointing whether the TKI had a molecular rationale or was given without one.

Molecular markers can assist in outcome stratification, but clinical markers already do much of the work. Advanced disease may contain more mutations, but their relevance is unclear. Approximately 16% of the cohort had autosomal-dominant germline variants, supporting genetic counseling. Mismatch-repair gene variants may inform immune-checkpoint inhibitor treatment, and MERTK expression is intriguing.

Is there a reason to conduct molecular tumor profiling routinely? My personal view is yes, but we should not raise unjustified hope for targeted treatment. A standard panel that includes fusions is likely to add little treatment information. Somatic mismatch-repair genes and genetic counseling are more relevant; MUTYH carriers may also have high tumor mutational burden. Even with mismatch-repair deficiency or MUTYH, however, immune-checkpoint inhibition is not a magic bullet.

## The crucial question: prior mitotane

Why was the outcome of molecularly informed treatment so poor? “After standard therapy” meant that 83 of 101 patients had received mitotane and 73 had received platinum chemotherapy before biopsy. Twenty-three patients died before the molecular tumor board recommendation or within three months afterward. These were late-stage patients with poor prognosis regardless of treatment. Reimbursement also delayed recommended therapy.

This brings us to the *Gretchenfrage*—the crucial question from Goethe's *Faust*: what is the role of mitotane?

Mitotane induces serum growth differentiation factor 15, or GDF-15. GDF-15 is an immunosuppressive cytokine that reduces antitumor immune infiltration. Serum GDF-15 rises after mitotane and correlates with plasma mitotane concentration. Among people treated with immune-checkpoint inhibitors, responders had significantly lower GDF-15 levels than nonresponders, although levels remained above those of healthy controls. Low and high GDF-15 also separated progression-free-survival outcomes.

We also examined prior mitotane exposure in the investigator-initiated CaboACC phase 2 trial of cabozantinib. Only the three patients without prior mitotane reached the expected cabozantinib exposure. Patients with prior mitotane but no detectable plasma concentration had lower exposure, and those with detectable mitotane had almost no cabozantinib exposure. The estimated mitotane half-life was 159 days. Patients with stable disease at four months tended to have greater cabozantinib exposure than those with progressive disease.

Thus, prior mitotane may impair the efficacy of second-line tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune-checkpoint inhibitors. Late-stage ACC may not be the right population for molecular testing because some patients die before results are available. First-line trials of immune-checkpoint inhibitors, TKIs, and combinations are warranted, and MERTK should be investigated further.

Thank you for your attention.

## Questions and discussion

**Matthias Kroiss:** No mitotane.

**Matthias Kroiss:** No mitotane. We should conduct clinical trials of cabozantinib with or without immune-checkpoint inhibition before starting mitotane.

**Moderator:** Would that apply to localized disease as well?

**Matthias Kroiss:** For localized disease, the treatment is surgery, and adjuvant therapy remains debatable. In patients with aggressive tumor biology, one could consider waiting for recurrence and then treating.
