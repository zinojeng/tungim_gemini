**Simone De Leo:** I want to thank the organizing committee for the opportunity to be here, and I thank Dr. Chen for the kind introduction. I will discuss the clinical presentation and pathologic features of pediatric thyroid cancer.

### Definitions, rarity, and changing presentation

In 2015, the American Thyroid Association, or ATA, published guidelines specifically addressing the management of thyroid nodules and differentiated thyroid cancer, or DTC, in children. The guidelines defined the pediatric population as patients up to 18 years of age. This is important for clinical practice and research because it enables homogeneous studies and valid comparisons between cohorts. In some settings, transition may extend from 18 to 21 years, but the guideline definition for pediatric studies is 18 years or younger.

Thyroid cancer is very rare in the pediatric population. The occurrence of DTC is approximately one case per million in children younger than 10 years and approximately one case per 75,000 among those aged 10 to 18 years. However, the incidence of DTC is increasing. The increase is particularly evident for tumors smaller than 1 cm and for papillary thyroid carcinoma, or PTC. Improved detection, probably related in part to wider use of ultrasonography, may explain some of the trend, although other contributing factors cannot be excluded.

Since the early 2000s, we have known that pediatric thyroid cancer has distinctive pathologic and clinical features. A study published more than 20 years ago reported increased risks of lymph-node and lung metastases in pediatric patients. However, that pediatric cohort included only 60 patients and defined pediatric age as younger than 21 years, which differs from the definition later adopted in the 2015 ATA guidelines.

The pediatric thyroid-cancer landscape is also changing. A Polish study enrolled 475 consecutive pediatric patients beginning in 1970. Across time, the investigators found no major change in sex distribution, age, or the proportion of cases with extrathyroidal extension. However, the proportion of follicular thyroid carcinoma, or FTC, declined approximately tenfold, from about 22% around 1990 to about 2% in the most recent period. The proportion of small tumors—those smaller than 1 cm or measuring 1 to 2 cm—increased significantly. Multifocality decreased, particularly among tumors smaller than 1 cm. Distant metastases also declined, from approximately 18% around 1990 to less than 10% more recently.

These changes suggest an apparent decrease in severity at presentation, perhaps because more patients with less aggressive DTC are being diagnosed at earlier stages. The decline in FTC may also relate partly to iodine supplementation introduced around 2000 in Poland, which had been iodine-deficient during much of the twentieth century.

To assess current clinical and pathologic features, we therefore need cohorts that use contemporary guideline definitions, include large numbers of patients, and represent patients diagnosed in recent years. Most previously published studies included fewer than 100 patients. Without these elements, apparent differences between pediatric series may reflect changing definitions, calendar period, or detection patterns rather than true biological differences.

### Contemporary Italian and North American cohorts

I will focus on two studies. The first is our Italian multicenter retrospective study, which included patients diagnosed since 2000 at 18 years of age or younger. The final cohort contained 538 patients. The second is the recently published Child and Adolescent Thyroid Consortium, or CATC, study. CATC was established in 2019 as an international multicenter initiative involving centers in the United States and Canada. That cohort included 715 patients.

The clinical characteristics were similar. Approximately 75% to 78% of patients were female, the age at diagnosis was about 15 years, and previous external-beam radiotherapy exposure was reported in approximately 5% in both studies.

PTC accounted for approximately 94% of tumors in both cohorts and was more common than in adult thyroid-cancer populations. Aggressive PTC subtypes also appeared more frequent than in adults. Approximately 25% of patients had T3 or T4 tumors. T4 tumors accounted for about 5% of the Italian cohort and 4% of the North American cohort, slightly higher proportions than in adults. These comparisons are important because the pediatric tumors are not merely smaller versions of the typical adult presentation; the stage distribution and histologic pattern differ.

Lymph-node metastases occurred in more than half of patients. Lateral-compartment lymph-node metastases, classified as N1b disease, affected approximately 30% in the Italian cohort and 40% in the North American cohort. Distant metastases occurred in approximately 10% to 15%, substantially more often than the estimated 2% to 4% in adults.

Tumors also tended to be larger in pediatric patients, and extrathyroidal extension was more frequent. Gross extrathyroidal extension occurred in approximately 11% to 17% across the two cohorts, compared with approximately 3% to 5% in adults. Vascular or angioinvasion was reported in 40.8% of the Italian cohort. Multifocality occurred in about 40% of the Italian cohort, a rate that may not differ substantially from the adult population. Its frequency may be declining in pediatric patients as tumors are detected at an earlier stage.

In summary, pediatric DTC has distinctive clinicopathologic features. PTC is the predominant histotype, and pediatric DTC presents with larger tumors and more frequent lymph-node and distant metastases. I retain a question mark regarding whether multifocality is truly more frequent than in adults, because the observed rate is not clearly different and may be changing with earlier diagnosis. Overall, however, the combination of larger tumor size, extrathyroidal extension, nodal disease, and distant metastases supports the conclusion that the disease presents more aggressively in children.

### Response to treatment and persistent disease

Despite the more aggressive presentation, 77% of patients in the Italian cohort had no evidence of disease at a median follow-up of seven years. Persistent disease was present in 23%: 12.6% had a biochemical incomplete response and 10.4% had a structural incomplete response.

In the CATC cohort, at a median follow-up of approximately 5.7 years, 66.5% had no evidence of disease and 33.5% had an incomplete response. A biochemical incomplete response occurred in approximately 12%, and a structural incomplete response occurred in 21.6%. A French Gustave Roussy cohort of 260 patients, with a median follow-up of 8.2 years, reported an excellent response in 74.3%, biochemical persistence in 6.5%, and structural persistence in 19.2%.

The explanation for the difference in structural persistence between the Italian and North American cohorts remains uncertain. The follow-up durations were broadly similar, so treatment intensity may be one factor. In Italy, only eight patients—1.5%—underwent lobectomy, compared with approximately 8% in the North American study. More than 90% of Italian patients received radioactive iodine therapy, compared with approximately 70% of the North American cohort. The Italian patients were therefore treated more aggressively, which could contribute to the difference, although this observational comparison cannot establish causation. Treatment strategy will be addressed more fully in the next presentation.

We examined predictors of persistence, especially structural persistence, because identifying higher-risk patients may allow treatment to be adapted appropriately. In the Italian study, gross extrathyroidal extension was independently associated with persistent disease, with an odds ratio of 2.81, and lymph-node uptake on whole-body scintigraphy after the first radioactive iodine treatment had an odds ratio of 3.31. For structural incomplete response, T4 disease had an odds ratio of 4.3, and lymph-node uptake on whole-body scintigraphy had an odds ratio of 3.39.

In the CATC study, structural persistence was associated with more advanced tumors, including T4 disease, N1b disease, and distant metastasis. Multifocality, histotype, and histologic subtype were not independently associated with persistence in the Italian study.

An important CATC finding was the relationship between extrathyroidal extension and both locoregional and distant metastases. Even minimal extrathyroidal extension appeared relevant in pediatric patients and was associated with higher rates of structural incomplete response and metastasis. This differs from what we understand about minimal extrathyroidal extension in adults.

### Survival and risk stratification

Even with aggressive presentation and persistent disease, survival is excellent. A Mayo Clinic study of 215 pediatric patients treated from 1940 through 2008 had a median follow-up of 28.7 years. There were no deaths from PTC during the first 20 years after surgery. Even among 12 patients with distant metastases, none died during that period. Two deaths from PTC occurred only after 25 years. We recorded no deaths in the Italian cohort. The CATC study recorded one death, but it was unrelated to thyroid cancer.

Because survival is so favorable, the AJCC staging system has limited utility in pediatric patients. All are younger than 55 years, so stage III and IV disease do not exist in this population: patients without distant metastasis are stage I, and those with distant metastasis are stage II. AJCC staging predicts survival rather than persistent or recurrent disease.

The ATA therefore developed a pediatric risk-stratification system tailored to the pediatric population and oriented toward persistent or recurrent disease rather than mortality. Low risk includes disease grossly confined to the thyroid, with N0 or Nx status, or only incidental microscopic central-compartment N1a disease. Intermediate risk includes extensive N1a involvement or minimal N1b disease. High risk includes regionally extensive N1b disease or locally invasive T4 disease, with or without distant metastases. This system is more clinically informative for the outcomes that pediatric endocrinologists actually need to anticipate.

### Prepubertal patients

I will conclude by focusing on prepubertal patients, who have particularly distinctive characteristics. In the Italian cohort, we compared 55 patients aged 10 years or younger with those older than 10. There was no female predominance in the younger group. Their tumors were more aggressive at presentation: aggressive PTC variants, gross extrathyroidal extension, T3b or T4 tumors, lateral cervical lymph-node metastases, and distant metastases were all more frequent.

The CATC study divided patients into groups aged younger than 10 years, 10 to 14 years, and older than 14 years. It produced similar findings. There was no female predominance among the youngest patients. Diffuse sclerosing PTC occurred in approximately 25% of the youngest group compared with 8.4% in older patients. Gross extrathyroidal extension occurred in 33.3% versus 9.4%, N1b disease in 76.9% versus 36.2%, and distant metastases in 46.2% versus 13.3%. Thus, distant metastases affected nearly half of the youngest patients.

### Age-related outcomes and conclusions

The more aggressive presentation was accompanied by worse response outcomes. In the Italian cohort, structural incomplete response was recorded in 16% of patients aged 10 years or younger compared with 10% among those older than 10. In CATC, structural incomplete response showed a strong decreasing gradient with age: 51.2% among patients younger than 10, 28.0% among those aged 10 to 13.9 years, and 15.8% among those aged 14 to 18.9 years. These findings are also consistent with a smaller Chinese study of 68 patients.

Prepubertal DTC therefore has higher rates of persistent structural disease, consistent with its more aggressive clinical phenotype. The age gradient is clinically important: the youngest group differs not only in sex distribution but also in diffuse sclerosing histology, gross local invasion, lateral nodal involvement, distant metastasis, and the probability of structural persistence.

My take-home messages are that pediatric thyroid carcinoma is rare, but its incidence is increasing. Pediatric DTC has clinicopathologic features distinct from those in adults and generally presents more aggressively. Prepubertal patients have an even more aggressive presentation and a somewhat worse response outcome. Nevertheless, survival in pediatric DTC remains excellent.

Comparative analyses of contemporary cohorts, such as the Italian and North American cohorts, may provide important insight into evolving treatment strategies and their effects on outcomes.

I want to thank my mentors, Professor Laura Fugazzola and Professor Luca Persani, my colleagues and friends, and all of you for your attention.
