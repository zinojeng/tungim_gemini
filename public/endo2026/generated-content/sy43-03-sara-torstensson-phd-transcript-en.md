### PMOS and clinical context

Hello everyone.

First of all, I would like to express how grateful I am to be given the opportunity to talk here today.

I will be presenting parts of my PhD project, which has revolved around the role of the immune system in PCOS, or PMOS.

So before I go on with the presentation, I just wanted to highlight the name change, which I'm sure many of you already heard about.

So PCOS is now called PMOS, or polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, which better reflects what the syndrome is.

So PMOS is the most common endocrine disorder, affecting at least one out of ten women of reproductive age.

And according to the Rotterdam criteria, you need to fulfill at least two out of the following three criteria in order to receive a diagnosis.

So the first one is hyperandrogenism, which means elevated levels of androgens, which can be assessed either clinically or biochemically.

The second is oligo- or anovulation, which is manifested as irregular menstrual cycles.

And the third is a polycystic ovarian morphology, which can be assessed by ultrasound or indirectly assessed by measuring anti-Müllerian hormone in blood.

And in addition to being an anovulatory disease, PCOS is highly associated with other reproductive complications, such as early miscarriage, reduced fertility, pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia.

And furthermore, PCOS is also associated with metabolic comorbidities such as insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

And importantly, this is independent of BMI.

So type 2 diabetes is highly prevalent among women with PMOS who have normal weight.

And we know that outside the context of PMOS, all of these complications are associated with immune dysfunction.

So that may indicate a role of the immune system in these comorbidities of PMOS.

There is also accumulating evidence of an aberrant immune profile in PMOS itself, and here I'm highlighting that with two studies.

One is a systematic review and meta-analysis that showed that C-reactive protein, which is an acute-phase inflammatory marker, is elevated in the blood of women with PMOS.

The second is a study showing that IL-18, which is a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is also elevated in blood of women with PMOS.

### Immune function in adipose tissue

But as I alluded to earlier, the immune system is also important for metabolic function.

So as you well know, our adipose tissue is an endocrine organ, and in adipose tissue we have a lot of immune cells.

And these immune cells interact with adipocytes in order to carry out many essential functions to sustain tissue homeostasis.

However, in people with obesity, there is an aberrant immune profile, and we say that it's associated with a chronic low-grade inflammation of adipose tissue.

And it's well established that this inflammation is contributing to insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

And in order to understand how the immune cells contribute to this, we need to acknowledge the complex interplay of immune cells in adipose tissue.

And here I will highlight some key players.

A central player is the macrophage.

So when macrophages acquire a so-called pro-inflammatory phenotype, they are known to contribute to insulin resistance.

And upstream of macrophages, we have NK cells that, when activated, produce IFN-γ and TNF-α, and this skews the phenotype of macrophages toward this detrimental phenotype.

On the other hand, we have macrophages with an anti-inflammatory phenotype, which instead are known to be beneficial for insulin sensitivity.

These macrophages are sustained by eosinophils through their release of IL-4 and IL-13, and eosinophils in turn are sustained by group 2 innate lymphoid cells, or ILC2s, through their release of IL-5.

So in this background, knowing the importance of immune function for metabolic function, we wondered how the immune system is involved in PMOS.

### Uterine immune cells and endometrial function

But as I said, the immune system is also important for reproductive function.

So in the endometrium, we have a wide range of immune cells that are important for tissue function.

And a central player here is uterine NK cells that are known to be essential for reproductive function as they promote implantation as well as the formation of spiral arteries that later sustain the fetus with nutrients and oxygen.

And these uterine NK cells fluctuate over the menstrual cycle, and they peak in number at the same time as the surge of progesterone following ovulation.

And this is interesting as the uterine NK cells do not express the progesterone receptor.

So this has been shown to be an indirect effect mediated by endometrial stromal cells that, when stimulated with progesterone, release IL-15, which is an essential cytokine for the differentiation and proliferation of NK cells.

And this indirect effect of sex hormones on immune cells led us to wonder how the immune cells in the endometrium of women with PMOS are affected, considering the aberrant hormonal milieu.

So in a normal healthy endometrium, we have these fluctuations of progesterone and estrogen and a concomitant tissue differentiation.

However, in anovulatory PMOS, we have a prolonged proliferative phase with unbroken estrogen exposure and endometrial thickening.

And it's believed that this endometrial dysfunction is contributing to these pregnancy complications in PMOS.

And my colleague Haojiang Lu has shown that maternal androgen exposure does affect pregnancy outcome.

So this was shown through a well-established mouse model of PMOS, and she showed that androgen exposure significantly decreased pregnancy rate in these mice.

She also showed that androgens then have a negative impact on embryonic development as well as the placenta.

So the overarching hypothesis in my PhD project was that hyperandrogenism alters immune function, which in turn contributes to insulin resistance and reproductive complications.

### DHT and flutamide mouse model

So in the first study that I'm presenting today, we asked how hyperandrogenism affects immune function and whether that might contribute to these common comorbidities.

So in order to study that, we utilized this well-established mouse model of PMOS, where female mice are exposed to dihydrotestosterone, or DHT, and thereby we're mimicking hyperandrogenism, which is believed to be the key feature of PMOS.

We also have a third group receiving co-treatment with flutamide, which is an androgen receptor antagonist, which allowed us to study androgen receptor activation.

These mice were thoroughly screened for reproductive and metabolic phenotypes, and thereafter we collected tissues that were analyzed by flow cytometry and Bio-Plex. Thereby, we did comprehensive immune profiling in which we analyzed both the adaptive and innate immune systems.

But as I said, the first thing we did was to phenotype the mice.

And what we found was that DHT-exposed mice had increased body weight but unaltered fat mass.

And when we challenged these mice with OGTT, we see that there is no difference in glucose uptake.

However, we see an increased insulin response 15 minutes after glucose administration.

And notably, this increase in insulin response was prevented in mice that were receiving co-treatment with flutamide.

Consequently, these PMOS-like mice had an increased HOMA-IR.

And when we measured long-term glucose levels by measuring glycated hemoglobin, or HbA1c, we saw that these PMOS-like mice had an increased long-term glucose load.

So to conclude here, we see that the DHT-exposed mice are a normal weight model of PMOS with a mild insulin resistance.

### Tissue-specific immune changes

So from here, we did immune profiling of these mice.

And the most drastic response we saw was the decrease of granular immune cells in the uterus of our PMOS-like mice.

And when we analyzed that more closely, we see that it is the eosinophil population that is drastically reduced.

Again, this is prevented in mice that are co-treated with flutamide.

We also see that eotaxin is lower in the uterus of these PMOS-like mice.

And eotaxin is an essential chemokine that recruits eosinophils.

On the other hand, we see no difference in IL-5, which is the cytokine that sustains eosinophils.

So we speculate that this reduced population of eosinophils in the uterus is due to an aberrant recruitment of eosinophils to the uterus.

In contrast to eosinophils, we see an increased population of NK cells in the uterus of PMOS-like mice, and this coincides with increased levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α in the uterus.

And these cytokines are normally produced by activated conventional NK cells.

However, we see no difference in expression of CD69, which can be a marker of activation on NK cells, but it can also be a marker of tissue residency.

So having seen clear alterations to immune cells in the uterus, we next wondered how the immune population in adipose tissue is affected.

And like the uterus, we see a decreased population of eosinophils in the adipose tissue of PMOS-like mice, although it is less pronounced than in the uterus.

In contrast to the uterus, however, we see no clear effect on eotaxin levels, while IL-5 is instead reduced.

So in contrast to the uterus, it appears that the reduced population of eosinophils in the adipose tissue is due to a lack of sustaining factors.

And we find this interesting, considering that I told you previously that eosinophils are important for sustaining macrophages with this beneficial phenotype, and that is done through the release of IL-4 and IL-13.

And indeed, we see lower levels of both IL-4 and IL-13 in adipose tissue of our PMOS-like mice.

So naturally, we asked how the macrophages are affected.

And the first thing we see is that there is no overall difference in the frequency of macrophages in adipose tissue of our PMOS-like mice, and this is in line with the fact that they had an unaltered fat mass.

However, we analyzed the macrophages more closely, especially on their expression of CD11c, which is a pro-inflammatory marker.

And indeed, we see that there is an increased frequency of CD11c+ macrophages in the adipose tissue of our mice.

And this is interesting in light of the fact that CD11c+ macrophages have been shown to be increased in the adipose tissue of people with obesity or mice fed a high-fat diet.

However, we see no clear effect on the expression of MHC class II, which is another pro-inflammatory marker.

So it's somewhat unclear whether these alterations in macrophages are contributing to the insulin resistance in our model.

Finally, we also investigated how the immune cells in the blood were affected, and we see an overall decreased immune cell count in our PMOS-like mice.

However, the eosinophils and NK cells in the blood were not really affected in our model.

So blood does not recapitulate the changes that we have seen in tissues, and this may highlight the impact of the local tissue environment on immune cells.

So to conclude the first study, we have a DHT-exposed model of PMOS that has a mild insulin resistance and which coincides with an aberrant immune profile in adipose tissue.

And we see overall changes to the immune profile with tissue-specific alterations that are not reflected in blood.

### Uterine NK-cell subsets and maturation

But on the basis of this previous study, we next wanted to assess how the uterine NK cells are affected more specifically and how that may contribute to reproductive complications in PMOS.

And this study is currently under revision.

So first of all, just to remind you, in our previous study, we had seen increased frequencies of NK cells in the uterus.

So now we utilized that same model and the same method to analyze more closely how the NK cells in the uterus were affected.

And the first thing we wanted to address was if this increased frequency of NK cells was due to an influx of conventional NK cells from the periphery or an expansion of tissue-resident uterine NK cells.

And these can be distinguished based on the markers CD49a, which is a tissue-residency marker, and CD62L, which is expressed on circulating conventional NK cells.

And what we see is that this increase of uterine NK cells is due to an increased frequency of conventional NK cells.

However, the tissue-resident population seems to be more or less unaffected in our model.

We also see an expansion of a double-negative population expressing neither CD49a nor CD62L, and this population has, to my knowledge, not been described in the literature before.

We also see an expansion of a small population of double-positive uterine NK cells.

So on this basis, we next wanted to analyze how these different subsets differ from each other.

So the first thing we analyzed is the maturation of these subsets.

So NK cells, when they mature, upregulate CD11b and downregulate CD27.

And what we could see in our controls was that the tissue-resident population was composed of a majority of immature NK cells, whereas the conventional NK-cell and double-negative populations were almost exclusively mature NK cells.

However, in our DHT-exposed PMOS-like mice, we see an obstruction of the phenotypic differences among all four subsets, with immature NK cells dominating all four subsets.

And when analyzing this data, I came across something that I found fairly peculiar.

It seems to me that there is a connection or a link between the expression of CD49a and CD27, the marker on immature NK cells.

So it looks to me like that when uterine NK cells acquired expression of CD49a, they concomitantly lose the expression of CD27.

### TGF-β hypothesis

This may be in line with a previous study showing an important role for autocrine TGF-β signaling in the differentiation of conventional NK cells into tissue-resident NK cells.

And among the things they showed in this study was that when they sorted NK cells into immature and mature NK cells and stimulated them in vitro with TGF-β, TGF-β had a greater capacity to upregulate CD49a in immature than in mature NK cells.

So naturally, we asked how TGF-β was affected in our model.

And indeed, we see that TGF-β is lower in the uterus of our PMOS-like mice than in our controls.

And we have not seen any publications showing autocrine TGF-β signaling in human endometrial NK cells.

So we utilized our already published single-nucleus RNA-sequencing datasets from endometrial biopsies, and here we show that endometrial NK cells coexpress both the TGF-β receptor and TGF-β.

So thereby, they fulfill at least the basic requirement for autocrine signaling with TGF-β.

However, this does not prove that it's actually an autocrine signaling, and I really believe that this should be investigated further.

### Implantation experiment

Considering these clear alterations of uterine NK cells, we next wondered how the reproductive phenotype is affected in our model.

And as I mentioned earlier, this very same model had been shown by my colleague Haojiang Lu to have a reduced pregnancy rate.

However, from this data, we could not decide whether this is due to implantation failure or due to an early miscarriage.

So in order to address that, we superovulated our mice and mated them with a normal healthy male.

And the reason that we superovulate these mice is that they are arrested in diestrus, which is an anovulatory cycle phase.

We then did a plug check, and the presence of a copulatory plug designated embryonic day 0.5.

We then followed these mice to embryonic day 5.5, right after the implantation occurs, and injected them with Evans blue.

And the natural changes in the vasculature around implantation sites leads to the spontaneous accumulation of dye, which leads to the visualization of an implantation site.

So you can see these implantation sites as blue bands.

This is not truly quantifiable.

And what we saw was that while one-third of our controls had implantation sites, not a single one of our PMOS-like mice had any implantation sites.

So we conclude that these PMOS-like mice suffer from implantation failure.

So what we wanted to do next was to analyze the NK cells in our controls that were pregnant and compare them with the nonpregnant controls, and then compare those further with our PMOS-like mice.

And to our surprise, we could see that following superovulation, we could no longer see this increase of uterine NK cells in our PMOS-like mice.

However, we could still see that the subsets were altered in our model, with still an increased proportion of conventional NK cells.

We could also see that the maturation was still impaired following superovulation.

### Conclusions

So all in all, we show that these PMOS-like mice have suffered from implantation failure,
