## Case 1 — initial presentation

This is a 7-year, 2-month-old White girl who presents to the clinic with a history of isolated breast development. Her parents report that they noticed breast development approximately two months earlier, with no associated body odor. There is no history of exposure to beauty creams or other potential sources of estrogen.

The family history indicates that her mother had menarche at age 12 years, with no relatives showing evidence of early pubertal development. Review of systems is negative for neurological symptoms, vaginal discharge, or acne.

Physical examination reveals a height of 118 cm at the 25th percentile and a weight of 23 kg at the 50th percentile, with an annualized height velocity of 6 cm/year. Her midparental height is at the 40th percentile. She has bilateral Tanner stage II breast development, with no axillary or pubic hair.

The growth curve indicates that the patient has been growing along the 25th percentile for quite some time. Her weight has edged up a little toward the 50th percentile as she approaches age 7. It is a fairly unremarkable growth curve.

## Recommendation 1 — watchful waiting

For girls who present with thelarche at Tanner stage B2 between ages 7 and 8 years, we suggest watchful waiting with periodic physical examinations every 4 to 6 months rather than immediately performing laboratory testing and/or radiological imaging. The certainty of evidence is very low.

A technical remark is that this recommendation is not intended to discourage primary care providers from referring these patients to a specialist, and it does not apply to girls who exhibit central nervous system (CNS) findings. This is very important because, even though this is likely to be a variation of normal development, the possibility of true endocrine pathology cannot be excluded.

## Background and geographic variation

An increasing prevalence of thelarche has been documented in 7- to 8-year-old girls. It remains unclear, however, whether this situation should be considered pathological or a variation of normal pubertal development.

The panel investigated several health-related outcomes in this group of patients, including adult height, psychological distress, missed pathogenic intracranial abnormalities, age at menarche, and long-term health risks, including estrogen-dependent cancers.

The data from Herman-Giddens and colleagues, published several years ago, are well known to most people in the audience. They indicate that a number of girls in the United States have breast development between ages 7 and 8—about 5% of White girls and about 15% of African American girls. At least in North America, finding breast development in this age group is therefore not that uncommon.

There are a couple of caveats about this study. First, these patients were not examined by palpation; this was based on observation, so some may have had lipomastia rather than breast development. Second, these patients were seeking medical attention. They were not assessed at their schools. This was a selected group seeking medical care, so the finding may apply to this particular group but may not necessarily be as common in the rest of the world.

A paper by our colleague Anders Juul and coauthors indicates that this is not a universal finding. In Europe, particularly Northern Europe, the prevalence of breast development between ages 7 and 8 is not as high as it is in the United States. The pathological significance therefore depends somewhat on which part of the world you are in. It is important to keep that in mind.

## Systematic review and Evidence-to-Decision factors

We identified 20 uncontrolled, single-arm studies of premature thelarche that reported its natural history, rate of progression, and risk factors for progression to central precocious puberty. The systematic review did not identify evidence regarding the potential advantages of immediate laboratory and imaging evaluation versus a period of observation without further testing.

Several studies of the evolution of this condition revealed a wide range of progression to central precocious puberty, from 7.7% to 39%.

Although it depends on the context, country, and health system, the resources required for the investigation and treatment of central precocious puberty may be significant. Evaluation—and treatment if indicated—of all girls who present with thelarche between ages 7 and 8 would require significantly more resources than watchful waiting.

There is also a wide range of acceptance of early puberty among the families of these girls. We encounter this every day in clinic, with some parents expressing significant anxiety about the condition.

The panel judged that there is insufficient evidence to estimate the benefits of laboratory and imaging evaluation in 7- to 8-year-old girls presenting with thelarche, while recognizing that the certainty of this evidence is very low. Indirect evidence on the natural history of girls with early breast development between ages 7 and 8 suggests that thelarche may not progress and may, in fact, regress in some cases.

An inability to reliably follow a watchful-waiting strategy—such as poor adherence to medical visits or having to travel a long distance to access subspecialty care—may be a reason to proceed with additional evaluation. This is a very important technical remark. If the patient will return for subsequent clinic visits, fine. If not, you may have to decide on a more aggressive initial evaluation.

## Case 1 — four-month follow-up

In this case, the patient is seen again four months later at age 7 years, 6 months. Her breast development has advanced to Tanner stage III, and her height velocity has increased to 8 cm/year. She has no pubic or axillary hair development, but the family reports subtle mood changes that she had not exhibited before.

Her bone age is compatible with the standard for a 9-year-old girl, even though she is now 7½ years old. There has been a change.

## Recommendation 3 — basal ultrasensitive LH first

In girls and boys with suspected precocious puberty, we suggest an initial evaluation with a basal serum LH level measured by an ultrasensitive assay rather than initially performing a GnRH or GnRHa stimulation test to distinguish those with central precocious puberty from those without the condition. The certainty of evidence is very low. It is very important to emphasize that point.

In this case, the random serum LH is **1.0 IU/L by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA)**.

## Evidence for rapidly progressive CPP

The patient appears to show evidence of rapidly progressive central precocious puberty. Breast development has advanced by one Tanner stage over a relatively short period of less than six months.

In addition, the basal serum LH measured by ultrasensitive assay is >0.25 IU/L by ECLIA, which points to the diagnosis of central precocious puberty independent of sex, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), bone age, or etiology.

The review team identified 11 case series regarding the rate of progression of central precocious puberty. Most were retrospective and examined factors that might predict rapid pubertal progression, including bone-age advancement, serum LH and estradiol, and uterine and ovarian morphology.

Overall, the proportion of patients who exhibited slowly progressive precocious puberty ranged from 33% to 67%—approximately one-third to two-thirds of all patients. Several publications described girls with onset of thelarche between ages 7 and 8 who were not treated with a GnRH agonist but achieved a normal adult height and had menarche at a normal age.

Several papers have examined this issue. One study from China asks in its title, “Do Most 7- to 8-Year-Old Girls with Early Puberty Require Extensive Investigation and Treatment?” I want to focus on two words: **most** 7- to 8-year-olds and **extensive** investigation. Our panel believes that the answer to this question is no.

Why do we say that? When you examine patients between ages 7 and 8 who have breast development alone, and assess markers such as bone age, LH, ovarian volume, and uterine volume, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves indicate that the ability of these markers to predict progression to rapidly progressive puberty is relatively low. The best “marker” may be bone age, and in this case its sensitivity is only 0.72.

From our perspective, an invasive workup using these parameters is not supported for every patient who presents between ages 7 and 8 with breast development alone.

## Justification and conclusion

The diagnosis of rapidly progressive precocious puberty is based on rapid changes in pubertal development, marked increases in height velocity, and fast advancement of bone maturation. Patients with this condition are strong candidates for treatment with a GnRH agonist, although access to treatment may limit feasibility in some contexts.

The impact of GnRH agonist treatment on adult height appears to be greatest at younger ages. Therefore, treatment of girls with onset of thelarche between ages 7 and 8 may not have a significant effect on final height.
