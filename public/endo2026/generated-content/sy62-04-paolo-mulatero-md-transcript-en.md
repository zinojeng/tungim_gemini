Moderator: Our next speaker is Paolo Mulatero, speaking on medical therapy for primary aldosteronism: new agents and new goals.

Paolo Mulatero, MD: Thank you, and thanks to the organizers for inviting me to discuss this topic today. I will go a little bit faster than predicted because of the short time. These are my disclosures.

After last year's Endocrine Society guideline, there was a little bit of a paradigm shift in the approach to primary aldosteronism, and the main recommendation is that all individuals with hypertension are suggested to be screened for primary aldosteronism. This would identify a lot of patients who potentially can be treated with either medical treatment or surgery. These are the criteria for a positive screen. I will not go into detail.

Already after screening, there is a population of patients who could be treated directly. These are patients who do not consider surgery an option or are not candidates for surgery because of comorbidity. They could be treated directly with antialdosterone therapy. At the moment that means an MRA, and still an MRA, but perhaps in the future also an aldosterone synthase inhibitor.

Another population that can be treated directly consists of patients who potentially could be treated surgically, but whose probability of having unilateral disease is so low that it is not worthwhile pursuing subtype diagnosis. The phenotype is so mild that the probability of unilateral disease is extremely low. Again, these patients should be medically treated.

Patients who are negative on an aldosterone suppression test should also be medically treated, because the probability of unilateral disease is extremely low. Finally, there are patients who display primary aldosteronism because of a florid phenotype or a positive suppression test, but who display bilateral disease on adrenal venous sampling. These are the classes.

At the moment, we can only use mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. Aldosterone synthase inhibitors are coming. There are a few differences between the two. In the case of an aldosterone synthase inhibitor, it is like turning off the tap. In the case of an MRA, it is like plugging the sink.

Going into detail, a potential advantage of an aldosterone synthase inhibitor is that we block not only the effect of aldosterone mediated by the mineralocorticoid receptor, but also the very rapid, non-genomic, non-MR-mediated effects of aldosterone, for example effects mediated by GPER. We do not know how important these are in clinical practice, but this is a potential advantage.

By contrast, with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, we block activation of MR not only by aldosterone but also by other factors, for example cortisol, Rac1, or others.

At the moment, we have spironolactone and eplerenone. Traditionally, spironolactone is a potent MR antagonist, but it also displays antiandrogenic and progestogenic effects. Male patients can have erectile dysfunction or gynecomastia, and fertile women can have menstrual irregularities. Eplerenone is less potent, has a shorter half-life, should be given twice daily, and is not available in many countries. Amiloride is a potential option for those who cannot be treated with an MRA.

As you can see, gynecomastia or breast pain appears already at low doses of spironolactone in 10% of patients. The side effects are dose-dependent and reach even 50% in patients treated with a high dose of spironolactone.

We now have two nonsteroidal MRAs: esaxerenone, which is available only in Japan, and finerenone, which is widely available. Finerenone has the peculiarity of lower penetration into the aldosterone-sensitive distal nephron, so there is less effect on potassium handling and less of the blood-pressure-lowering effect mediated by blocking aldosterone. That is the reason finerenone is much less potent than spironolactone in terms of blood-pressure reduction.

We do, however, have some prospective studies in primary aldosteronism. This one is a study performed in Chongqing. Spironolactone and finerenone were started at 20 milligrams and titrated to 40 milligrams in patients with, let us say, mild primary aldosteronism. But there were twice as many patients with unilateral primary aldosteronism in the spironolactone group as in the finerenone group—10 versus five.

The blood-pressure reduction was similar between the two, but in most cases renin levels were still suppressed, so the doses were not enough to completely block the effect of aldosterone. We also have to say that hypokalemia persisted in two patients treated with finerenone.

This other trial was a single-arm trial. The authors displayed quite good reductions in office systolic and diastolic blood pressure and in daytime blood pressure. However, plasma renin activity was desuppressed in only 32% of cases, and complete biochemical success was achieved in only 29% of patients.

Finally, in this study, because of a problem importing eplerenone into Chile, eplerenone was substituted with finerenone. You can see that the renin level dropped after the switch, and in general the clinical and biochemical response was much worse with finerenone.

Esaxerenone, as I said, is available only in Japan, so I will go very quickly. It is very efficient in reducing blood-pressure levels. Generally it is well tolerated, with no gynecomastia. Of course, there is an increase in potassium level, as expected. In this trial, the authors also displayed reductions in microalbuminuria and natriuretic peptide in patients with primary aldosteronism treated with esaxerenone.

Let us move to aldosterone synthase inhibitors. It has been a theoretically good target for ages, but the problem was achieving selectivity in inhibiting aldosterone synthase.

Aldosterone synthase performs three reactions—11β-hydroxylation, 18-hydroxylation, and 18-oxidation—to convert deoxycorticosterone, or DOC, to aldosterone. The enzyme 11β-hydroxylase converts 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol. CYP11B1 and CYP11B2 are very similar in structure. There are only 35 amino-acid differences between the two enzymes, and only two residues are really important in conferring aldosterone synthase activity. If we substitute the two relevant CYP11B2 residues into CYP11B1, 11β-hydroxylase becomes an aldosterone synthase enzyme.

Many drugs have been tried to block aldosterone synthase. Here you can see LCI699, which displayed quite good blood-pressure reduction in patients with primary aldosteronism, but this came at the price of also suppressing CYP11B1 and reducing cortisol levels, while greatly increasing 11-deoxycortisol and DOC. This drug is now known as osilodrostat and is used for the treatment of Cushing syndrome.

We have four aldosterone synthase inhibitors, two of which have been tested in primary aldosteronism. Baxdrostat is the most widely studied drug. In this phase 2 trial in patients with resistant hypertension, you can see a dose-dependent blood-pressure reduction with the drug.

They also performed a phase 3 trial, BaxHTN, with a quite complex design: placebo and two different doses of baxdrostat, then most patients went on 2 milligrams of baxdrostat, followed by a double-blind randomized-withdrawal phase. You can see quite a large reduction in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Interestingly, there was a further blood-pressure reduction in the second randomized phase after placebo withdrawal. Again interestingly, even after withdrawal of the drug, the aldosterone level increased only slightly from week 24 to week 32 of the study.

The reduction in the aldosterone-to-renin ratio was directly correlated with the level of baxdrostat achieved in the patient. The degree of blood-pressure reduction was directly correlated with reductions in aldosterone level and the aldosterone-to-renin ratio, and inversely correlated with the increase in plasma renin activity.

In this 24-hour ambulatory blood-pressure-monitoring study, similar to the other, the authors displayed a huge blood-pressure reduction with baxdrostat in patients with resistant hypertension. You can see blood-pressure reduction at all different times of the day, and a large increase, from 16% to 70%, in the proportion of patients with blood pressure at target.

They also performed a safety study with an ACTH stimulation test before and after baxdrostat. Cortisol levels, both basal and after ACTH, were not significantly affected by treatment with baxdrostat.

There was also a study without a placebo control performed in patients with primary aldosteronism, the SPARK study. The dose of baxdrostat was progressively increased from 2 milligrams up to 8 milligrams, much higher than previously used in other trials. You can see a very large blood-pressure reduction and a 97% reduction in the aldosterone-to-renin ratio. Hypokalemia was corrected in all patients. We can see quite a large increase in DOC, which is expected because of the very high doses used in this trial.

Now there is a BAX-PA trial ongoing: baxdrostat 2 milligrams titrated to 4 milligrams versus placebo, then everybody goes on baxdrostat, and then there is another randomized-withdrawal phase.

I can show you just a few patients from my unit. You can see patient 1. We do not know whether it is placebo or baxdrostat, but I guess it is placebo because blood pressure is not reduced during the blinded treatment. Then everybody goes on baxdrostat 2 milligrams. You can see that there is a huge drop in blood-pressure level, so we had to stop verapamil, which was the background therapy.

Patient 2 probably was on active treatment with baxdrostat. You can see the huge blood-pressure reduction, and in the open phase we had to stop the background therapy. In the third patient, there was an even larger blood-pressure reduction, so we really had to stop olmesartan at eight weeks. We have a fourth patient who just started and probably is on active treatment.

Lorundrostat is another aldosterone synthase inhibitor. This was the phase 2 Target-HTN trial in patients with uncontrolled or resistant hypertension. There was not a dose-dependent reduction in blood-pressure levels; the reductions were similar with 50 milligrams and 100 milligrams. Interestingly, there was a much larger effect of the drug in patients with obesity and those under thiazide treatment.

But this was not confirmed in the phase 3 Launch-HTN trial in patients with uncontrolled hypertension or resistant hypertension, all taking diuretics this time. There was no difference between body mass index above or below 30, and again there was no dose-dependent effect.

There is another trial, Advance-HTN, in which patients were all taking olmesartan plus a diuretic, with or without amlodipine. Again, there was similar blood-pressure reduction with 50 or 100 milligrams.

Vicadrostat has been tested only in patients with chronic kidney disease. I can tell you that it will be licensed only in association with empagliflozin, so it will probably not be available for patients with primary aldosteronism or hypertension.

Then we had dexfadrostat, which was tested in this phase 2 trial with three doses, a placebo run-in, and a placebo-withdrawal phase. Again there was a large, 92% reduction in the aldosterone-to-renin ratio and a reduction in 24-hour blood pressure, both systolic and diastolic. The effect was visible with all three dosages.

In this case, during the withdrawal phase we observed practically a restoration of blood pressure, potassium, and the other parameters, in contrast with baxdrostat. There was similar efficacy in unilateral versus bilateral disease. As observed with baxdrostat, there was an increase in DOC, but probably without an effect on clinical parameters.

Unfortunately, it seems that dexfadrostat development will be abandoned, so at the moment we will be left only with baxdrostat for the treatment of primary aldosteronism.

One important point is that, as others said before, adherence to antihypertensive therapy is a problem in patients with hypertension, because 30% to 50% of patients do not take the therapy. It is very expensive, or not possible, to perform therapeutic drug monitoring to see whether the patient is taking the drug. With an aldosterone synthase inhibitor, we will just measure aldosterone and check whether the patient is taking the drug.

To summarize: MRA blockade blocks all MR-mediated effects, whether mediated by aldosterone, cortisol, or other factors, but it has no effect on rapid non-genomic, non-MR-mediated effects. Spironolactone and eplerenone are available, with different problems. Esaxerenone is available only in Japan, and finerenone is probably too weak for patients with primary aldosteronism.

Aldosterone synthase inhibitors block all aldosterone-mediated effects, both rapid and genomic, but may lack effects on non-aldosterone MR-mediated pathways. These drugs will allow us to evaluate treatment adherence. At the moment, only baxdrostat seems to be in the running to become an antialdosterone therapy for patients with primary aldosteronism.

Thank you very much for your attention.
