### Opening and definitions

Good morning, and thank you very much for this session. I'm really happy to be here to talk about this topic. I have nothing to disclose related to this particular topic.

This is a highly specific topic. Still, it is quite complex and potentially broad, so I will try to keep it as simple as possible and give you some insights into the mechanism, clinical presentation, and management of ectopic ossification.

Starting from the definition, ectopic ossification is the abnormal formation of bone in extraskeletal subcutaneous tissues and, eventually, skeletal muscle, tendons, and surrounding soft tissues. It is an active process. The first message is that it has to be distinguished from calcinosis or ectopic calcification, in which we just have deposition of calcium and phosphate crystals in nonmineralized tissues.

We have different types of ectopic ossification, and the most prevalent ones are acquired, not genetic. They are considered an abnormal tissue-repair response following trauma. It can follow surgery, fracture, burn, or neurologic trauma. We also have specific genetic forms of ectopic ossification, and these are the main topic of my talk today.

The other important message is that ectopic ossification has nothing to do with serum calcium, phosphate, or calcium–phosphate product. Again, this differs from ectopic calcification, which can be related to serum calcium, phosphate, and the calcium–phosphate product.

### Classification and the Gαs–cAMP pathway

This is the classification of ectopic ossification. As I just said, we can distinguish acquired forms of traumatic origin, with all the triggering factors shown here, from the genetic forms. Basically, we have two genetic forms. I will talk a little about both, even if my talk will focus mainly on this one. You have already heard about Albright hereditary osteodystrophy, or AHO, and pseudohypoparathyroidism. I am also introducing progressive osseous heteroplasia, or POH, which is the extreme end of the PHP/AHO phenotypic spectrum.

Why does bone form where it should not? You have seen this cartoon. I'm not going to go through the classification and different nomenclature. It is not interesting this morning. I mean, it is interesting for me and a few others, but I don't think for you.

You have learned that we are not talking about just one disease; we are facing many disorders. Some are highly overlapping, and one specific signaling pathway is disrupted: the Gαs–cyclic AMP pathway. We have mutations up here at the parathyroid hormone/parathyroid hormone-related peptide receptor type 1, or PTH1R, then going down to the Gαs level, and then to PKA and PDE defects in acrodysostosis. Genes acting within the nucleus can also give rise to similar phenotypes. It is a very complex but relevant picture, and this is why a European group proposed renaming this group of disorders.

If we think about ectopic ossification, however, we should focus on Gαs. Across this entire spectrum, the diseases linked with ectopic ossification so far are those caused by genetic alterations affecting Gαs. These include the different PHP subtypes with coding GNAS mutations, AHO, and POH. Mutations in the other genes shown here, as well as methylation defects at the GNAS locus, do not appear to be associated with ectopic ossification.

### GNAS as a brake on osteogenic differentiation

Many studies have tried to link Gαs defects to ectopic ossification. Early work, mainly in murine models, included studies by groups involving Frederick Kaplan and Bob Pignolo in Philadelphia. These studies clearly showed that knocking down Gαs shifted the fate of mesenchymal progenitor cells, pushing them toward osteogenic differentiation and bone formation.

It is now quite clear that GNAS physiologically acts as a brake on ectopic bone formation: it keeps the system inhibited. Many other studies using conditional-knockout mouse models have confirmed this, as have studies using human in vitro models, including the work from our group shown on the right. Another important point is that we are talking about a predominantly intramembranous process. This makes it different from other types of ectopic ossification because the bone tends to form without passing through a cartilage intermediate.

As you heard earlier, GNAS is a complex imprinted locus. We have multiple transcripts, with shared exons 2 through 13 and distinct first exons that give specificity to the different transcripts. In addition to Gαs, there is an extra-large αs transcript and protein, XLαs, which can also activate cyclic AMP and is paternally expressed.

Patients with the most severe form of ectopic ossification, POH, carry the mutation on the paternal allele. Initially, it was thought that mutations in exons 2 through 13, which are shared by Gαs and XLαs, might explain the more severe phenotype because they could disrupt two gene products rather than one. By contrast, a mutation in Gαs exon 1 might lead to a less severe phenotype. We now know that this does not provide the explanation. We still do not understand why some patients have more severe forms and others have less severe forms.

### Hedgehog and Wnt/β-catenin signaling

Ectopic ossification in GNAS disorders is not linked only to cyclic AMP. This is a very elegant study to which the next speaker contributed. It shows that Gαs physiologically inhibits another master regulator of osteogenic differentiation, the Hedgehog signaling pathway. When Gαs is functioning normally, Hedgehog signaling is kept under restraint. When Gαs is disrupted, the pathway is upregulated, mesenchymal progenitor cells are recruited within soft tissues, and they are pushed toward osteoblast differentiation.

Importantly, this effect has been shown to be PKA-mediated. Hedgehog signaling was demonstrated to be both necessary and sufficient to cause heterotopic ossification in these models. If the pathway is modified genetically or pharmacologically, bone formation can be inhibited. This is important from a therapeutic point of view.

At the other end of the slide, activating Gαs mutations lead to fibrous dysplasia, either isolated or as part of McCune–Albright syndrome. In this setting there is impaired bone formation within the skeleton, rather than bone formation in soft tissue. This is thought to be linked to upregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. We now think that GNAS normally helps maintain a balance between these pathways, preserving appropriate bone formation and preventing bone from forming in tissues where it should not.

We are not talking about the skeleton today, but GNAS does not regulate only osteoblast differentiation in soft and subcutaneous tissues. It can also regulate osteocytes and osteoclasts. Studies mainly from Emily Germain-Lee's group demonstrated, at least in murine models, that the skeletal phenotype depended on which parental allele was disrupted. Paternal Gnas disruption led to a more fragile bone phenotype, shifting toward greater bone resorption and less bone formation. These findings have not been strongly demonstrated in human models.

GNAS disruption can also modify the microenvironment of the subcutaneous tissue in which resident mesenchymal progenitor cells are found. We do not yet understand this very well. Depending on whether the ectopic ossification is superficial or deep, the disturbed microenvironment appears to involve different sites within the subcutaneous tissue. In the superficial ossification seen in AHO or PHP, the crucial site may be around the hair follicles, where progenitor cells are recruited and differentiate toward osteoblasts. This does not appear to happen in the same way in deeper forms of ossification.

### Distinguishing GNAS-related ossification from FOP

It is very important to distinguish GNAS-related ossification—POH, AHO, and PHP, however we want to name them—from fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva, or FOP. These are different diseases with different characteristics.

FOP is a very rare but severe form of ectopic ossification caused by activating mutations in ACVR1, activin A receptor type 1, which upregulate BMP–SMAD signaling. Onset is early, usually before the first decade, and ossification is severe. It begins in skeletal muscle and becomes progressively more impairing. Children can be recognized by a characteristic great-toe malformation. The pattern of progression is also quite reproducible, typically starting in the back and neck and then extending downward. The disease is severely disabling, and life expectancy is shortened.

By contrast, GNAS-related ossification is a spectrum. POH is the most severe end, but more superficial ossification can occur in the different PHP subtypes and in AHO. The mechanisms are different. GNAS-related ossification is associated with an imbalance involving increased Hedgehog signaling and is predominantly intramembranous.

In FOP, the activating ACVR1 mutation upregulates BMP signaling, and inflammation is crucial. Patients have a basal inflammatory response in affected tissues, with recruitment of macrophages and monocytes. On top of this chronic inflammation, even minor trauma, such as an intramuscular injection or vaccination, may trigger a flare-up, either worsening pre-existing ossification or leading to new bone formation. The ossification is endochondral, with an intermediate cartilage phase.

Acquired heterotopic ossification is mechanistically more similar to FOP than to POH or PHP because inflammation also plays a crucial role. There is an abnormal tissue-repair response, with recruitment of inflammatory cells and release of cytokines such as interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor. Both endochondral and, sometimes, intramembranous ossification can occur.

### Clinical spectrum

Focusing on the clinical findings, we have limited information because these diseases are rare. Ectopic ossification is present by definition in all patients with POH. It is also highly prevalent in PPHP/AHO and occurs in a substantial proportion of patients with PHP1A. It has been reported only very sporadically in PHP1B, which is associated with GNAS methylation defects, and has not been reported in patients with acrodysostosis.

There is no scientific evidence that inflammation or trauma contributes to ectopic bone formation in GNAS-related conditions. Nevertheless, ectopic bone often develops at sites subjected to high pressure loads, such as the heel. Microtrauma or chronic trauma might therefore predispose a tissue to ectopic bone formation.

The phenotypes are different. The most common phenotype in AHO or PHP is superficial ectopic ossification that does not extend deeply into skeletal muscle or tendons. Ectopic ossification may precede the other signs of disease, including hormone resistance, and it tends to progress slowly over time. At the extreme end, POH produces deep, highly progressive ossification. We have clinical criteria for POH. One point to emphasize is that these patients usually have a lean phenotype, may have a history of intrauterine growth restriction, and typically have very early onset.

The subtypes are not always sharply separated. Some PHP patients have deep and progressive ossification, and some patients classified as POH show features of AHO. This was demonstrated in a study of 111 individuals with confirmed GNAS mutations and has been replicated by other groups, including ours.

Diagnosis should be primarily clinical. If there is any concern about FOP, biopsy should be avoided because it may trigger a flare-up and worsen the ossification. Imaging is usually unnecessary unless the anatomic extent must be defined, for example when surgical treatment is being considered. Surgery is not contraindicated in GNAS-related ossification.

The lesions are heterogeneous. They can appear as small osteoma cutis-like papules, mobile plaques, larger nodules, or more extensive areas of ossification.

### Imprinting and renal PTH resistance

Does imprinting have something to do with this? Imprinting is highly relevant in GNAS-related disorders. In a typical family, a maternal mutation leads to the more complete phenotype, with Albright hereditary osteodystrophy and multihormone resistance. Paternal inheritance generally leads to Albright hereditary osteodystrophy without hormone resistance, although there are exceptions. Ectopic ossification can occur with either parental inheritance.

Let me briefly digress to show why imprinting is crucial. The kidney is one of the most important organs in which Gαs imprinting has a physiologic role, and renal PTH resistance is central to the pathophysiology that gave pseudohypoparathyroidism its name.

In the proximal renal tubule, the paternal Gαs allele is silenced. If a mutation is present on the maternal allele, functional Gαs expression is therefore markedly reduced. In the distal tubule, Gαs is biallelically expressed, so a maternal mutation still leaves approximately 50% expression and function.

This is clinically relevant. In the proximal tubule, PTH normally promotes vitamin D activation and phosphaturia. A maternal mutation therefore produces PTH resistance, whereas a paternal mutation does not. Preservation of Gαs expression in the distal tubule has consequences as well: these patients have a lower risk of hypercalciuria, which can modulate the severity of the hypocalcemic manifestations.

These are unpublished data from our group comparing cohorts with hypoparathyroidism and pseudohypoparathyroidism. The patients with PTH resistance had higher serum calcium levels but lower urinary calcium, as expected.

### Genotype, parental origin, sex, and age

Bone and subcutaneous tissues are different because there is no evidence that Gαs is imprinted in these tissues. An important study from Emily Germain-Lee's group examined 67 patients with maternally or paternally inherited GNAS mutations. It focused on subcutaneous ossification and showed a similar prevalence in PHP1A and PPHP: 69% in PHP1A and 72% in PPHP. GNAS imprinting did not appear to influence ossification in these tissues, and hormone resistance was not associated with the ossification phenotype.

Curiously, males were affected more often and more extensively than females—81% versus 57% in the study. We do not know why. There may be a sex-related factor, or testosterone might influence ectopic bone formation, but I do not have an answer.

The mutation type is also important. Deleterious mutations, particularly nonsense and frameshift variants, were associated with more frequent and extensive ossification than missense variants. The fourth point is that ossification seems to progress slowly over time in all patients. The greatest progression is observed during childhood and adolescence, but progression can continue later in life.

In recent years, we have been interested in defining the phenotype at different ages because diagnosis is often delayed. In a recent study with Agnès Linglart's group in Paris, we examined 117 children for whom detailed information was available from the first years of life. Among the major, more specific criteria, only ectopic ossification tended to present before two years of age. PTH resistance and brachydactyly tended to develop later, at roughly four years of age or beyond.

The less-specific minor criteria tended to appear earlier. TSH resistance, sometimes detected as hypothyroidism during neonatal screening, and severe early-onset obesity were highly prevalent before age two. We think that their combination should at least trigger clinical suspicion for an inactivating PTH/PTHrP signaling disorder or pseudohypoparathyroidism.

### Current management

What about management? We have very little—almost nothing. We can consider prophylactic strategies intended to prevent or limit heterotopic ossification and strategies intended to improve symptoms and function.

These recommendations are extracted from an international consensus statement proposed several years ago. Most intervention data come from non-genetic heterotopic ossification. Radiation therapy is used prophylactically after surgery in selected high-risk settings, such as hip arthroplasty. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are also widely used for postoperative prophylaxis in acquired heterotopic ossification.

Bisphosphonates have been proposed for GNAS-related disorders, but the results are highly controversial. We do not have sufficient evidence to recommend bisphosphonates for asymptomatic ectopic ossification in these patients.

Surgery is not contraindicated. There is a risk of recurrence, but surgery can be considered for a delimited superficial lesion that is painful, approaches a joint, or limits movement. The patient should be referred to a surgeon with experience in ectopic ossification.

Topical sodium thiosulfate has also been proposed. It is used mainly for calcinosis rather than true ossification, but there are reports of seven children with PHP1A treated with topical sodium thiosulfate, with clinical and radiologic improvement reported.

### Emerging therapies

What about the future? If you enter POH into ClinicalTrials.gov, the result is disappointing: there are no studies. If you enter FOP, however, there is much more activity. This is relevant because the pathways interact and converge. Even if the primary mechanisms differ, it may become possible to target a shared downstream pathway in both diseases.

For FOP, palovarotene, a retinoic acid receptor-γ agonist, has already been approved. Garetosmab, an anti-activin A monoclonal antibody, has completed a phase 3 study. Other compounds target BMP receptors, retinoic acid signaling, Hedgehog signaling, mTOR, HIF-1α, Wnt/β-catenin, inflammation, and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition. Hopefully, some of these pathways will eventually become therapeutic targets in POH and PHP as well.
