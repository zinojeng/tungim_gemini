## Recommendation 4: routine brain MRI

This is our recommendation: in girls ages 6.0 to 8.0 years and boys ages 8.0 to 9.0 years with central precocious puberty who do not have any evidence of central nervous system abnormalities, we suggest that brain MRI should not be routinely performed. An important technical remark is that this does not apply to patients who have symptoms or findings of CNS abnormalities, in which case, of course, a brain MRI should be obtained regardless of sex or age.

## Systematic review: benefits and harms of universal MRI

The panel decided that the systematic review should include missed pathologic findings, adverse consequences related to incidental findings, and adverse events related to undergoing brain MRI as outcomes of interest.

We had 35 uncontrolled studies across 19 countries, for a sample size of 5,541. The mean age was 7 years, and 83% of these patients were girls. The overall rate of CNS abnormalities was 18%, but pathologic findings—defined as brain tumors and hypothalamic hamartomas—were present in only 5.5%.

Among the 14 studies that allowed prevalence of pathologic findings to be assessed by age, the rate was 11% in girls younger than 6 years but only 1% in girls between 6 and 8 years. In boys, the prevalence was 28% in those younger than 8 years but 0% in boys between 8 and 9 years.

The panel judged that the desirable effect of brain MRI scanning in all children would be that no lesion would be missed. The undesirable effects of universal brain MRI scanning would include risks associated with sedation and IV contrast, anxiety for patients and their parents, cost, and the need for repeat imaging because of incidental findings. It was felt that avoiding unnecessary imaging could potentially enhance health equity.

Our justification for this recommendation—which, again, is conditional and pertains only to girls ages 6 to 8 years and boys ages 8 to 9 years—is based on the following: there are extremely low rates of pathologic findings at these ages; the undesirable effects of routine brain MRI likely outweigh the small desirable effect of not missing a single abnormal finding; and the balance of effects likely favors not performing routine MRI. However, individual family risk tolerance must be taken into consideration through shared decision-making.

## Recommendation 5: routine genetic testing

This is Recommendation 5 for children with central precocious puberty: we suggest against routine genetic testing. When we are talking about genetic testing in this context, we are referring to targeted genetic testing for monogenic forms of central precocious puberty, including `MKRN3`, `DLK1`, and/or `MECP2`, rather than unbiased genomic sequencing. However, genetic testing should be considered in patients with familial central precocious puberty through shared decision-making with the family.

## Systematic review and Evidence-to-Decision considerations

Our systematic review considered the following outcomes important: missing a genetic cause; the timeliness of identifying comorbidities, because some monogenic forms are associated with comorbidities; psychological stress related, for example, to finding variants of uncertain significance, which I know we all deal with all the time; the timeliness of identifying other affected family members; and the timeliness of treatment.

For example, one could make a preclinical diagnosis and perform surveillance before a child even begins to have symptoms. However, we could identify no studies comparing outcomes from genetic testing versus no genetic testing.

Our Evidence-to-Decision factors included a number of other considerations: genetic testing requires specialized laboratories and medical centers; it could potentially reduce health equity; its feasibility and acceptability are likely to vary; it may be more useful in familial central precocious puberty; it could potentially obviate the need for a brain MRI; and exactly how genetic testing would translate into health-related outcomes remains unclear.

Our justification for this conditional recommendation is based on the lack of studies comparing the benefits and potential harms of genetic testing versus no genetic testing; variability in the ease and availability of genetic testing across countries and centers; and the conclusion that testing should be considered in cases of familial central precocious puberty.

## Monogenic causes and `MKRN3`

I want to remind you of the monogenic forms of central precocious puberty that have been identified. Far and away, the most common are loss-of-function mutations in `MKRN3`. `MKRN3` acts as a brake on pubertal onset, so a mutation releases that brake and permits early activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis.

It has now been shown that `MKRN3` mutations account for 33% to 46% of familial cases of central precocious puberty and approximately 9% of cases overall.

Another really interesting thing about `MKRN3`—and this is true of `DLK1` as well—is that it is maternally imprinted and paternally transmitted. Here is a representative three-generation pedigree. We see that the grandmother had precocious puberty. She passed the mutation to her son, but because the gene is maternally imprinted, he did not exhibit the phenotype. He then passed it on to three of his offspring, all of whom had precocious puberty.

Just to remind you, `MKRN3` mutations have now been reported across the globe, and even this map is, of course, out of date now.

The other really interesting finding is the striking difference in the ratio of girls to boys reported with `MKRN3` mutations, with far more girls than boys identified. There are now males who have been found to carry the mutation but do not exhibit the phenotype. Thus, `MKRN3` mutations have variable penetrance in males, and I am sure we will learn a lot more about this as time goes on.
