## Clinical problem and study design

**Konstantinos Stefanakis:** Think about a patient we all see in clinic. They may have obesity, insulin resistance, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or mildly elevated liver enzymes. At the epicenter may be metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), but only some patients have its dangerous form.

This matters because MASLD affects an enormous number of people worldwide. We are particularly interested in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and moderate-to-advanced fibrosis, stages F2–F3. These patients are entering a treatment era with newly approved and upcoming medications.

Liver biopsy can identify these patients, but it is costly, invasive, prone to sampling error, and impossible to scale to the population at risk. We also do not fully understand how circulating biochemistry corresponds to the signals a pathologist sees under the microscope. The practical bottleneck is therefore clear: if millions of patients are at risk, how can we identify those most likely to need treatment without biopsy?

We pooled multinational biopsy-proven cohorts spanning healthy controls through cirrhosis from metabolic and gastroenterology clinics. The dataset comprised 391 adults: 67 healthy controls and 324 participants with MASLD, including 207 with MASH, 148 with at-risk MASH, and 20 with cirrhosis or F4 fibrosis. Serum was available for 165 participants and EDTA plasma for 226.

Using SomaScan 7K, we measured 7,595 shared protein targets and anchored the analysis to three histologic endpoints: NAFLD Activity Score (NAS), fibrosis stage, and at-risk MASH.

## Histology-linked proteomic signatures

We found a strong, reproducible proteomic signal that was concordant across the two cohorts and sample matrices and robustly associated with all three histologic endpoints.

The biology was coherent. As NAS increased, metabolic-stress and immune-inflammatory pathways increased. As fibrosis progressed, signals related to matrix remodeling, extracellular-matrix signaling, and WNT/developmental signaling increased. We also identified a specific signature associated with at-risk MASH that consistently separated patients with the disease from the rest of the population in both serum and plasma.

This was not merely biology for biology's sake. The best-performing imaging-based noninvasive test had an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.67 for detecting MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis. A model combining five proteins with two clinical variables increased AUROC to 0.80 and outperformed the other evaluated noninvasive tests. The slide analysis reported an AUROC gain of 0.13 and a positive-predictive-value gain of 0.30.

The take-home message is that blood proteomics may enable a biology-based shift from traditional risk stratification to a more informed approach that reflects underlying mechanisms and better identifies patients for treatment. If scalable and combined with other tests, this could reduce unnecessary biopsies and potentially save hundreds of millions of dollars.

## Audience Q&A

**Audience member:** Did the proteomic markers correlate differently across stages of disease?

**Konstantinos Stefanakis:** Yes. We could not include everything in this short talk, but our longer oral presentation shows trajectories across NAS from 0 to 8 and fibrosis stage from 0 to 4. Many proteins progressively increased with NAS and fibrosis, while others progressively decreased. The trajectories were stable across clinical endpoints including MASH, MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis, and cirrhosis. We also identified signatures specific to MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis.

**Audience member:** [Question regarding FibroScan-based measures and correlations.]

**Konstantinos Stefanakis:** FibroScan was used, as were FibroScan-based risk-stratification indices such as Agile 3+. We correlated the shared proteins with noninvasive tests and with hormones and related mediators, including follistatin, activins, growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15), and adipokines.

In the longer presentation, we show a heat map of correlations between the leading proteins in the shared signature and other measures. In many cases, they correlate positively with GDF-15 or Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4). However, for distinguishing the clinically important group with MASH and F2–F3 fibrosis, neither FIB-4 nor the measured hormones performed as well as the proteomic model.

**Audience member:** Are you considering the signature as diagnostic, prognostic, or as a treatment-response marker that should change with therapy?

**Konstantinos Stefanakis:** This is a work in progress. The first step was a cross-sectional baseline analysis of the proteins. The next step is to combine proteomics with other omics to develop more powerful tests. The third step is prognostic work, which is under way. We have measurements from patients at the beginning and end of interventions, including surgery and other treatments, and will test whether the baseline signature predicts treatment response. We hope to identify still more informative predictors.

Thank you.
