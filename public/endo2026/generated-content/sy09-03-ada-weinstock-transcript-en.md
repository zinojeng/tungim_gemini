### Atherosclerosis, necrotic cores, and efferocytosis

The project I will discuss today focuses on atherosclerosis, the most dangerous disease associated with obesity because it underlies myocardial infarction and stroke. Atherosclerosis involves unresolved inflammation in the arterial wall. Lipid accumulates, immune cells accumulate in an attempt to clear it, and a vicious cycle of inflammation develops.

Microscopically, this is the arterial wall, and everything visible in the lumen here is plaque or lesion tissue that should not be present. The plaques contain many macrophages, which exacerbate and help drive the disease. You also see empty spaces that are considered among the most dangerous regions of the plaque. These are necrotic cores, where cells die but are not cleared. The cells become necrotic and more inflammatory, and degraded matrix accumulates. Because the arterial wall is exposed to high pressure, these unstable regions are particularly prone to rupture.

Macrophages are remarkable cells. In normal tissues throughout the body, millions of cells die naturally every day. Macrophages can ingest and clear them before they become necrotic and inflammatory through a specialized phagocytic pathway called efferocytosis. In atherosclerosis, the ability of macrophages to engulf dead cells is impaired for reasons that remain incompletely understood. This failure permits necrotic cores to form and expand.

### A caloric-restriction model that separates weight loss from lipid lowering

I began this project as a postdoctoral fellow with Ed Fisher at NYU, and my team at the University of Chicago is now continuing parts of it. We started with a simple question. People with obesity and atherosclerosis typically receive two interventions at the same time: lipid lowering, such as statin therapy, and lifestyle intervention or weight loss. Lipid lowering is extremely beneficial in reducing cardiovascular and atherosclerotic risk. We asked how weight loss might influence established disease independently of lipid lowering.

We turned to mouse models and adapted a caloric-restriction protocol published by Tony's laboratory more than 10 years ago. We use mice with LDL-receptor deficiency, allowing them to develop hyperlipidemia and atherosclerosis. They receive a high-fat, high-cholesterol (HFHC) diet for 24 weeks to establish both obesity and atherosclerosis. One group is then switched to caloric restriction: the amount of the same HFHC diet is reduced by 30%.

Instead of eating three slices of pizza every day, the mice eat two. It is not a healthier diet, just less of the same diet, and they lose weight because they eat less.

As expected, the mice lose weight and their metabolic parameters improve, including glucose tolerance. Their cholesterol levels, however, remain unchanged, which is a requirement of the model. Both groups remain hyperlipidemic.

When we examined atherosclerosis, we were surprised to find that two weeks of caloric restriction, despite persistent hyperlipidemia, produced several features of atherosclerosis resolution. These included fewer inflammatory plaque macrophages and smaller necrotic cores. Thus, caloric restriction in obesity produced multiple features of atherosclerosis resolution, with reduction of dangerous necrotic cores being especially important to us.

### Communication between adipose tissue and plaque

We asked whether signals from adipose tissue might influence plaque. We recently learned that the Fondation Leducq will fund our further study of communication between adipose tissue and atherosclerotic plaques.

To begin addressing whether such communication occurs during weight loss, we performed adipose-tissue transplantation studies. We collected visceral adipose tissue—epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT)—from mice with obesity before and after caloric restriction. We transplanted that tissue subcutaneously into lean mice with established atherosclerosis and examined their plaques two weeks later.

Recipients of adipose tissue from calorically restricted donors had smaller plaque necrotic cores. This suggests that something in adipose tissue communicates with plaque to resolve inflammation and reduce the necrotic core. The question is whether the signal involves secreted factors, cell trafficking, or both.

More than 10 years ago, an elegant study from Andrew Murphy's group showed that adipose-tissue macrophages produce interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). Secreted IL-1β can signal to macrophages and myeloid cells and exacerbate atherosclerosis in obesity. We asked whether caloric restriction interrupts this IL-1β-driven cycle between adipose tissue and plaque.

The Fisher laboratory repeated our transplantation experiments. Before I show those results, I will show IL-1β measurements from adipose tissue. In mice with obesity, deleting IL-1β from adipocytes did not substantially change adipose IL-1β levels. In contrast, myeloid-cell- or macrophage-specific IL-1β deficiency reduced adipose IL-1β. The reduced levels were comparable to those in mice undergoing caloric restriction. This indicates that caloric restriction reduces the amount of IL-1β produced by adipose-tissue macrophages.

To test whether adipose-derived IL-1β influences plaque, the Fisher laboratory transplanted adipose tissue from mice with obesity that were either wild type or had macrophage-specific IL-1β deficiency into lean mice with established atherosclerosis. Recipients of adipose tissue lacking macrophage IL-1β developed smaller necrotic cores, comparable to those in recipients of adipose tissue from calorically restricted donors.

These results show that caloric restriction reduces secretion of IL-1β from adipose-tissue macrophages and from adipose tissue more broadly. That may be one reason necrotic cores shrink with caloric restriction: fewer inflammatory signals are released from adipose tissue to act on plaque. A reduction in inflammation, however, is not necessarily the same as active resolution of disease. We therefore wanted to understand more broadly how adipose tissue changes during caloric restriction.

### Fcgr4/CD16A-positive macrophages after weight loss

We performed single-cell RNA sequencing of adipose immune cells before and after weight loss. We identified a new macrophage subtype that is rare in lean and obese conditions but markedly expands during caloric restriction. One hallmark gene is mouse `Fcgr4`. For simplicity, I will refer to its human counterpart, FCGR3A, as CD16A. CD16A is an Fc gamma receptor for IgG antibodies and participates in phagocytosis of antibody-bound antigens. These macrophages also express a broader phagocytosis-associated program.

To determine whether a similar macrophage population appears after weight loss in humans, we collaborated with Evan Rosen and Margo Emont, who had a dataset of subcutaneous adipose tissue sampled from patients before and after bariatric surgery. We found a macrophage population that expanded after weight loss and also expressed CD16A. We therefore think we have identified a macrophage subtype that accumulates with weight loss in both mice and humans.

We next asked whether this highly phagocytic population actually performs efferocytosis in atherosclerotic plaques. In mice, we used in situ staining for CD16A, CD68 as a macrophage marker, and TUNEL to detect apoptotic cells. During caloric restriction, CD16A-positive macrophages engulfed dead or dying cells in atherosclerotic lesions.

We also correlated the abundance of these cells with necrotic-core size. Mice with more CD16A-positive macrophages in visceral fat and plaque had smaller necrotic cores, suggesting that the cells contribute to necrotic-core shrinkage during caloric restriction.

So far, I have shown that caloric restriction in obesity leads to accumulation of macrophages that we call CR macrophages. We find them in both adipose tissue and atherosclerotic plaques. Caloric restriction promotes atherosclerosis resolution and reduces necrotic cores, possibly because these macrophages have an enhanced ability to engulf dead and dying cells.

### Is CD16A a marker or a functional efferocytosis receptor?

We next asked whether CD16A is merely a marker or is itself involved in efferocytosis. Two very talented people in the laboratory are working on this project: our postdoctoral fellow Dr. Lourdes Caceres and undergraduate researcher Hunter Bershtein. We have also been fortunate to receive support from some of our favorite foundations and NIH institutes.

To test whether CD16A is functionally involved in efferocytosis, we either knocked out CD16A in macrophages or overexpressed it, then measured how many dead cells the macrophages engulfed. CD16A-knockout macrophages showed reduced engulfment of dead and dying cells.

For the overexpression studies, we collaborated with Yun Fang's group at the University of Chicago. They generated nanoparticles that deliver CD16A mRNA to macrophages. Increasing CD16A mRNA and protein enhanced the macrophages' ability to engulf dead cells. Thus, CD16A is involved in efferocytosis: loss of CD16A impairs efferocytosis, while increased CD16A enhances it.

We then wanted to determine whether CD16A participates directly in the engulfment process or instead induces transcriptional or other changes that indirectly promote efferocytosis.

Hunter developed an in vitro model that tracks efferocytosis over time. Macrophages are labeled green and are fed apoptotic cells stained with a pH-sensitive dye. Live imaging detects macrophages that become red. The red signal indicates that a macrophage has ingested an apoptotic cell and delivered it to an acidified lysosome, where the pH-sensitive fluorophore becomes brighter. In the images, macrophages initially show only green signal and then acquire red signal as they ingest and begin degrading apoptotic cells.

### Direct binding of CD16A to apoptotic cells

Using this system, we asked whether CD16A on macrophages directly binds dying cells and promotes their engulfment. We performed two experiments. In one, we blocked CD16A on macrophages with blocking antibodies. In the other, we pretreated apoptotic cells with soluble CD16A. If CD16A binds the apoptotic-cell surface, soluble CD16A should occupy those binding sites before the cells are presented to macrophages.

In the live-imaging assay, CD16A-overexpressing macrophages again engulfed dead cells more efficiently than control macrophages. Pretreating apoptotic cells with soluble CD16A impaired macrophage engulfment. Blocking CD16A and other Fc receptors on macrophages with an Fc block also reduced efferocytosis. These in vitro findings indicate that CD16A directly binds apoptotic cells to mediate efferocytosis.

### Adipose-macrophage CD16A knockdown in vivo

Finally, we asked whether CD16A is required in vivo for efferocytosis and reduction of plaque necrotic cores. In collaboration with Myriam Aouadi at Karolinska Institutet, we delivered either control siRNA or CD16A-targeting siRNA to mice undergoing caloric restriction, reducing CD16A specifically in adipose-tissue macrophages.

The experimental groups included mice with obesity and established atherosclerosis, along with two caloric-restriction groups. One caloric-restriction group received control siRNA, while the other received CD16A siRNA. The images show the adipose-tissue macrophages after they engulfed the siRNA-delivery particles.

In plaque analyses, caloric restriction with control siRNA reduced necrotic cores, consistent with atherosclerosis resolution. When CD16A was knocked down in adipose-tissue macrophages, that improvement was impaired.

### Working model and conclusions

The model I have presented is that, during caloric restriction in obesity, communication between adipose tissue and plaque promotes atherosclerosis resolution, particularly reduction of necrotic cores. We think this involves both a decrease in inflammatory signaling, including reduced IL-1β, and accumulation of a newly identified macrophage subtype in adipose tissue. These macrophages have enhanced efferocytic capacity and may help shrink plaque necrotic cores.

I am a little over time. It looks as though we still have time for questions. Thank you.

**Audience member:** Do you know what triggers CD16A expression? Identifying that signal might be therapeutically useful.

### Q&A — caloric restriction versus weight loss

**Audience member:** From a clinical-applicability perspective, do you think this is a direct effect of adipose-tissue reduction and weight loss, or could caloric restriction produce it without weight loss?

**Ada Weinstock:** That is a very interesting question, and we have not yet considered it directly. I think the answer will depend on what adipose tissue ultimately secretes, because that signal may be key to recruiting these macrophages and producing the specific phenotype. My guess is that caloric restriction without major weight loss might still promote the phenotype, but it is something we need to think about and test. Thank you; that is a great question.
