## Case 3: clinical presentation and treatment decision

I will now present the third case and highlight some of the recommendations from this panel.

This is Case 3, a girl who presented with breast development at the age of 4 years. You can see from her growth chart that she exhibited growth acceleration and an advanced bone age when it was last performed, resulting in a predicted adult height of 162 cm.

To summarize, this girl presented with isolated breast development for 6–8 months, no vaginal bleeding, no CNS symptoms, no other symptoms, and no family history of central precocious puberty. She had growth acceleration, with an annualized growth velocity of 8.5 cm/year. Her current height was at approximately the 80th percentile, with a midparental target height near the 50th percentile. She had Tanner stage 3–4 breast development—more stage 4 than stage 3—and Tanner stage 1 pubic hair. Her basal serum LH using an ultrasensitive assay was 1.3 IU/L. She had a negative brain MRI and a bone age advanced by approximately 3 years, corresponding to 7.1 years.

This is, I think, in most people's opinion, quite clear-cut. She was started on a long-acting GnRH agonist. My rhetorical question is: What is actually the evidence for a gain in adult height in this particular girl?

## Systematic review: adult-height benefit of GnRH agonist therapy

This was reviewed by the Mayo Clinic team in a systematic review and meta-analysis, as illustrated here. The top part separates adult height in GnRH agonist-treated girls treated for at least 3 years from those treated for less than 3 years. In the longer-treatment subgroup, the pooled mean difference was almost 4 cm. You may not be able to see it, but it is true. Across both duration subgroups, the pooled adult-height mean difference was 2.74 cm (95% CI, 1.10–4.39) in a meta-analysis of 1,497 patients. The certainty of evidence was very low because the evidence was observational and the effects were imprecise.

Another way of looking at the evidence was to compare studies according to baseline predicted adult height above versus below 155 cm. The subgroup with baseline predicted adult height below 155 cm had a pooled mean difference of 2.50 cm (95% CI, −0.91 to 5.91). In the subgroup with predicted adult height above 155 cm, the pooled mean difference was 1.88 cm (95% CI, −1.37 to 5.14). The overall pooled effect was 2.13 cm (95% CI, 0.59–3.67), with no evidence of a subgroup difference.

These meta-analyses led to Recommendation 6: For many children with central precocious puberty, we suggest gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist treatment, although certain patient subgroups may not achieve a net benefit with this therapy. This is a conditional recommendation based on very-low-certainty evidence.

## Monthly versus longer-acting GnRH agonists

This is based on the meta-analysis, again conducted by the Mayo Clinic team, comparing monthly versus longer-acting GnRH agonists. Ten studies—3 randomized controlled trials and 7 observational studies—included 759 participants with a mean age of 8.32 ± 1.31 years.

For the degree of gonadotropin suppression, measured as peak LH, the pooled mean difference was −0.16 IU/L (95% CI, −0.50 to 0.19). The take-home message is that there was no meaningful difference between monthly and longer-acting formulations in the degree of gonadotropin suppression.

Similarly, the studies comparing growth velocity showed no meaningful difference between monthly and longer-acting GnRH agonists; the pooled mean difference was −0.18 cm/year (95% CI, −0.84 to 0.48).

This led us to Recommendation 7: In patients with central precocious puberty who will use a long-acting GnRH agonist in the long term, we suggest initiating therapy with a long-acting GnRH agonist preparation rather than initiating therapy with a monthly preparation for a period of time first. So, jump directly to the one you want to use.

## Adult-height consequences of untreated central precocious puberty

Now let us modify the case and discuss final height and adult height. We know from historic data—this is an old Danish thesis from 1961 by Erik Thamdrup, examining a nationwide series of untreated girls with central precocious puberty—that these were, by and large, girls who presented very early and had severe disease.

In this particular case, the girl had menarche at age 6 and ended up with an adult height of 135 cm, remarkably below her genetic potential. The thesis also presents a series of longitudinal growth curves from untreated central precocious puberty. Without doubt, in these severely affected historic series from Denmark, the United States, and elsewhere, adult height was compromised.

The actual girl you saw had a baseline predicted adult height of 162 cm, which is not remarkable. But let us imagine another case in which the predicted adult height was lower. For a 7-year-old girl with a height of 120 cm (−1 SD) and a bone age of 11 years, the prediction graph gives a predicted adult height of 146 cm.

## Audience poll: adding growth hormone

You have started a GnRH agonist, but would you add growth hormone in such a girl? Yes or no? This is a question that I would like you all to answer. Take your time and make your bid. Da-da! It was almost split, but the majority favored no addition of growth hormone.

Let us see what the guideline recommends. We suggest against the routine addition of growth hormone to a GnRH agonist in children with central precocious puberty.

This recommendation is based on a systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 observational studies involving 1,024 patients. The certainty of evidence was very low. The analysis did not demonstrate a meaningful improvement in adult height from adding growth hormone: the pooled mean difference was −0.16 cm (95% CI, −2.12 to 1.81).
