## Burden of diabetes and trial evidence

So as you all know, the number of people with diagnosed diabetes in the United States is increasing dramatically.

In 2002, it was about 12 million people.

In 2022, 20 years later, over 29 million people.

And when we see these figures, we tend to think about the problem of the increasing incidence of diabetes.

And clearly that is a problem.

The incidence of diabetes in the U.S. and globally is increasing.

But there are a number of other reasons for the increasing number of people with diagnosed diabetes that are not necessarily bad.

We've seen improved diabetes detection, population growth, population aging, increasing population diversity, and decreasing diabetes mortality, all of which contribute to the increasing number of people with diabetes globally.

So what do we know about diabetes prevention and interventions to delay or prevent the development of type 2 diabetes?

The evidence base for the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions is among the strongest data we have in medicine today.

There are four randomized controlled clinical trials: the Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study, the U.S. DPP, the Indian Diabetes Prevention Program, and the Chinese Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Study, that have shown that the incidence of type 2 diabetes can be reduced by 29 to 58 percent over three to six years.

In addition, the numbers needed to treat, that is the number of people who would need to be enrolled in these interventions to prevent one case of diabetes over three to six years, is very low.

These interventions are very effective.

Only five to seven people would need to be enrolled in the intervention to prevent one case of diabetes over three to six years.

All of these interventions have involved diet, physical activity, weight loss, and behavioral support.

There have been minor differences in the diets prescribed, but all have been reduced calorie, some low fat, others low carbohydrate, often plant-centered and high fiber.

Physical activity has been an integral component: 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week, generally operationalized as 30 minutes of brisk walking five or more days per week.

The goal has been modest weight loss, 5 to 7 percent over the intervention period, and most interventions have achieved about 5 percent weight loss.

And they have all involved intensive behavioral support involving ongoing self-monitoring, coaching, and education.

We also have compelling evidence from observational follow-up of these clinical trials showing that the intervention effects do persist over time.

The Finnish DPP showed a 43 percent risk reduction at seven years follow-up.

The U.S. DPP / Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study (DPPOS), a 34 percent reduction at 10 years and a persistent 25 percent reduction at 20 years.

And the Chinese Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Study, a remarkable 43 percent reduction at 20 years and a persistent 39 percent reduction in the incidence of diabetes at 30 years follow-up.

## Translation to population-level practice

The question remains, though, how do these interventions play out in routine clinical population-level environments?

And it's been highlighted that there are a number of issues with translating the results of these rigorous clinical trials to community practice.

The first limitation is that the recruitment of highly selected subjects for clinical trials may result in overestimation of intervention effectiveness in real-world practice.

And the second is if only a small proportion of eligible people actually are selected and participate in an intervention, a lot of achievable benefit is not achieved at a population level.

And this clearly is the fact with these randomized trials.

This slide just shows recruitment for the U.S. Diabetes Prevention Program, and 158,177 people were screened in order to randomize 3,819 people.

So in the DPP, though hugely effective, only 2.4% of people who were deemed to be potentially eligible were actually randomized.

This is going to maximize effectiveness, but clearly limit the population-level impact of this intervention.

## NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme

So what have we then observed in health-system and primary-care-level interventions?

There are two studies that I want to present.

One is the National Health Service Diabetes Prevention Programme that was implemented through English general practices.

So in England, they were able to recruit 94% of general practices in three waves, the first involving over 3,200 general practices, or 51% of the total in wave one.

About a quarter of practices were recruited in wave two, and a final quarter in wave three.

The diabetes prevention program then was delivered to eligible patients as a 9- to 12-month duration intensive lifestyle intervention.

The providers were picked through a competition of people offering rigorous programs.

Patients were eligible if they were over 18 years of age, and there were strict biochemical criteria.

You had to have either an HbA1c of 6.0% to 6.4%, higher than the levels for prediabetes recommended in the U.S., or a fasting glucose between 100 and 125 mg/dL.

Interestingly, there was no BMI criterion.

And uptake in the NHS DPP was actually pretty remarkable.

Through these general practices, 513,312 patients were referred to the DPP.

Of these, 271,208 (53%) had an initial assessment, and 101,175 (20%) attended at least 60% of the sessions.

To evaluate this, the investigators used difference-in-differences regression models to compare Waves 1 and 2 with Wave 3.

The models were adjusted appropriately for practice and population-level variables.

Diabetes outcomes were rigorously ascertained using the National Diabetes Audit, and outcomes were expressed as incidence rate ratios, or IRRs.

And this slide shows the results of the evaluation of the NHS DPP.

When wave 1 was compared to wave 3 practices, the incidence rate ratio was reduced by about 6%, and when wave 2 was compared to wave 3 practices, the risk reduction was about 7%.

So participants in the NHS DPP reduced the incidence of type 2 diabetes by about 6% at two years.

Waves 1 and 2 prevented about 8% of the expected incident cases of type 2 diabetes, and the number needed to treat was 37.

In other words, 37 people would need to be referred to the NHS DPP to prevent one case of type 2 diabetes at three years follow-up.

This is considerably higher than the number of 6 or 7 observed in the clinical trials, but is probably not unexpected in light of the fact that the intervention was only 9 to 12 months, whereas in the clinical trials it was 3 to 6 years, whatever the duration of the trial was.

## U.S. REALITY Research Network

The second example I want to present of population-level intervention or implementation is the U.S. REALITY Research Network results.

This is a CDC-funded study involving academic centers looking at the longer-term impact of National DPP prevention on diabetes incidence and cost-effectiveness.

The data I'm going to present come from three cohorts of U.S. health plan members and involved National DPP enrollees who attended one or more sessions versus matched eligible non-enrollees.

The programs offered were all CDC-recognized National DPP programs that involved at least 22 sessions over 12 months, and they could be in-person, hybrid, or even virtual.

The participant eligibility was very different from the English study and from the clinical trials in that it involved participants over the age of 18 with BMI ≥25 kg/m² (≥23 kg/m² if Asian), but you could be eligible based on HbA1c criteria, which were broader than those used in the English study, or simply a claims diagnosis of prediabetes, or a history of gestational diabetes with no glycemic criteria, or an elevated score on the ADA/CDC risk test, which is a sensitive but very nonspecific screening test for risk of prediabetes.

Unlike in the English study, uptake was relatively low, but the numbers were large. There were 130,840 individuals with prediabetes exposed to one or more sessions and 5,713,807 unexposed individuals in the study.

In the REALITY Study, we performed an individual participant-level meta-analysis with sequential matching on age, sex, criteria for eligibility, eligibility date, and social deprivation index.

Diabetes outcomes were rigorously ascertained using ICD codes, labs, and medications, and propensity-score-weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess hazard ratios.

And this slide shows the results from the three cohorts studied.

The hazard ratios ranged from 0.78 to 0.89.

The overall hazard ratio was 0.85, suggesting a 15% relative risk reduction in the incidence of type 2 diabetes over three years.

The absolute risk reduction was small, though, only 0.9%, and as a result, the number needed to treat here was 113 compared to 37 in the English study and six or seven in the clinical trials.

So 113 people would need to be enrolled in the National DPP to prevent one case of type 2 diabetes at three years follow-up.

And this, again, probably reflects the less intensive intervention, which is going to affect effectiveness, as well as the broader eligibility requirements, which will improve population reach but reduce the effectiveness if you're intervening on people who are at low risk for developing type 2 diabetes.

## Barriers to implementation

So now I want to turn to some of the barriers to targeted lifestyle interventions for diabetes prevention, and I'll go through each of these in sequence.

The first is defining the target population, and the question arises: should the target population be identified using the same criteria used to determine eligibility for the clinical trials?

And I think the answer clearly here is no.

If you use these restrictive eligibility criteria, you'll improve effectiveness, but the population reach, in other words, the impact at a population level, will be small.

And so the implications of using these broader criteria are basically that if you use broader criteria, the second bullet point here, you'll reduce the apparent effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of the intervention, but increase the reach.

At the same time, if you use rigorous biochemical screening tests, you'll improve the effectiveness, but you'll miss a lot of potentially eligible subjects and limit the reach or the population impact of the intervention.

The next issue is case finding, and, you know.

Unlike the case of screening for risk of type 1 diabetes, you probably don't really need to screen to identify at-risk individuals.

Clearly, this is a barrier to enrollment in diabetes prevention interventions.

But a study we did suggests that almost 80% of nondiabetic patients receiving medical care have some assessment, objective assessment, of glycemia over the past three years.

And I think really what we should be doing is mining the electronic health record, looking at direct measures of glycemia and other risk factors like BMI and history of hypertension to identify potentially eligible subjects.

Another barrier is provider referral.

Data suggest that only about 30% of U.S. primary care physicians report that their initial management approach for prediabetes is referral to a National DPP.

PCPs cite lack of motivation as a barrier, but it's pretty clear that there is low awareness of the National DPP, and increasing program availability and better referral coordination at a system level would likely enhance referrals.

The next issue is enrollment, and most targeted interventions use passive enrollment.

I think proactive outreach and incentives may improve enrollment.

These data come from a study we did with the National DPP, comparing letters from the health plan inviting people with prediabetes to participate in the DPP, in which case the uptake was about 8%.

If the patient's practice generated a portal message or letter, uptake was increased to 12%, and a phone call from a medical assistant actually led to a 15% uptake.

So it's pretty clear that proactive outreach and also probably incentives can also promote enrollment and adherence to lifestyle interventions.

Obvious things are reduce or eliminate out-of-pocket costs and provide financial incentives, but also invoking loss avoidance helps.

And the idea of loss avoidance comes from behavioral economics, and in this instance suggests that when a variety of diabetes prevention programs are offered and when failure to participate is associated with increased out-of-pocket costs, participation as a result is high and adherence at one year tends to be very good.

## Cardiovascular outcomes

So the last issue I want to touch on relates to the impact of lifestyle interventions on cardiovascular outcomes.

And the question arises: should the goal of targeted interventions be to prevent progression to type 2 diabetes or to prevent cardiovascular disease or both?

And unfortunately, in the two randomized trials that have rigorously assessed cardiovascular outcomes, there has been no impact of the interventions on cardiovascular outcomes.

The first study was the Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study, which compared cardiovascular events at 10 years follow-up among participants randomized to the lifestyle versus the placebo intervention, and the rate of fatal and nonfatal cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality was actually 4% higher in lifestyle-intervention participants.

Similar data have been generated from 21-year follow-up of the U.S. Diabetes Prevention Program, comparing lifestyle-intervention participants with placebo participants, and looking at nonfatal myocardial infarction, there was an 18% nonsignificant risk reduction, but all of the other cardiovascular outcomes were actually nonsignificantly higher in lifestyle-intervention participants compared to placebo participants.

And this is perhaps not surprising, thinking about the epidemiology.

We know that compared to people with normal glucose regulation, those with impaired glucose tolerance have an increased risk of all-cause mortality.

That's not that different than that observed among people with newly diagnosed diabetes.

So intervening to delay progression from impaired glucose tolerance to type 2 diabetes may not be sufficient to prevent cardiovascular outcomes, and achieving normal glucose regulation may be necessary to prevent cardiovascular disease.

## Conclusions

So, in conclusion, lifestyle interventions are effective in delaying or preventing type 2 diabetes, although in real-world practice the number needed to treat is large.

Criteria for National DPP eligibility must be tailored to optimize effectiveness and maximize population-level benefit.

And further research is needed to prospectively define the degree of improvement in intermediate outcomes such as glycemia, weight, blood pressure, lipids, etc., required to reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

Thank you.
