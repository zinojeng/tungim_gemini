## Historical landscape

**Christopher McCartney:** Good morning, everyone. Thank you for being here. I am going to give you a brief overview of the progression of the Endocrine Society's guideline program.

In the historical landscape, clinical guidance in the 1970s was largely based on expert consensus. In the 1980s, as many of you know, the evidence-based medicine movement began to prioritize research evidence over expert opinion and led to the development of more rigorous guideline systems.

By the 1990s, however, there were many competing systems for guideline development. In 2000, a group of experts formed the GRADE Working Group. GRADE stands for **Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation**. The group sought to establish best practices for assessing the certainty of evidence underlying recommendations and for making clinical recommendations.

In 2005, the Endocrine Society's governing Council—now the Board of Directors—approved the Society's guideline program. The first guidelines were published the following year, in 2006, and the Endocrine Society was an early adopter of GRADE.

## Institute of Medicine standards for trustworthy guidelines

One key subsequent event occurred in 2011, when the Institute of Medicine—now the National Academy of Medicine—published standards for developing trustworthy clinical practice guidelines.

I apologize for reading the excerpt, but it is important. The purpose of the publication was to enhance guideline trustworthiness. It stated that trustworthy guidelines should:

- be based on a systematic review of the existing evidence—not cherry-picking, but collecting all relevant evidence systematically;
- be developed by a knowledgeable, multidisciplinary panel of experts and representatives from key affected groups;
- consider important patient subgroups and patient preferences, as appropriate;
- be based on an explicit and transparent process that minimizes distortions, biases, and conflicts of interest;
- provide a clear explanation of the logical relationships between alternative care options and health outcomes, and provide ratings of both the quality of evidence and the strength of recommendations; and
- be reconsidered and revised when important new evidence warrants modification of recommendations.

This is what the GRADE system seeks to do. It provides a transparent, structured framework for minimizing bias and distortion. It also explicitly rates the certainty of evidence and the strength of recommendations.

## Strengthening the Endocrine Society guideline program

I will now return to the Endocrine Society. Around the time I joined the Clinical Guidelines Committee, the Society was carefully evaluating its menopause clinical practice guideline using the AGREE II instrument. AGREE II helps assess how well a guideline conforms to standards for trustworthy guideline development.

Soon afterward, Endocrine Society Strategic Plan 4 was approved. One highlighted component was strengthening the clinical practice guideline program. This led to the formation of a Clinical Practice Guidelines Task Force in 2018.

Among its recommendations, the task force called for full implementation of GRADE. The Society had used GRADE, but not to its full extent and therefore had not been able to take advantage of all its benefits.

The task force also recommended standard operating procedures for areas such as topic identification, guideline development panel selection, conflict/duality-of-interest management, guideline updating, and global relevance. Another recommendation was to have an in-house methodologist.

That recommendation was not implemented exactly as first envisioned, but it directly led to the recruitment of the four Endocrine Society Guideline Methodologists participating in this session. We are endocrinologists embedded in guideline panels to guide them through the GRADE process. In my view, this has been extremely helpful.

In 2019, the conflict/duality-of-interest policy for guideline panels was strengthened. It has been strengthened again very recently, and this is critically important.

In 2020, a strategic business plan and investment request were submitted and ultimately approved to enable full implementation of GRADE. An important aspect of the plan was partnership with the McMaster University GRADE Centre—an internationally recognized center with deep GRADE expertise—and the Mayo Clinic Evidence-Based Practice Research Program.

These partners helped the Society learn how to implement GRADE as fully as possible. Their methodologists supported three initial guidelines, and the Society's methodologists learned from them:

- *Management of Hyperglycemia in Hospitalized Adult Patients in Non-Critical Care Settings*;
- *Management of Individuals With Diabetes at High Risk for Hypoglycemia*; and
- *Treatment of Hypercalcemia of Malignancy in Adults*.

These guidelines were published in 2022 and 2023.
