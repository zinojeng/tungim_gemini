## Introduction

**Moderator:** We are going to move on to the second talk, by Dr. Daria Igudesman from AdventHealth in Orlando, Florida. The presentation title is “Weight Management in Type 1 Diabetes: Current Challenges and Novel Perspectives.”

**Daria Igudesman:** Thank you very much. I would like to thank the organizers for the kind introduction. I am going to switch gears a little. Instead of talking about the potential effects of obesity on the acceleration of type 1 diabetes, I will talk more about lifestyle approaches to managing weight in the context of established type 1 diabetes.

## Weight as a cardiometabolic risk factor in type 1 diabetes

I think many of us—perhaps all of us—are familiar with the landmark Diabetes Control and Complications Trial. It demonstrated that intensive glycemic control with three or more daily insulin injections lowered hemoglobin A1c and kept it lower over time relative to conventional insulin therapy consisting of one or two daily injections. This was associated with lower risks of retinopathy, microalbuminuria, and clinical neuropathy.

For a long time, we concluded that the MVP of cardiometabolic risk reduction in type 1 diabetes was glucose lowering. That is not wrong, but there are other players that are equally important, including weight management.

More recent publications from the DCCT and its Epidemiology of Diabetes Interventions and Complications follow-up show that people who received intensive treatment but gained excess weight over time lost the cardioprotective benefit of intensive insulin therapy. The group shown by the red line gained six BMI units during approximately 17 years of follow-up, and its cumulative cardiovascular-event risk approached that of the conventionally treated group shown in green. In contrast, those who received intensive treatment and gained minimal weight, shown by the blue line, maintained that cardioprotective benefit.

As Dr. Ahmad showed, a review by Guo and colleagues published last year highlights how large the problem of obesity is becoming, both in the United States and globally. According to U.S. electronic-health-record data from more than 200,000 young people, the most recent estimate of combined overweight and obesity in youth with type 1 diabetes is almost 50%; in adults it is approximately 70%. Across six continents, the pooled prevalence is lower because North America has the highest prevalence, but it is still approximately 30% in youth and 54% in adults. Prevalence is increasing over time on every continent except Africa.

There are substantial U.S. disparities in obesity prevalence by race and ethnicity. The absolute prevalence in youth of Hispanic ethnicity or Black race is approximately 20 percentage points higher than in youth of Asian or white race. Similar differences occur between commercial and Medicaid insurance, and racial, ethnic, and insurance-related disparities are also seen in adults with type 1 diabetes. There are age- and sex-related differences as well: middle-aged adults have the highest prevalence of obesity over time, and females tend to have a higher prevalence than males. This context is important when we design targeted weight-management interventions.

GLP-1 receptor-agonist prescribing is increasing exponentially among people with type 1 diabetes in the United States. This is true in both youth and adults, particularly among those with class 2 or class 3 obesity. Even adults without overweight or obesity are receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists with increasing frequency, partly because of factors such as insulin resistance.

As a dietitian, my charge is to impress upon you that nutrition therapy, and lifestyle therapy overall, is not less important in the era of GLP-1 receptor agonists. It may be just as important, or more important, because adverse effects such as gastrointestinal distress, nutrient deficiencies, lean-mass loss, and disordered eating can be prevented or treated by a registered dietitian. Type 1 diabetes also adds unique risks, including hypoglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis. Later I will discuss work at the Translational Research Institute that is addressing some of those problems.

## The AIM framework

The bulk of this talk focuses on three challenges in weight management for type 1 diabetes and three opportunities for the field to strengthen the evidence base. I organized them into the acronym AIM:

- **A — Add** type 1 diabetes-specific lifestyle weight-management recommendations. The evidence base is currently scant.
- **I — Integrate** cardiometabolic outcomes beyond weight. Weight is only one determinant of cardiometabolic health, and insulin resistance is also important.
- **M — Measure** diet rigorously in all weight-management and metabolism trials, including pharmacotherapy trials.

Diet measures in weight-management trials are lacking. We need to work with nutrition epidemiologists and registered dietitians to add rigorous dietary assessment to these studies.

## A: Add type 1 diabetes-specific recommendations

The ADA Standards of Care include a section titled “Treatment of Obesity in Type 1 Diabetes.” They recommend applying obesity-management strategies used in the general adult population, including GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy and metabolic surgery. Understandably, these recommendations carry evidence grades B and C because we lack the evidence needed to manage weight as well as possible in type 1 diabetes.

Recognizing these gaps, several years ago I worked with multinational collaborators, including Dr. Chantal Mathieu, on a systematic review and meta-analysis of dietary patterns for weight management in type 1 diabetes. We included studies published from January 2011 through June 2024 and identified 11 trials reported in 12 studies, with 668 youth and adults. The interventions included healthful food-based dietary patterns, moderate- or low-carbohydrate diets, Mediterranean diets, and low-fat diets, in both randomized and nonrandomized trials. Most evidence was of low or very low certainty, with only a few findings of moderate certainty.

Data sparsity allowed only a couple of meta-analyses for weight. The first involved two randomized controlled trials of Mediterranean dietary patterns in 64 adults with type 1 diabetes. There was no additional benefit of the Mediterranean intervention for body weight, in kilograms, relative to low-carbohydrate or low-fat diets. This is consistent with the broader nutrition literature: there probably is not one diet that works best for everyone. We should think about how to personalize dietary therapy rather than assume that, for example, a low-carbohydrate diet is best for every person with diabetes.

The second meta-analysis included two single-arm, nonrandomized low-carbohydrate trials in 38 adults with type 1 diabetes. These studies were designed for glycemic management rather than weight management. Nevertheless, there was a nonsignificant trend toward an average body-weight reduction of approximately 2 kilograms over three to six months. This finding also came from low-certainty evidence.

To move toward evidence-based lifestyle recommendations, we established the Advancing Care for Type 1 Diabetes and Obesity Network, or ACT1ON Consortium. It was led across several institutions by Dr. Richard Pratley at AdventHealth, Dr. David Maahs at Stanford, and Dr. Beth Mayer-Davis at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Our NIDDK-funded proposal began with developmental work intended to inform a personalized, diet-based weight-management intervention for young adults with type 1 diabetes.

First, we deeply phenotyped young adults with and without type 1 diabetes in a whole-room indirect calorimeter. Energy expenditure was approximately 100 kilocalories per day higher in those with type 1 diabetes than in matched controls. That might imply lower obesity rates in type 1 diabetes, but we know that is not the case. The finding could nevertheless inform studies that use energy-estimation equations to calculate caloric requirements.

Second, we examined the clinical anecdote that more hypoglycemia causes hyperphagia, higher carbohydrate intake, or greater body weight. In an observational dataset, 122 adolescents contributed 274 days of dietary intake from a completed randomized controlled trial. Using 24-hour dietary recalls, we compared days with and without hypoglycemia in terms of calorie and carbohydrate intake. We found no evidence that hypoglycemia led to excess dietary intake, nor that a higher incidence of hypoglycemia was associated with estimated adiposity trajectories during 18 months. These are observational data, but they help alleviate the concern that hypoglycemia is an insurmountable barrier to weight management.

With those foundational data, we completed the first randomized trial of dietary interventions focused specifically on weight management in adults with type 1 diabetes. Our purpose was not to prove that one diet was best; we wanted to characterize the range of responses so that future, fully powered trials could personalize diets and improve the likelihood of success from the outset.

This was a pilot study. Regardless of whether participants followed a hypocaloric low-carbohydrate diet, a hypocaloric low-fat Look AHEAD diet, or a Mediterranean diet without caloric restriction, average weight loss over three months was approximately 2% of body weight. About 58% of the young adults were re-randomized to an alternative intervention. This was a SMART pilot—a sequential multiple assignment randomized trial—in which participants who did not achieve sufficient weight loss at three months could be randomized to another intervention. That means the initial dietary response was suboptimal for more than half. Identifying the best initial diet for each person could save time, money, and clinical resources.

## I: Integrate cardiometabolic outcomes beyond weight

I will focus on insulin resistance. People with type 1 diabetes often have some degree of insulin resistance, particularly peripheral resistance in skeletal muscle, regardless of whether they have overweight or obesity. A recent review suggested that exogenous insulin, dietary factors, and perhaps a genetic background associated with type 2 diabetes may all contribute. Insulin resistance could also make body-weight management more difficult.

We therefore need an entire team of risk factors to understand cardiometabolic risk in type 1 diabetes. It is not only glucose and not only weight. Other factors, such as insulin resistance, must be addressed to mitigate diabetes-related complications.

A meta-analysis by Sun and colleagues in *Diabetes Care* showed that every one-unit increase in estimated glucose disposal rate, or eGDR, was associated with a 17% lower composite risk of cardiovascular disease. This again indicates that glycemia, weight, and insulin resistance all matter.

What interventions might help? Lower-carbohydrate diets are an immediate option that many clinicians consider. A meta-analysis by Zeng and colleagues included 13 studies with 312 participants. Carbohydrate-restricted diets providing 45% or less of total energy from carbohydrate reduced total daily insulin dose by a mean of 5.63 units per day relative to comparator diets or usual care. The certainty of evidence was low. These diets did not change BMI on average, suggesting that reducing carbohydrate intake might lower insulin requirements—and perhaps peripheral hyperinsulinemia—even without weight loss.

Supplemental data from our dietary-pattern review also describe insulin-dose changes. A healthful dietary pattern did not reduce insulin dose relative to carbohydrate counting or usual care. Findings from low-carbohydrate diets were mixed; only one study showed an approximately 10-unit-per-day reduction compared with a high-carbohydrate diet, primarily through less bolus insulin. Mediterranean-diet studies were mixed as well: one showed an approximately 5-unit-per-day increase in total daily insulin dose, and the other showed no reduction. There is more work to do, and the response may need to be personalized.

## M: Measure diet rigorously

The final part of AIM is a call to measure diet in every weight-management intervention, including trials of adjunctive pharmacotherapy. I will give two examples from the Translational Research Institute.

The first is a recently funded U01 study using continuous ketone monitoring to mitigate DKA risk during SGLT2 inhibitor therapy in people with type 1 diabetes. These drugs have been associated with increased DKA risk but remain under investigation because of their cardiorenal benefits. In phase 1, we will examine ketone responses to insulin withdrawal before and after SGLT2 inhibitor initiation. We will collect 24-hour dietary recalls at those time points to understand not only how carbohydrate intake and meal frequency affect DKA risk, but also how other dietary factors contribute.

We will do the same in phase 2. During six months of a generalized risk-mitigation strategy and six months of a personalized risk-mitigation strategy, we will collect diet data regularly and examine the contribution of diet to clinically significant ketosis during SGLT2 inhibitor therapy.

The second example is Dr. Kaitlin Love's K23 project. She is investigating the effects of a GLP-1 receptor agonist versus placebo on vascular endpoints over 14 weeks in 32 adults with type 1 diabetes, aged 18 to 40 years, with BMI from 19 to 31.9 kg/m². Dr. Love is allowing me to add 24-hour dietary recalls at baseline and follow-up. We need quantitative evidence to examine the clinical perception that diet quality can deteriorate when caloric intake falls during adjunctive pharmacotherapy, particularly in someone with poor diet quality at baseline. We can also investigate whether diet modifies the effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists on vascular endpoints.

My final request is to consider the rigor of dietary-assessment methods. Nutrition epidemiologists and registered dietitians can help account for the errors inherent in different instruments and anticipate emerging technologies.

There are only four recovery biomarkers for quantitative dietary intake: doubly labeled water for total energy intake, nitrogen balance for protein intake, and urinary sodium and potassium for their respective intakes. These expensive measures can be collected in a study subgroup. Less costly 24-hour recall data can then be collected from the remaining participants and calibrated against the recovery-biomarker measurements.

Many studies report carbohydrate intake estimates from insulin-pump downloads. Those estimates are substantially biased downward—even more than 24-hour dietary recalls. I encourage investigators to consider adding recalls when the participant burden is acceptable. AI-enabled food photography is promising but is not yet ready for fully quantitative use. Advances are also coming in metabolomic biomarkers, food-DNA detection, and stool-based chloroplast markers.

In summary, we should add dietary interventions to strengthen the evidence base for weight management in type 1 diabetes; integrate glucose, weight, and other cardiometabolic risk factors; and measure diet in all trials of weight management and metabolism in type 1 diabetes.

I would like to thank the principal investigators of all the studies I presented, our NIH grant support, and our study participants. I will be happy to take questions.

## Questions and discussion

**Moderator:** Thank you, Daria. That was lovely. You demonstrated increased energy expenditure and an unexpected carbohydrate relationship. It is useful to measure these factors more closely, and it is interesting to consider how insulin sensitivity might be integrated into the diet you prescribe. But I am feeling a little depressed: you measured the usual players. How is insulin sensitivity going to help us?

**Daria Igudesman:** Many people are thinking about peripheral hyperinsulinemia in type 1 diabetes. Increasing insulin requirements may result not only from excess weight gain but also from dietary factors and other lifestyle factors, such as low physical activity. What can we do to reduce insulin requirements and thus peripheral hyperinsulinemia?

Possibilities include lower-carbohydrate eating—even if it is not truly low-carbohydrate or ketogenic—and aerobic exercise, which may improve skeletal-muscle insulin sensitivity and reduce insulin requirements independently of weight loss. If insulin requirements fall, that might relieve the brake on lipolysis and fatty-acid metabolism. Those would be my thoughts. I hope that helps.

**Gerry Greenfield, Sydney, Australia:** I enjoyed your talk. Is one of the major issues that we do not deliver insulin into the portal vein and therefore produce marked peripheral hyperinsulinemia, which may contribute to insulin resistance, perhaps through weight gain? When patients start GLP-1-based therapy, we often see a marked and rapid reduction in insulin dose. Is that improvement a direct effect on insulin resistance, a reduction in carbohydrate or protein intake, or both? What are your thoughts?

**Daria Igudesman:** I generally think of weight loss after GLP-1 therapy as leading to the reduction in insulin dose. In terms of carbohydrate, I think it is a quality issue: not all carbohydrates are created equal. It is not only carbohydrate quality, either; fat quality matters because saturated fat can promote insulin resistance. We need a global perspective. Ultimately, weight loss during GLP-1 therapy is probably the predominant driver of lower insulin requirements.

**Gerry Greenfield:** Interestingly, insulin doses often fall even before weight loss occurs. That is fascinating. I wonder whether the mechanism is dietary intake, a direct effect on insulin resistance, or something else.

**Daria Igudesman:** I have heard speculation that it may be due to reduced caloric intake, which of course precedes weight loss. There may also be molecular mechanisms we do not yet understand and should study. Thank you for the question.

**Audience member from Hawaii:** I have had type 2 diabetes for 25 years, and I gain weight if I eat more than two meals a day. It comes down to caloric intake, activity, and the fact that injected insulin is not delivered normally into the portal vein. The way to control weight is to reduce calories, whether through medication or food.

**Daria Igudesman:** I agree. Part of personalization will be identifying predictors of who can adhere to a particular diet. That is an excellent point.

**Audience member:** In insulin-resistant people with type 1 diabetes, restricting carbohydrate can reduce insulin requirements, promote weight loss, and improve diabetes control. But if they are also receiving an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist, they must be monitored much more closely for DKA. I tell such patients to monitor ketones very carefully. They may not be very symptomatic because their glucose is normal and therefore may not pay attention to it.

**Daria Igudesman:** I completely agree. Perhaps continuous ketone monitors will prove useful in weight management as well.

**Marina Basina, Stanford:** Have you studied satiety and dietary modification in people with type 1 diabetes versus people without type 1 diabetes, particularly given the loss of amylin and reduced satiety? I often hear people with type 1 diabetes say that it is difficult to feel satiated and that they remain hungry after a meal. How would you incorporate that into a diet when it is simply harder for them to stop eating and feel full?

**Daria Igudesman:** There is a great deal we do not understand about appetite in type 1 diabetes. Subjective appetite is partly driven by appetite hormones, but reward mechanisms also matter, and the appetite and reward regions of the brain interact.

It is a great question, and I am sorry to say that we do not know enough. Anecdotally, people with type 1 diabetes do report impaired satiety signals, but as far as I understand, that evidence remains anecdotal. It would be valuable to study mechanistically how different diets affect satiety pathways in people with type 1 diabetes compared with people with type 2 diabetes, for example.

**Moderator:** Excellent.
