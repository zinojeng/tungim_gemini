Anne M. Dorrance, PhD: Well, I would like to start by thanking Elise and June for inviting me to share some of the work that we have been doing over several, several years. My lab is really interested in the effects of mineralocorticoid receptor activation in cerebrovascular disease and in the potential that it leads to the development of dementia. Sadly, I have absolutely nothing to disclose.

I am going to start with my take-home message, which is that mineralocorticoid receptor activation contributes to the development of cerebrovascular insufficiency and dementia in models of hypertension.

Before I describe why we think that is the case, I want to introduce some of the concepts around dementia development. Dementia is really an umbrella term that covers all forms of cognitive dysfunction that lead to impairments in memory and an inability to live a normal daily life. Recently, there has been a lot of interest in the idea that cerebrovascular insufficiency leads to cognitive impairment, and that is referred to as vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia, or VCID. That is a stand-alone condition in some cases, such that it leads to between 20% and 30% of dementia cases. But what is really important is that this vascular insufficiency also exacerbates other forms of dementia. When we look at patients with Alzheimer disease, we find that 50% of them have cerebrovascular disease when we examine their brains postmortem.

Most of that cerebrovascular disease presents as a condition called cerebral small vessel disease. That precedes the development of other symptoms in patients, particularly in patients with modifiable risk factors. When we think about vascular causes of dementia, there are acute cases, such as patients who have had large-vessel strokes or traumatic brain injury. But my lab is much more interested in the chronic conditions that lead to cerebral small vessel disease. There are many risk factors, but we are going to focus specifically on hypertension today.

Cerebral small vessel disease presents as an impairment in blood–brain barrier function and also as an increase in myogenic tone generation in the cerebrovasculature. Myogenic tone is the ability of blood vessels to contract in response to increasing intraluminal pressure. That, combined with impaired endothelium-dependent dilation, leads to an inability of the brain to regulate blood flow and, in particular, an inability to regulate what we call neurovascular coupling, which is the ability of the brain to connect neuronal activity to blood flow.

There is a great deal of evidence that hypertension is a risk factor for VCID. This is my favorite study because it considers hypertension not just in terms of the elevation in blood pressure, but also in terms of how long you have been hypertensive. It is very clear that the global risk of developing dementia is positively correlated with increases in blood pressure.

The question then is: why do we not just lower blood pressure? Would that not fix everything? We have tried that, and it did not really work. The SPRINT MIND trial shows that very aggressive blood-pressure lowering does not significantly reduce the incidence of probable dementia and causes only small reductions in the incidence of mild cognitive impairment. And the fact is that, while that might work, most patients with hypertension do not have their blood pressure completely under control.

That caused our lab to think about other things that could affect the cerebral vasculature. The mineralocorticoid receptor has been proposed as a possible target for improving cognitive function for many years. Way back in 2011, a study showed that when we look at cognitive test scores and plasma aldosterone levels, there is a direct inverse relationship. The higher the aldosterone levels, the lower the cognitive test scores and therefore the greater the risk of dementia. When patients who have high aldosterone are placed on a mineralocorticoid receptor blocker, either eplerenone or spironolactone, cognitive function can improve after just six months of treatment.

More recently, an excellent study examined differences between patients with primary aldosteronism and essential hypertension. These patients were blood-pressure matched, so it took blood pressure out of the equation. When we look at the MoCA score—the Montreal Cognitive Assessment score, which is a very good measure in patients who have vascular dysfunction—we can see that the MoCA score is much lower in patients with primary aldosteronism than in patients with essential hypertension. When we consider the number of patients with a pathological MoCA score, defined as anything under 26, almost half of the patients in the primary aldosteronism group had a pathological score, whereas only three patients in the essential-hypertension group had a score like that.

How can we think about MR activation in the cerebrovasculature and the ways in which it controls blood flow? Blood flow within the brain is coordinated by a group of cells that we refer to as the neurovascular unit. These include the blood vessels, with their endothelial and smooth-muscle cells, but also the cells that surround the blood vessels: astrocytes, neurons, pericytes, and macrophages. All of these cell types express the MR, so they could all be involved in the regulation of cerebral blood flow and cognitive function.

I want to note that 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2, or 11β-HSD2, is present in smooth-muscle and endothelial cells. That means that in these cells the MR is being activated by aldosterone and not cortisol.

There is a great deal of evidence from the peripheral vasculature showing that the MR has detrimental effects on the vasculature. These effects span a variety of conditions, but those that are also important in the cerebral vasculature include increased myogenic tone, smooth-muscle remodeling, and increased vascular stiffness. In fact, my own lab showed many years ago that, if we treat a hypertensive rat with spironolactone for six weeks as its blood pressure is increasing, we can correct the arterial remodeling that we normally see in the brain. To do this, we looked at the classical marker of arterial remodeling, the wall-to-lumen ratio. In the hypertensive rats the wall-to-lumen ratio is very high, but in hypertensive rats treated with an MR blocker we could reduce that arterial remodeling.

That set us off with a plan to test whether MR antagonism can improve cognitive function in a rodent model of pre-existing hypertension and whether that effect can be associated with improved endothelium-dependent dilation. We took male stroke-prone spontaneously hypertensive rats, or SHRSPs. These rats are a model of human essential hypertension, and they also exhibit signs of dementia and cerebral small vessel disease. We treated them with eplerenone for one month, beginning at 20 to 22 weeks of age, and compared them with male Sprague–Dawley rats. I want to point out that the SHRSPs have elevated aldosterone levels compared with Sprague–Dawley rats.

The first thing we always do when we consider a treatment in an SHRSP is measure blood pressure. I want you to focus on the latter two bars here: the SHRSP and the SHRSP treated with eplerenone. You can see that, in this case, eplerenone does not lower blood pressure. We have shown that time and again, as have others.

Similarly, eplerenone had no effect on cerebral blood flow in these animals. We measure cerebral blood flow by focusing on the blood vessels in the pial arteries on the surface of the brain. We can see that, while blood flow is lower in the hypertensive rats, it cannot be improved by eplerenone treatment.

But blood flow on the surface of the brain does not provide a good correlate of what is happening in the cerebral microcirculation. To study that, we examine the cerebral parenchymal arterioles. These are the small blood vessels that dive off the pial arteries into the brain parenchyma to perfuse the cerebral microcirculation. These vessels are unique: they have very few branches, very little collateral blood flow, and very high levels of myogenic tone. In fact, they are classified as a bottleneck in the perfusion of the cerebral microcirculation because they can limit blood flow to the capillaries.

The first thing we want to do is examine these blood vessels' ability to generate tone. We mount the vessels on glass cannulas and pressurize them. This is a vessel under passive conditions, and this is a vessel after it has been pressurized to physiological pressures. You can see that the lumen diameter is reduced because the blood vessel has generated tone. We and many others have shown that this ability to generate tone is increased in hypertensive models, and eplerenone treatment does not correct that either.

What eplerenone does do is improve endothelium-dependent dilation. To assess endothelium-dependent dilation, we use carbachol, which is a broad-spectrum vasodilator. As we would have predicted, the control SHRSPs have very little endothelium-dependent dilation, whereas the SHRSPs treated with eplerenone become much more like blood vessels from normotensive rats, with markedly improved vasodilation.

Carbachol activates several vasodilator pathways. My lab has previously shown that the pathways most people think of as the typical pathways are not important in these parenchymal arterioles. We blocked both the nitric oxide synthase pathway and the cyclooxygenase pathway using L-NAME and indomethacin and showed that these pathways do not really affect dilation in the parenchymal arterioles. The important pathway is the EDH/EDHF pathway—the pathway driven by hyperpolarization.

In my lab, we think of this as a pathway activated by the transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 channel, or TRPV4. When TRPV4 is activated, it leads to calcium entry into the endothelial cells. That activates the small- and intermediate-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels, leading to hyperpolarization of the endothelium and the smooth-muscle cells.

We can activate TRPV4 with a compound that we call GSK101. You can see that GSK101-mediated dilation is essentially absent in a hypertensive rat. But when we treat that hypertensive rat with a mineralocorticoid receptor blocker, we can dramatically improve the vasodilation and bring it back to the same level seen in a normotensive animal.

This is when people say to me, “Anne, how can one tiny ion channel have an effect on cognitive function?” I am going to take you on a little side quest to point out that this channel is really vital. We used endothelial cell-specific TRPV4 knockout mice and measured their cognitive function using the novel-arm setup of a Y-maze. In this test, we allow a mouse to explore arms A and B for five minutes. We then give it time to rest and consolidate its memories before bringing it back and opening arm C. A normal mouse will spend more time in arm C than in the other two arms because mice really like novel environments. A mouse with cognitive dysfunction will explore all three arms equally because it will not remember arms A and B.

That is exactly what we see in the TRPV4 knockout mice: they have impaired spatial memory. They also have impaired behaviors of daily living. A normal mouse will build a nest—even male mice build nests—and we can score those nests according to how well the mouse would be kept warm and hidden from predators. The TRPV4 knockout mice do not build nests that are nearly as good as those of the control mice. This suggests that the ion channel we have identified as being regulated by the MR is potentially very important for cognitive function.

I am going to take us back to the MR now to show that cognitive function is also improved in our animals treated with eplerenone. Again, we use the same test as we used in the mice. The hypertensive rats not treated with eplerenone spend very little time in the novel arm and do not enter it as frequently, whereas rats treated with the MR blocker eplerenone have dramatically improved cognitive function, although it does not return completely to the level of a normal rat. It is important to note that these rats already had cognitive impairment when we began eplerenone treatment. We are reversing a cognitive impairment, not preventing it.

We also looked at several other markers that are clinically important in vascular cognitive impairment: activation of microglia and activation of astrocytes. Those two processes lead to increased inflammation and oxidative stress and, subsequently, to neurodegeneration.

When we examine microglia, we stain them for the protein Iba1. We can count the number of microglia and see that they are elevated in the brains of hypertensive rats. Eplerenone treatment does not correct that; it does not statistically reduce the number of microglia. What it does do is improve microglial activation. When microglia are activated, they retract their processes and their soma size increases. We can therefore use soma size as a marker of activation. It is elevated in the hypertensive rats, and blocking the mineralocorticoid receptor reduces that activation. Thus, there might be a reduction in neuroinflammation.

We performed similar studies of astrocytes. Again, the number of astrocytes is elevated in hypertensive rats compared with controls. When we treat the animals with eplerenone, we can reduce the number of astrocytes and bring it back to the same level seen in a normotensive rat. We can also examine astrocyte volume as a marker of reactivity. Unlike microglia, which retract their processes, activated astrocytes extend more processes and become more ramified. We can therefore use astrocyte volume as a marker of activation status. The astrocytes are indeed activated in the SHRSPs, but that can be corrected by eplerenone treatment, again suggesting that eplerenone reduces neuroinflammation in these animals.

I showed you earlier that the mineralocorticoid receptor is expressed throughout the vasculature in the brain, as well as in neurons and astrocytes. The question then became: which mineralocorticoid receptor is important? To assess that, we used endothelial cell-specific mineralocorticoid receptor knockout mice, which we obtained from Iris Jaffe at Tufts. We had MR-intact and endothelial cell-specific MR knockout mice. We treated a second group with angiotensin II to make them hypertensive and induce small vessel disease. Thus, we had MR-intact mice treated with angiotensin II and MR knockout mice treated with angiotensin II.

Just as we did in the rats, we first looked at blood pressure. Angiotensin II treatment elevates blood pressure in MR-intact mice, and it also elevates blood pressure in endothelial cell-specific MR knockout mice. This suggests that the absence of MR in the endothelium does not affect the ability of angiotensin II to elevate blood pressure.

We also looked again at perfusion in the pial arteries. As predicted, in hypertensive mice with an intact mineralocorticoid receptor, perfusion in the pial arteries is reduced. That is partially, but not completely, corrected when we knock the MR out only in the endothelium.

What happens in the parenchymal arterioles that dive into the brain? The angiotensin II-treated mice have increased levels of myogenic tone in both the MR-intact and MR knockout groups. But the MR-intact mice have markedly impaired endothelium-dependent dilation to carbachol, such that they have essentially no, or very little, vasodilation. By knocking the MR specifically out of the endothelium, we can improve that vasodilator capacity even though the mice are still hypertensive.

We see the same thing when we consider TRPV4-mediated dilation. Again, we activate TRPV4 with GSK101. There is very little GSK101-mediated dilation in the MR-intact hypertensive mice, but it is almost normalized in the MR knockout mice treated with angiotensin II. There is, however, a small shift in the sensitivity of the blood vessels to GSK101.

The last measure we made in the mice was of the mechanism of neurovascular coupling. Within the brain, blood flow is tightly regulated, and the brain has no capacity to store energy. That tight regulation is therefore very important in the development of dementia and neurodegeneration.

We can measure how good that regulation is by tickling a mouse's whiskers. When we tickle the whiskers, we activate neurons in the whisker-barrel cortex, and we can measure blood flow in that cortex using laser speckle contrast imaging. Here we can see blood flow within the brain, and the whisker-barrel cortex is the small region that lights up when we activate neuronal activity.

This is a tracing of what we see. This is baseline blood flow. When we tickle the mouse's whiskers, we see an increase in blood flow in response to that stimulation. As soon as we stop tickling the whiskers, blood flow decreases again. We can quantify that as the increase in blood flow from baseline.

We and many others before us have shown that angiotensin II-hypertensive mice have an impaired neurovascular-coupling response. You can see that here: there is very little increase in blood flow in response to neuronal activation. That is completely corrected when we knock the mineralocorticoid receptor out of the cerebral endothelium. This suggests that, at least in hypertensive mice, the MR in the endothelium is very important in regulating pathways that control neurovascular coupling.

In our genetic model of hypertension, the stroke-prone spontaneously hypertensive rat, MR antagonism improves vasodilation, spatial-recognition memory, neuroinflammation, and astrogliosis.
