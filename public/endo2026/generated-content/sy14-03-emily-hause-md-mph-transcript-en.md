### FOP and missing patients

But the disease I'd like to focus on today is fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva, or FOP.

It's a rare genetic condition.

It turns connective tissues irreversibly into bone, forming a second skeleton and rendering the person unable to move.

It typically presents in childhood.

Early signs and symptoms include the formation of exquisitely painful lumps, typically in the paraspinal region.

They get biopsied or misdiagnosed as cancerous early on.

These lumps eventually ossify and become less painful, but leave decreased range of motion in joints.

It progresses relentlessly through the body, leaving patients with severely restricted mobility.

Flares of the disease can be triggered by injury or tissue damage, but also can happen without clear provocation.

Over time, the disease flare-ups lead to stiffening and drastically impaired function of the joints, including the TMJs and rib cage, making it difficult for patients to breathe and eat.

Patients face a lifetime of challenges with mobility, activities of daily living, and in some areas of the world, significant stigma.

There is currently no known cure for FOP.

It is caused by one single gene variant in the ACVR1 gene.

This activating mutation turns all normal tissue repair function into bone formation.

Researchers have developed a very good mouse model for FOP, and research has been ongoing in search of a cure.

FOP is actually easy to diagnose and identify, as the majority of patients present with a characteristic great toe malformation at birth.

They are typically short, malformed, or angled abnormally.

This is a highly conserved feature of the disease, and combined with the characteristic bony lumps is virtually pathognomonic.

Fun fact, this is actually something that was tested on my Peds board exam, but none of my co-fellows or anybody I had talked to had actually ever seen an FOP patient.

After working with Dr. Scott in South Africa and meeting three FOP patients, I can confidently say, as he often does, that once you know the disease, you can diagnose it from across the room.

You don't need a fancy genetic test to recognize patients with FOP.

However, the story of FOP is the story of the missing.

The rate of disease is approximately one in a million people, so these patients are one in a million.

There are approximately 8 billion people in the world and only about 800 diagnosed cases of FOP.
