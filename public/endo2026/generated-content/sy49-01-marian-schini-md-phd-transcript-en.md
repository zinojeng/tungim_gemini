## Denosumab in osteoporosis

Denosumab is an anti-osteoporosis medication. It is a fully human monoclonal antibody to receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand, or RANKL. It inhibits osteoclasts and decreases bone resorption.

The pivotal FREEDOM trial randomized postmenopausal women to denosumab or placebo for three years. Serum CTX, a bone-resorption marker, fell rapidly after denosumab began—by 86% at month 1 and 72% at month 36. Because bone formation and resorption are coupled, the bone-formation marker P1NP fell more gradually, by 18% at month 1 and 76% at month 36.

FREEDOM was extended to a total of 10 years. Participants originally assigned to denosumab continued it for 10 years, while those originally assigned to placebo crossed over to denosumab for seven years. In the 10-year group, BMD increased by 21.7% at the lumbar spine and 9.2% at the total hip. Substantial gains also occurred in the seven-year crossover group. Compared with placebo, denosumab reduced vertebral fractures by 68%, hip fractures by 39%, and nonvertebral fractures by 19%.

## Reversibility after discontinuation: loss of BMD and overshoot in turnover

Bisphosphonates accumulate in the skeleton, so benefit can persist for a period after they are stopped. Denosumab is different. In a study in which women received denosumab for two years and were followed for another two years, BMD increased during treatment and then fell after discontinuation, reaching below baseline particularly at the total hip and one-third radius.

The term *rebound* is often used for bone-turnover markers, but rebound means to bounce back or recover. Google may associate the term with basketball or relationships, but neither describes this biology. The more accurate term is *overshoot*: going beyond the intended stopping or turning point.

In the same discontinuation study, CTX and P1NP fell during treatment. If there were simply a rebound after stopping, the markers would return to baseline. Instead, they rose above baseline: mean CTX increased 63% and P1NP 47% above baseline. This is an overshoot, not merely a rebound.

Early in clinical use, patients often received nothing after denosumab stopped. Case reports then described vertebral fractures and multiple vertebral fractures, raising an alert.

## Vertebral-fracture risk after discontinuation

Investigators performed a post hoc analysis of FREEDOM and its extension. Participants had received at least two doses of denosumab or placebo, discontinued treatment, and had at least seven months of follow-up after the last dose. The analysis included 1,001 participants who stopped denosumab and 470 who stopped placebo.

The rate of vertebral fracture after denosumab discontinuation was 7.1 per 100 participant-years, similar to the 8.5 per 100 participant-years after placebo discontinuation. In that sense, overall vertebral-fracture risk returned to its pretreatment level. Multiple vertebral fractures were different: among participants with an off-treatment vertebral fracture, 61% of those who had stopped denosumab had multiple fractures, compared with 39% after placebo. The annualized multiple-vertebral-fracture rate was also higher after denosumab discontinuation, 4.2 versus 3.2 per 100 participant-years.

This first analysis had limitations. More than 97% of spine radiographs in the original trial were obtained at scheduled visits, but only a minority of extension participants had off-treatment radiographs. Women without radiographs tended to be older and to have lower T-scores, so fractures may have been missed.

An additional post hoc analysis therefore included only women with imaging after discontinuation: 475 who had stopped denosumab and 227 who had stopped placebo. The sample was smaller, but every participant had imaging, and the measured vertebral-fracture rates were higher than in the earlier analysis.

How common are vertebral and multiple vertebral fractures after discontinuation? The answer is difficult because studies have different follow-up durations and many participants receive bisphosphonates. In the additional FREEDOM analysis, median follow-up was 11 months; 25.5% received subsequent treatment, of whom 80.6% received a bisphosphonate. The annualized rates per 100 participant-years were 8.7 for vertebral fracture and 5.1 for multiple vertebral fractures.

A retrospective study of 797 Swiss women had 27.5 months of follow-up, and 63% received a bisphosphonate afterward. There was no standardized imaging protocol. Vertebral fractures occurred in 10.3% of patients, an incidence of 4.1% per year. The average number of vertebral fractures per affected patient was three to five, with a range of one to 11. In broad terms, perhaps one person in 10 will have a fracture after stopping.

There is a critical window. Fractures have been reported from 7 to 30 months after the last injection, with peak risk at approximately 9 to 15 months. Even a delay of weeks beyond the date when the next dose is due may matter.

The anatomical distribution does not appear unique. Before denosumab, fractures were most common at T12, L1, L3, T11, and L2. The distribution of fractures after discontinuation was similar.

## Evidence limitations for risk-factor analyses

The main part of my talk considers risk factors for greater BMD loss after discontinuation, risk factors for multiple vertebral fractures, and whether the two outcomes are linked. Most evidence comes from retrospective observational studies, and many participants received bisphosphonates afterward. Definite conclusions are therefore difficult. I have focused on larger studies rather than small case series.

## Baseline BMD and BMD gain during treatment

Data on baseline BMD are unclear. Two studies assessed whether BMD before treatment predicts loss after discontinuation; only one suggested that lower baseline lumbar-spine BMD might predict greater loss at the lumbar spine.

BMD gain during treatment is more consistently associated with subsequent loss: the more a patient gains, the more the patient tends to lose after discontinuation.

For vertebral fractures, baseline BMD and the change in BMD during treatment do not appear to have an effect. A higher total-hip BMD level during treatment may be protective.

## Bisphosphonate use before denosumab

Many patients have received bisphosphonates before denosumab. This is difficult to evaluate because studies differ in dose, duration, and washout period.

For BMD loss, smaller studies found no effect, while a more recent study suggested possible protection at the lumbar spine only. For vertebral fractures, smaller studies and the initial FREEDOM analysis found no effect. In FREEDOM, however, prior bisphosphonate exposure was no longer than three cumulative years and was followed by a one-year washout. The Swiss study suggested a protective association, with an odds ratio for multiple vertebral fractures of 0.1 and a p value below 0.0001—an observation that is difficult to ignore, though it is observational.

There are two possible explanations. Prior bisphosphonate use is associated with lower CTX at denosumab discontinuation, which may be protective. It is also associated with smaller BMD gains during denosumab treatment, leaving less gained BMD to lose afterward.

## Vertebral fractures before treatment

A history of vertebral fracture before denosumab does not appear to affect post-treatment BMD loss, but it clearly increases the risk of vertebral fractures after discontinuation. Across studies, odds ratios for multiple vertebral fractures range from approximately 3.1 to 5.3. In the FREEDOM analyses, rates were substantially higher in participants with prevalent vertebral fractures.

## Duration of denosumab treatment

Studies use different treatment-duration thresholds, most commonly 2.5 to 3 years. Longer exposure is associated with greater BMD loss. Women treated for 10 years tended to lose more BMD after discontinuation than women treated for seven years at the lumbar spine, total hip, and femoral neck.

Two earlier studies did not identify a relationship between duration and vertebral fractures, but each had important limitations. The first FREEDOM analysis treated women without radiographs as fracture-free, even though approximately one-third of vertebral fractures are asymptomatic. The 797-person Swiss study lacked a standardized imaging protocol, included women receiving aromatase inhibitors, and included many who received bisphosphonates afterward.

The additional FREEDOM analysis included only women with radiographs and used a three-year threshold because the original trial lasted three years. After short-term denosumab treatment of three years or less, multiple-vertebral-fracture rates were similar to placebo. After more than three years, the rate was more than twice as high, with an odds ratio of 3.0 compared with shorter treatment. Almost all participants who sustained four or more vertebral fractures had received long-term denosumab. The two placebo participants with four or more fractures were also receiving glucocorticoids.

## Age, bone-turnover markers, BMI, sex, and glucocorticoids

One might expect older age to predict accelerated BMD loss, but the literature instead associates younger age with greater loss. Data from Dr. Bente Langdahl's group showed greater loss in younger participants at the total hip and femoral neck; the lumbar-spine result was not significant.

Age does not appear to modify vertebral-fracture risk consistently. In the Spanish BIFAP study, older patients appeared to have a higher point estimate, but the interaction p value was not significant.

Most studies of baseline bone-turnover markers evaluated CTX. One found that higher baseline CTX predicted greater lumbar-spine BMD loss, but baseline CTX did not predict vertebral fracture.

BMI data are also unclear. One study suggested that lower BMI might predict lumbar-spine loss only; studies did not identify an effect on vertebral fractures.

Men remain underrepresented in osteoporosis studies. The BIFAP analysis included only small numbers of men and did not show a statistically different effect on multiple-vertebral-fracture risk. Glucocorticoid data are similarly limited; one study did not identify an effect on BMD loss.

## Practical risk summary and the link between BMD loss and fractures

The most important factors to remember are these. Be cautious when stopping denosumab in younger patients because accelerated BMD loss may be greater. Be especially cautious in patients with prior vertebral fractures because their risk of further fractures is higher. Patients who gain more BMD, particularly at the total hip, tend to lose more after discontinuation. Longer treatment is associated with greater BMD loss and, in the better-imaged analysis, a higher risk of multiple vertebral fractures.

Are BMD loss and vertebral fractures directly linked? The Swiss study found no association in its multivariable analysis. In the FREEDOM and FREEDOM Extension post hoc analysis, participants with multiple vertebral fractures had significantly greater annualized BMD loss than those without a vertebral fracture. The additional analysis found that each 1% increment in annualized total-hip BMD loss predicted multiple vertebral fractures, with an odds ratio of 1.19 and 95% confidence interval of 1.08 to 1.32. These associations are weak, however, and bone quality probably also has a role.

## Considerations when starting denosumab

Before starting, consider who would be at high risk of multiple vertebral fractures and accelerated BMD loss if treatment were later discontinued. Younger patients with higher bone-turnover markers may have greater accelerated loss. Treatment-naïve patients with prevalent vertebral fractures are at higher risk of further vertebral fractures.

Also consider who is likely to discontinue. Older patients are more likely to stop treatment; they may move to a nursing home or lose continuity of care. Keep patients, relatives, and clinicians informed because even short delays of weeks may be associated with fractures.

Consider who may have no easy exit strategy. In chronic kidney disease, bisphosphonates—an important protective option after denosumab—are generally unavailable when eGFR is below 35 mL/min/1.73 m². Denosumab itself also poses a risk of hypocalcaemia, which may be severe in advanced CKD.

Some patients obtain greater BMD responses: those with low baseline BMD, high bone-turnover markers, and no prior osteoporosis treatment. That is a double-edged sword because greater gain is associated with greater loss after stopping.

## Is it ever safe simply to stop?

Vertebral fractures have been reported after discontinuation following only two injections, although those patients may already have had high baseline risk.

An earlier dose-ranging study administered a single injection of denosumab to postmenopausal women. The group most similar to clinical use received 1.0 mg/kg. Urine NTX remained suppressed for a time after treatment and then began to rise, but at nine months it had not reached baseline and showed no overshoot. This study suggests that one injection may be less problematic, but the evidence is limited.

## Why not treat indefinitely?

BMD continues to rise with longer treatment, so why not continue forever? Until recently, clinical-trial data extended only to 10 years. Two rare adverse effects require consideration.

Osteonecrosis of the jaw, or ONJ, often follows a dental extraction in which the gum fails to heal. Thirteen cases occurred in the FREEDOM Extension, a rate of 5.2 per 10,000 participant-years. ONJ also occurs with bisphosphonates. Observational comparisons suggest a higher risk with denosumab, although previous bisphosphonate exposure may add risk.

Atypical femoral fracture, or AFF, is another rare antiresorptive adverse effect. Two cases occurred in the FREEDOM Extension, during years 3 and 7 of denosumab, equivalent to 0.8 per 10,000 participant-years. Evidence is more limited than for bisphosphonates. If ONJ or AFF occurs, continued antiresorptive treatment may not be possible, leaving the difficult question of what to do next.

A 2026 report described 54 women treated beyond 10 years. Only 11 continued denosumab; their BMD remained stable beyond year 10. No ONJ or AFF was observed, but the numbers were small.

## Does denosumab stop reducing fractures as BMD rises?

Serge Ferrari and colleagues examined the relationship between total-hip BMD and nonvertebral fractures in FREEDOM. The relationship begins to plateau between total-hip T-scores of approximately −2.0 and −1.5, independent of age and prior nonvertebral fracture. Increasing a low T-score by one unit produced significant further reductions in nonvertebral-fracture risk, but the same increment from a higher starting T-score did not.

The curve for vertebral fractures did not show a clear plateau, suggesting that vertebral-fracture benefit may continue as total-hip BMD rises. Fractures after discontinuation are not limited to the spine; hip and other nonvertebral fractures have also been reported, although the literature is much smaller.

## Conclusion

Stop and think before starting denosumab.

This reminds me of an Aesop fable. Two frogs walking through a desert were very thirsty and found a well full of water. One frog was about to jump in, but the other held him back and asked, “If you jump in, how will you get out?” Clinical practice is similar.

Think carefully about the patients selected for denosumab. Older patients at high fracture risk may be appropriate candidates; Dr. Langdahl will discuss this further. Think about treatment risks and, before the first injection, think about an exit strategy.
