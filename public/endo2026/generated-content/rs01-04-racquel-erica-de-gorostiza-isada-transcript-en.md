## The hidden metabolic target

**Racquel Erica D. Isada:** Good morning, esteemed colleagues. I am Racquel Isada, and I will present my report, “The Hidden Target.” One in eight women will develop breast cancer in their lifetime. With the advent of targeted therapies, breast-cancer survival has improved significantly. Sometimes, however, these targeted therapies affect more than the tumor.

Let me tell you about a patient. She was a 68-year-old woman with hypertension and prediabetes who had stage IV metastatic hormone receptor–positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–negative (HR+/HER2−) breast cancer. She began the targeted AKT inhibitor capivasertib.

Within 24 hours after initiation, her blood glucose increased dramatically from 163 mg/dL to 557 mg/dL. She required aggressive intravenous insulin therapy at rates as high as 180 units/hour. Her total daily insulin requirement was approximately 1,880 units—the highest I had encountered. Because she was prone to fluid retention, we concentrated the insulin infusion and added subcutaneous insulin to reduce fluid overload.

Despite these measures, her blood glucose did not improve adequately. Capivasertib was discontinued, and glycemia abruptly normalized 58 hours after discontinuation.

## Balancing oncologic efficacy and metabolic safety

We then faced a critical question: How could we treat the cancer without harming the patient's metabolism? This is where endocrinologists step in.

Targeted therapies such as capivasertib inhibit the AKT pathway and, in combination with fulvestrant, improve progression-free survival. The same pathway is central to insulin signaling; its inhibition can therefore produce profound insulin resistance and hyperglycemia.

Management must be individualized. We worked with oncology and resumed capivasertib at half the previous dose. At the time of this presentation, the patient remained able to receive her cancer therapy with normal blood glucose and without glucose-lowering medication.

This is not only about numbers. It is about balancing oncologic efficacy with metabolic safety, and innovation with vigilance. As targeted therapies evolve, physicians must evolve as well. In precision medicine, the true target is not only the tumor—it is the whole patient.

Thank you.

## Audience Q&A

**Audience member:** Was hyperglycemia a known adverse event? Did the event itself or its severity surprise you, and was it reported to the manufacturer?

**Racquel Erica D. Isada:** Hyperglycemia was reported in the landmark CAPItello-291 trial in 16.3% of patients treated with capivasertib plus fulvestrant. This is why the trial excluded some patients with diabetes, particularly those requiring insulin therapy. We had not yet notified the manufacturer about this event, but we would consider doing so to add to the available safety information.

**Audience member:** Are there other known cases in people with prediabetes? That could signal underlying insulin resistance. Was this considered before therapy began?

**Racquel Erica D. Isada:** Yes. Because this was a known risk, the endocrinology service was involved before the drug was started so that we could mitigate a possible rise in blood glucose. We did not expect glucose to increase to this degree.

**Audience member:** What are your thoughts on combining this therapy with a ketogenic diet, which has been proposed in relation to PI3K-inhibitor effects?

**Racquel Erica D. Isada:** One metabolic complication that concerns endocrinologists is diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). In a patient following a ketogenic diet, there is concern that greater ketosis could complicate assessment or increase risk. I would therefore be cautious about recommending such a diet in this setting.

Thank you.
