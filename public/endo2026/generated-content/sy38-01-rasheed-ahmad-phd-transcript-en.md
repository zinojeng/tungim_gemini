## Diabetes, obesity, and the changing phenotype of type 1 diabetes

First, I would like to give some epidemiological data regarding the prevalence of diabetes and obesity. More than 589 million people are living with diabetes in this world, with more than 3.4 million deaths related to diabetes. Health expenditure is more than US$1 trillion per year.

Regarding the prevalence of type 1 diabetes, more than 9.2 million people are living with type 1 diabetes globally, including more than 1.8 million children and adolescents. More than 30,000 deaths among children and youth with undiagnosed type 1 diabetes were recorded.

When we look at obesity, 2.5 billion adults were living with overweight in 2022, according to WHO, and 890 million adults were living with obesity. As we know, obesity is a major risk factor for the development of type 2 diabetes.

The obesity rate is growing all over the world. If we look at type 1 diabetes, one recent publication from 2025 showed that baseline BMI is rising in type 1 diabetes. It shows that type 1 diabetes is no longer a disease only of lean people.

Here is a clearer picture of the prevalence across different age groups. Among adults with type 1 diabetes, obesity increased sharply over three decades, from 3.3% in 1985–1990 to 23.7% in 2015–2020, while overweight reached 31.4% in 2015–2020. The prevalence is also high in children and adolescents: obesity increased from 6.3% in 1990 to 12.3% in 2010, then declined slightly to 9.3% in 2020, while overweight remained stable at approximately 20%.

## Adipose-tissue and systemic meta-inflammation

When we look at obesity, it is not good for metabolic health. There is a transition from lean to obese adipose tissue. In obesity, adipocyte size grows and immune-cell infiltration increases. These immune cells change from an anti-inflammatory phenotype to an inflammatory phenotype.

Most importantly, interactions between immune cells and adipocytes trigger the production of multiple cytokines and chemokines. These mediators cause inflammation in adipose tissue, which we call meta-inflammation because it is induced by obesity.

This meta-inflammation becomes systemic inflammation and affects multiple organs. It causes inflammation in the hypothalamic region of the brain and disturbs satiety, so people eat more. It affects muscle cells, which take up less glucose and become insulin resistant. It affects the liver, causing fatty liver and more liver inflammation. It also affects the pancreatic islets and causes β-cell dysfunction and apoptosis.

When we consider the increasing prevalence of obesity in type 1 diabetes, our view has to change. The traditional view is that type 1 diabetes is caused by autoimmune destruction of β-cells, is genetically driven, and is associated with a lean phenotype. But now that obesity is increasingly prevalent in type 1 diabetes, we see that the disease is influenced by both autoimmunity and metabolic factors. The interaction among adiposity, inflammation, and immune-cell dysregulation may accelerate the progression of type 1 diabetes.

Because obesity prevalence is increasing and is harmful to metabolic health, it is important to understand the mechanisms linking adiposity with β-cell autoimmunity.

## Mechanistic links between adiposity and β-cell autoimmunity

As I mentioned, meta-inflammation may play a significant role in accelerating the onset of type 1 diabetes. It can affect the pancreatic islets, cause islet inflammation and β-cell failure, induce endoplasmic-reticulum stress, and affect insulin production by β-cells.

Obesity and overnutrition upregulate leptin. Leptin activates the mTOR pathway in T cells. Conventional T cells are then less likely to convert into regulatory T cells, or Tregs, which are very important for suppressing immune overactivation. With persistent mTOR activation, FOXP3—the major transcription factor involved in Treg development and maintenance—becomes impaired. This shows that obesity-induced mTOR activation blocks immune-tolerance programming.

Leptin also inhibits the proliferation and function of Tregs and sustains proinflammatory Th1 and Th17 responses. This imbalance shifts the immune system toward an autoimmune phenotype.

There are two very important chemokines that also play a significant role in autoimmunity: CCL2 and CXCL10. They are highly expressed by obese adipose tissue. CCL2 is also upregulated in the pancreas of people with obesity. It recruits immune cells, particularly monocytes and macrophages, which produce cytokines and chemokines and cause local inflammation.

CXCL10 is also upregulated in this setting. It attracts Th1 cells and cytotoxic CD8-positive T cells through CXCR3. These cells enhance inflammation and induce apoptosis in β-cells.

When β-cell apoptosis occurs, the cells release autoantigenic proteins, including insulin, GAD65, and IA-2. These proteins are processed and captured by antigen-presenting dendritic cells. The dendritic cells present those antigens to autoreactive T cells, amplifying the autoimmune process and causing further β-cell damage. The resulting vicious cycle further activates autoimmunity.

Another important factor is obesity-induced gut dysbiosis and its role in type 1 diabetes. People with obesity may have increased intestinal permeability—a “leaky gut.” Lipopolysaccharide, or LPS, and other bacterial components pass through the barrier. Dendritic cells encounter these antigens, become overactivated, and may enhance autoimmune disease.

Overall, what we have seen in the previous slides suggests that adiposity accelerates β-cell autoimmunity and loss.

## Accelerator hypothesis and double diabetes

When we talk about adiposity accelerating β-cell autoimmunity and loss, we come to the accelerator hypothesis proposed by Dr. Wilkin. In obesity, increased insulin resistance requires β-cells to produce more insulin. This places a great deal of stress on the β-cells, which then undergo apoptosis. Excess weight acts as an accelerator, not necessarily a cause, of type 1 diabetes.

This hypothesis has contributed to discussion of how diabetes should be defined when characteristics of type 1 and type 2 diabetes overlap. The phenotype has been called “double diabetes.” It is characterized by type 1 diabetes with insulin resistance, poor glycemic control, and features of metabolic syndrome.

Patients with double diabetes are more prone to diabetes-related complications. They are at higher risk of cardiovascular disease and microvascular complications, including diabetic retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy, and diabetic neuropathy. Many patients with double diabetes also develop fatty liver disease.

We have a major challenge in managing diabetes in people with type 1 diabetes and obesity. They have increased body fat and need more insulin to control glycemia. When they need higher doses, they gain more weight. With weight gain, they may become less physically active. Dietary challenges and fear of hypoglycemia may lead them to eat more. They may also face psychological barriers, including depression. Managing diabetes in such patients is very difficult.

## Therapeutic implications and future directions

I think we have to use therapeutic education. For people with type 1 diabetes, insulin is not enough. We have to use an integrated approach that includes lifestyle and nutritional changes, education, optimization of insulin therapy, and screening for obesity-related comorbidities because these patients are more susceptible to those diseases.

Adjunctive therapies traditionally used in type 2 diabetes—including weight-loss drugs, GLP-1 receptor agonists, dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors—may be helpful in combination approaches to improve outcomes in type 1 diabetes.

What is the future direction? When I reviewed this subject, I found a great deal of literature related to regulatory T cells. How can we maintain the number of Tregs? How can we improve their function? Tregs generally produce IL-10 and TGF-β to control overactivation of the immune system.

We should also look at epigenetics and how we can promote and sustain transcription of FOXP3. This transcription factor is very important for the conversion of conventional T cells into regulatory T cells.

We need to investigate further mechanistic links between obesity and islet autoimmunity, identify new biomarkers that predict obesity-related progression of type 1 diabetes, develop precision-medicine approaches for double diabetes, study long-term cardiovascular outcomes, and investigate novel therapies that target both immune and metabolic pathways.
