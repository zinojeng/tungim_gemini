## Clinical question

**Mohammad Jay:** My talk asks a simple question: Does parathyroidectomy prevent renal complications in primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT)? This matters because renal involvement is a guideline indication for surgery, specifically prior renal stones or low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

Parathyroidectomy is curative biochemically. It lowers calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH). But the question that is important to clinicians and patients is whether this biochemical cure translates into renal improvement. We therefore asked: After parathyroidectomy, which renal outcomes are more likely to improve, and which patients are more likely to benefit?

## Methods and renal-stone outcomes

We conducted a systematic review of studies comparing parathyroidectomy with nonsurgical intervention. The analysis presented here included 24 comparative studies and approximately 180,000 patients with PHPT. Because the studies differed substantially, we used a narrative synthesis rather than pooling a single estimate.

The clearest signal of benefit concerned incident stones, meaning new renal stones. The only randomized time-to-event analysis showed that parathyroidectomy was associated with an approximately 66% lower hazard of a new stone, although the confidence interval was wide because events were sparse. A large adjusted observational cohort also found a 23% lower hazard of an incident stone after surgery.

For recurrent stones, however, the evidence was less reassuring. Established stone formers did not show a clear early reduction in recurrence. One study did show that stone risk attenuated over time.

## Renal-function outcomes and interpretation

For renal function, a randomized trial and rigorous observational studies—including a target-trial emulation—did not show a clear benefit in reducing eGFR decline after surgery. Possible benefit was seen in selected subgroups, such as younger patients and those with elevated calcium, but not clearly in patients with low eGFR.

The take-home message is that renal benefit after parathyroidectomy is not uniform. It appears to depend on the outcome, timing, and patient. For clinical practice, this shifts the question from “Does this patient meet a renal surgical indication?” to “Which patient is most likely to benefit, and which outcome is most likely to be affected?” These findings suggest that prior renal disease alone may not identify everyone who will benefit and support a more individualized approach to surgical decision-making.

## Audience Q&A

**Audience member:** I have a naïve question: How confident are we about the mechanism by which PHPT leads to renal stones, since the organ is removed and stones still occur?

**Mohammad Jay:** Thank you. First, in terms of certainty of evidence, we graded the evidence and found low-certainty evidence for incident stones and very-low-certainty evidence for recurrent stones. Inconsistency, imprecision, and risk of bias were the main reasons.

In terms of physiology, one way to think about recurrent renal stones is that the stone-forming milieu may already have been established, and parathyroidectomy does not act immediately. As mentioned, one study showed that stone risk in patients with an established stone decreased over time. This suggests there may be a benefit, but a reduction in recurrence is not seen immediately.

**Audience member:** In terms of renal benefit, how much did eGFR change, and over what period? Secondly, was there information about the development of hypoparathyroidism among those who underwent surgery?

**Mohammad Jay:** eGFR was usually assessed as a continuous variable. The change was approximately 6 eGFR units; in the randomized controlled trial, the percentage difference was approximately 3%–4%. Median follow-up was approximately 5 years.

Complications were rarely assessed. Hypoparathyroidism was either not observed or not reported in the included studies.
