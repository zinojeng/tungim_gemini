## Obesity: social, environmental, and genetic factors

**Prof. I. Sadaf Farooqi:** Sorry, the music is slightly off-putting, especially for a Brit. Great to be here. Thank you very much for the kind introduction, and it's wonderful to be able to share with you the research that we've been doing over a number of years to understand what is effectively the commonest endocrine condition, and that is obesity. Now, we know that obesity is highly prevalent.

In the UK, certainly 25% of the population have obesity. In the US, it's much higher, and the global rates of obesity, of course, are rising exponentially. And we know that that rise in the prevalence of obesity is driven strongly by our environment. The type of food we eat, the amount of food we eat, and the fact that this food is easily and cheaply available mean that it's very easy to tip the energy balance equation and for energy intake to exceed energy expenditure.

And that really explains the reason why populations are gaining weight. And of course, this has adverse consequences on lots of health issues: type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, obesity overtaking alcohol as a major driver of chronic liver disease, and soon to overtake smoking as a major driver of cancer. Now, if we want to understand why some people develop obesity more than others, you just really need to think about the fact that within a given environment, there's huge variability in how much weight people gain. Some people develop severe obesity, and others can eat what they like and they stay slim.

We're also interested in studying those people as well. Now, what we know is that there's very strong evidence that the variability in body weight is strongly driven by our genes. And we know this from studies of identical twins whose adult body weight is about 90% concordant, even if they're separated at birth and live in different environments. And adopted children have a body weight that's very similar to their biological parents rather than the adoptive parents with whom they share the childhood environment.

So our genes play a major role in how we respond to the environment. And so we can use genetic approaches to try to dissect the mechanisms underlying that variability.

## Hypothalamic regulation and the set-point range

Now, where are those genes going to work? Well, we've known for over 100 years that the hypothalamus is key to the regulation of weight because tumours that disrupt the hypothalamus and the base of the pituitary will cause obesity, reproductive dysfunction, etc. And of course, we know that the hypothalamus's main function is to maintain homeostasis, a constant internal environment. And the hypothalamus does that by detecting both external and internal signals and then changing three main outputs.

And I'll give examples of each. So we can change our behaviour, we can change autonomic nervous system activation, and we can change hormone secretion. Now, when it comes to energy balance, in animals, there's very clear evidence that for energy homeostasis, there's a set point. So if animals are starved—their food intake is restricted—and then you release that restriction, they will eat more to get back to that set point.

Now, in humans, we probably don't have a specific set point. We probably more realistically have a set point range through which we oscillate depending upon things like external stresses and the environment. And what is likely is that our genetic background influences where your set point range lies. Some people will have a lower set point range and will often stay slim throughout their lives.

Other people will always be predisposed to obesity and oscillate through this range.

## The leptin–melanocortin pathway

Now, really, the first details as to the molecular components of this pathway that regulates our weight came from the study of mice. And you can see here the ob/ob mice, an inbred strain of mice that develop severe obesity, shown in 1994 by Jeff Friedman and colleagues to be missing a hormone called leptin. And they identified leptin and showed that the animals had no leptin. And when you give them back leptin, you completely correct their severe obesity.

And this discovery led to the unraveling of a pathway that we now know is critical for the regulation of weight in mice, but also in humans. And that pathway is centered on the hypothalamus, which receives signals from our fat, which is an active endocrine organ releasing hormones telling the brain about nutrient availability. We also get signals from our gut, both neural signals and hormonal signals telling us about satiety or fullness in relation to meals. And all of that information is integrated to maintain energy homeostasis over time.

Now, if we zoom in on the hypothalamus, in the arcuate nucleus, where there's a permeable blood-brain barrier, there are distinct populations of neurons that respond to those peripheral hormonal signals such as leptin. Now, shown in green are the AgRP neurons, whose primary function is to prevent you from starving to death. In the fasted state, those AgRP neurons are activated and they drive hunger and an increase in food intake. On the opposite side, you have POMC neurons, pro-opiomelanocortin neurons, which in the hypothalamus are cleaved to yield α-MSH, or melanocortin peptides, whose function is to tell you to stop eating after a meal.

Now, the melanocortin peptides then bind to the next set of neurons, which express the melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R), and this is a critical brake on eating. So when that receptor is activated, you stop eating, and when it's inhibited by AgRP, you eat more. So it's fascinating to have this single molecule that's integrating those signals to control your eating behavior. And if you lose MC4R, as shown in mice, those mice will keep on eating and develop severe obesity.

## Human monogenic obesity and leptin physiology

So we and others showed that this fundamental pathway is critical in human physiology through the study of children whom we found were completely missing the hormone leptin, just like those mice. In a classical endocrine experiment, we then did a clinical trial giving those children injections of recombinant leptin, completely reversing their severe obesity, as you can see on these before-and-after images. And the ability to treat these children gave us an opportunity to learn about leptin's physiological role. And what we learned is in the absence of leptin, these children were constantly hungry, always wanting to eat, and would eat any kind of food to satisfy their appetite, including English hospital food, which is obviously a real disorder of eating behavior. And what we learned from studying these children was that after four days of giving them a leptin injection, their eating behavior changed.

They started to turn away food, and now they would eat just the palatable, rewarding food and turn other food away. And this was a powerful demonstration that appetite, which until that point many people assumed was just down to voluntary control, had a strong biological basis. And we also learned from the study of these children that leptin regulates immune function. Without leptin, they get frequent infections and a high mortality with this condition.

With leptin, you can reverse that immune dysfunction. Without leptin, they fail to go through puberty. With leptin, they'll go through puberty and reach reproductive maturity. Now, the other critical control points in this pathway were POMC and MC4R.

And we and colleagues showed that mutations in those molecules will also cause hyperphagia, this increased drive to eat, and severe obesity. And indeed, mutations in MC4R are the commonest genetic form of obesity. Five percent of our cohort of children with severe obesity and 1% of all people with a BMI greater than 30 kg/m² will have an MC4R loss-of-function mutation. So these are not as rare as you might think.

Now, what we've been doing over many years, and this is just a summary of that work, is studying other children with very severe obesity in whom we've identified other single-gene disorders that are causing that phenotype. And fascinatingly, those genes, while initially they seemed quite distinct, they're converging on the same pathway. Here's a zoomed-in view of those POMC neurons that respond to leptin, and as well as having mutations in the leptin receptor, you can see there are mutations in channels that regulate the depolarization of those neurons. There are mutations in other genes which affect the signaling downstream of leptin and also in the transcription of POMC.

So the genes are working in different ways, but they're converging on the same system that regulates our appetite and body weight.

## Obesity genes and innate behavior

Now, one of the fascinating things that we learned from the study of these disorders is that these genes are affecting a wide range of behaviors. All of these genetic conditions will cause hyperphagia, an increased drive to eat, constant hunger, failure to suppress the appetite, and impaired satiety. But some of these genetic disorders were also causing other behavioral abnormalities, which seemed strange. So some of the disorders cause autism, and by “cause,” I really do mean cause.

We can show in patients, but also in mouse models of the human mutations, you can cause these disrupted behaviors. Some of the mutations were causing aggressive behavior, anxiety behavior, and fascinatingly, postnatal depression, which I'll show you in a second. So why should the genes for obesity also cause these other behaviors? Well, if you study mice and other animals, you know that these behaviors are very closely linked, because what they have in common is that these behaviors are all hardwired in the brain and they're fundamental for survival.

And so these behaviors are often called innate or instinctive behaviors. You don't need to learn to do these things. An animal will developmentally know that it has to eat, that it has to escape from a threat, that it has to reproduce, and that it has to socialize and interact with other animals and cooperate. And so what we're seeing is that these obesity genes are identifying critical nodes in the system that regulates human instinctive behavior.

## TRPC5, maternal care, and postpartum depression

Now, just to show you one example, which I found completely fascinating, is that we found a deletion of one of these genes called TRPC5. This is a channel that regulates the electrical activity of different neuronal populations, including those POMC neurons that respond to leptin in the hypothalamus. We found boys, males, who had a deletion of just one gene, and it was TRPC5. It's on the X chromosome, so they didn't have any TRPC5 at all.

They had a very strong phenotype. They had increased food seeking, food hoarding behavior. They would hide food away for when they might need it. They developed obesity.

They had anxiety, autism, aggressive behavior in response to sensory stimuli. They would often stay awake for 48 hours at a time. And fascinatingly, their mothers had postnatal depression. Now, this was only two families, and you could say, okay, well, what does that mean?

Two mothers had postnatal depression. But I've seen hundreds of patients with different genetic disorders, and it struck me as being unusual as to why both of these mothers should get really bad postnatal depression. So here we'd collaborated with my colleague Yong Xu, who had made the mice that carried the human mutation, providing a model of the human disease. And I didn't know really what to say.

I said, could you just look at the mothers, the mice that are mothers that have the mutation? And he sent me this video. And you can see on the photograph on the bottom left that a normal mouse, or the wild-type mouse, when it's a mother, will make a nest, a nice tidy nest, and it will put the babies in the nest, and it will feed the babies. Whereas if you look at the nest, the bedding of the mouse with a mutation, it's a complete mess.

These mice are not interested in caring for their offspring. They make a really poor nest. They sometimes will leave the babies. Sometimes they will trample them, and they will not put them properly in the nest and look after them.

So there's impaired maternal care with this single mutation. We then looked at depressive behavior. There are ways of doing this in mice. You can do a forced swim test, where you have a cylinder of water and see what the mice will do.

And if mice are exhibiting despair, they'll try, but then they fail and they give up. So these mice exhibit depressive behavior. They also don't like sucrose, which is anhedonia. So depressive behavior, fascinatingly, only in the mice that are mothers, not in male mice nor in virgin female mice.

So we see impaired maternal care and depressive behavior in mothers carrying this human mutation. Two possible mechanisms: one, prolactin release. Prolactin goes up with suckling, but it's impaired in these mice because the channel regulates dopamine release. And also, fascinatingly, the channel is expressed on oxytocin neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus.

If we delete the channel just in that location, you can cause all of these behaviors. If you put it back, you can correct everything. So here we have, for the first time, a neural circuit in the hypothalamus that links maternal care and depressive behavior in female mice, analogous to human postnatal depression. And really, this has led us to realize that we have a lot that we could learn from the study of our patients with severe obesity, who can tell us about these highly conserved innate behaviors.

And so with my colleagues Tiago Branco in London and Yong Xu, we're going to be studying human mutations that cause disrupted behaviors, making mouse models, and also studying the patients in a joint program of research.

## Nicotine preference and instinctive behavior

And just to whet your appetite for some of the exciting and quite challenging things that we're finding: we have a particular gene that we found in humans is associated with smoking intensity. We were looking for a BMI signal, and there is a BMI signal, but there's an even stronger signal with number of cigarettes smoked, or pack years. So we had the mouse with the human mutation because we were studying its obesity. We put the mouse through a challenge where we exposed it to nicotine.

And normally, wild-type mice don't like the nicotine; they prefer to go to the other chamber because they find it aversive. But after repeated exposure to the nicotine, they'll start to get addicted and go towards the nicotine. These mice with the human mutation immediately go to the nicotine chamber. They exhibit a massive increase in nicotine preference.

So this is really challenging the way that we think about human behaviors, demonstrating a strong biological basis.

## MC4R, autonomic function, blood pressure, and lipids

Now, I mentioned that the other major mechanism by which the hypothalamus can regulate homeostasis is through activation of the autonomic nervous system. And this probably explains the particular phenotype we saw here, where patients with MC4R mutations are relatively protected from hypertension despite their severe obesity. They also have impaired sympathetic tone, reflected in a reduced heart rate response to a physiological stress such as a clamp. And when our colleagues at Lilly developed the first generation of MC4R agonists, they found that these drugs, whilst they reduced weight, increased blood pressure.

So MC4Rs in the hypothalamus are directly connected to the sympathetic nervous system and therefore to the regulation of blood pressure. And this explains why, in common obesity, when people gain weight their blood pressure goes up, and when they lose weight their blood pressure comes down: this system is directly connected to the sympathetic nervous system and to blood pressure regulation. It's likely that the sympathetic nervous system also explains this other phenotype that we recently defined: patients with MC4R mutations, despite severe obesity and a mean BMI of 45, have entirely normal levels of LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, quite in contrast to people with common obesity. And here is data from UK Biobank where we compared people with mutations versus those without at the same BMI and the same age, and you can see a consistently reduced LDL cholesterol and triglycerides.

And we think this is because MC4Rs in the brain, through the sympathetic nervous system, are regulating the liver, the uptake of LDL, and the recycling of LDL receptors, something that has been shown clearly in mice. It's also likely that the sympathetic nervous system is regulating postprandial lipid clearance. We see differences when we give a high-fat meal challenge to people with these mutations versus controls, and that's probably because the system is set up to favor lipid storage. And this really reminds us that, as well as the brain, of course we're regulating peripheral hormone function through the sympathetic nervous system.

And of course the final piece of the puzzle is to understand how our hormones interact with the brain, both by sending signals and, of course, mediating the effects of those signals on peripheral metabolism.

## Hormone Cell Atlas

And here we really set out to use the latest technologies to understand hormone action at a really unparalleled new level. This work was just published in *Science* last week, and the full paper will come out in a couple of weeks. It represents a collaboration between my lab and the lab of Sarah A. Teichmann in Cambridge, who's a world leader in the Human Cell Atlas. So this is where you can take any human tissue and you can separate out all the cells and look at the RNA or the genes that are expressed in every single cell in that tissue. And what we did here with Lijiang Fei, who's a postdoc in Sarah's team, and Isabel Huang-Doran from my team, was put this together to see whether we could understand how hormones work at the cellular level.

And so what we did is we took 47 different human organs and tissues, as you can see on the map on the right, and we put all of that information together and aligned it so that we could look right across all of those tissues to make an atlas. We then made a repository of all the hormones, the receptors, processing enzymes, and other enzymes that you need to make a hormone, and we integrated the two. And what this allowed us to do is to map out where are hormones being made and where are they acting right across the body at cellular level. And we learned some interesting things.

So firstly, we learned that actually our human tissues display very unique signatures of hormone gene expression. There are very specific hormones being made in our different organs, which we know, but we're finding some new ones as well. We can then really start to dissect out things at a cellular level. So this type of map is called a UMAP, where you're basically trying to identify clusters of cells that share the same pattern of gene expression.

And those clusters in these coloured blobs show that, within the pituitary and other endocrine tissues, you're beginning to identify very distinct signatures of the different cell types that exist there. And when you connect those, you can also try to map out the endocrine axes. Now, when we look at the receptors, we can really learn where are all the receptors in every single organ, in every single cell. And you start to see certain patterns where, if you're looking at the adrenergic receptors that mediate the effects of the sympathetic nervous system, there are very specific adrenergic receptors on some organs and within those organs on some cells, which we can really map out in huge detail.

## Receptor expression and drug effects

And of course, by trying to understand where the receptors are, we can think about the drugs that target those receptors. And here's one example from the GLP-1 receptor, the GIP receptor, and the glucagon receptor. It allows us to think about the effects of the drugs, but also their side effects, because drugs will work wherever those receptors are expressed. And fascinatingly, we found that the GLP-1 and the GIP receptors are expressed on the sinoatrial node in the heart, which controls heart rate, of course. And it may explain why these drugs, in all of the clinical trials, are increasing heart rate, because you have those receptors in a very precise location on the sinoatrial node.

Now, some of these effects may be protective, and sometimes there may be adverse effects. And I think there's a real potential here for us to use this resource to try to understand much more about the effects of drugs that we see.

## Adipose tissue heterogeneity

And one of the things that we're keen to do is to try to use this knowledge to advance our understanding of metabolic organs and processes. And here we've focused on adipose tissue, where we've integrated the data from multiple different adipose tissue data sets and tried to say what are the hormones that are being produced specifically in adipose tissue, and where are the hormonal signals being received. And we can see different patterns, that hormones are being produced differently in different depots. Some data here from the androgen receptor show that receptor expression differs in people with different levels of BMI.

And you can really start to understand the different signatures when you're starting looking right across all of these adipose tissue depots. We also see that not all adipocytes behave in the same way. So if you start to look at what these genes are doing and perform a pathway analysis, you see patterns such as this, where the adipocytes around the heart are specifically involved in fatty acid metabolism, in contrast to adipocytes found in different locations. So this is all generating questions that need to be then tested experimentally.

So this is a huge resource which we've made freely available. I encourage you to go and explore our website, the Hormone Cell Atlas, where you can download data. We also have made the scripts available so you can interrogate your own RNA sequencing data for the different hormones that are being produced and acting, and so you can start to build and expand on knowledge. Particularly, I think the next interface is going to be when we're taking samples from patients with different disorders: where are those hormones being made and where are they working?

And finally, of course, we wanted to see the relevance of this for human disease, and we looked at around 400 monogenic diseases to see where those disease genes are expressed. This may give us new insights into why patients have particular phenotypes, depending upon where those genes are expressed.

## Mechanism-based therapies

And really, just to finish with the patients, because the point of our work is to translate our discovery science to improve things for patients, we've learned that this melanocortin pathway is so fundamental to the regulation of weight that targeting it can be very effective for therapy. The second generation of MC4R agonists was setmelanotide, very effective in POMC deficiency and in other genetic obesity syndromes. Fascinatingly, also very effective in patients with acquired hypothalamic obesity, such as those with craniopharyngiomas, where you're disrupting the hypothalamus. Presumably you still have enough MC4Rs.

Those patients are responding very well to this drug. The phase 3 trial is about to be published. Very exciting data for those patients. Now, patients with MC4R mutations—the commonest genetic cause of obesity—will of course not respond to an MC4R agonist, but we showed here that they do respond to GLP-1 receptor agonism.

We collaborated with Lilly to retrospectively examine their data from the SURMOUNT-1 trial. Interestingly, we found that about 1.4% of people in the trial—32 of 2,291 participants—happened to have an MC4R mutation; they just didn't know it. They were a little bit younger, a little bit heavier, but they responded as well to treatment with tirzepatide. So this is really first-line therapy for patients with MC4R deficiency, and it's likely that a different pathway is activated by GLP-1 in contrast to MC4R agonism.

And of course, new things keep coming up all the time based on understanding the mechanisms underlying the disease. We showed many years ago that many of the MC4R mutations cause misfolding of the receptors, and there are now chaperones or drugs being developed that correct that misfolded protein. And we're very excited that later this year we'll run the first phase 1b trial of a new form of corrector therapy with Congruence Therapeutics, which aims to fix misfolded proteins in patients with MC4R deficiency.

## Conclusions

So I just want to finish by hoping to convince you that obesity is complex, it's fascinating, it's an endocrine disorder worthy of study, and it arises from the complex interaction of social, environmental, and indeed genetic factors. Obesity is strongly driven by genetic factors, particularly for people who have severe obesity, but even those who are thin have genetic drivers for their thinness. And we have now an entire program of research studying people who are healthy but thin to find the genes that are keeping them thin, which we hope then will be effective targets for weight loss therapy. Many of the genes work on regulating appetite, and this proves that appetite has a strong biological basis and is not simply under voluntary control.

And we can use these insights to develop new targets for therapy. Importantly, the direct relevance for patients is that genetic testing is now recommended, particularly where obesity is severe and starts at an early age, often before the age of 5. There are mechanism-based therapies, and an increasing number are being developed. I think it's an exciting stage for these patients who have suffered for a very long time.

But importantly, by bringing scientific inquiry to a condition that was often neglected and ignored and considered purely to be down to a lack of willpower, we can really start to change the way we think about obesity and try and remove the stigma faced by people with obesity, including the stigma and discrimination they have traditionally faced from healthcare professionals. And we think that's an important off-target effect of our research. And critically, we can show that these central pathways are relevant for the regulation of appetite more broadly, and of course that is now evidenced by the many new drugs that are emerging for the treatment of common obesity.
