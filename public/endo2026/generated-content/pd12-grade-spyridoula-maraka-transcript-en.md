### Opening and overview of GRADE

Dr. Maraka.

Thank you, Chris. Good morning, everyone. Thank you so much for coming. So now that you have the historical context, I will proceed with an overview of the GRADE methodology.

In a simplified way, in guidelines we go from the question, to the evidence, to finally reaching statements that include recommendations. For this process, as we heard, we follow the GRADE framework. GRADE is a structured approach to assessing and rating the quality of the best available evidence and developing health care recommendations. It includes the use of Evidence-to-Decision (EtD) frameworks to help ensure that all important health-related and contextual factors are considered when we make recommendations.

The Endocrine Society believes that faithful adherence to GRADE processes is critically important to maximize guidelines' credibility and trustworthiness by minimizing distortions, such as biases, in the guideline-development process; increasing transparency in the guideline-development process; and, ultimately, supporting adherence to National Academy of Medicine standards.

### From themes to focused recommendations

During that process, we have moved from themes to recommendations in terms of what guidelines look like. On the left, you can see an older-looking guideline where, basically, in addition to all the recommendations, we were giving an overview of the whole topic. Now we have moved to what you can see on the right, where we identify questions, evaluate the evidence, and move to the recommendations. This usually involves fewer recommendations, limited writing assignments, and shorter guidelines, but it provides more clarity and is easier to follow in clinical practice.

### Guideline Development Panel and conflict-of-interest policy

One of the first parts of the guideline-development process is to form a Guideline Development Panel, or GDP. That panel includes subject-matter experts. The specialties can vary depending on the topic of the guideline. For example, we can have adult endocrinologists and pediatric endocrinologists. Sometimes we have representation from obstetrics and gynecology, typically general internal medicine, and we can also have experts from nutrition or epidemiology.

Another essential component of the guideline panel is two methodologists: one who takes the lead on GRADE implementation, and another who takes the lead on systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Nowadays, guideline panels include at least one patient representative because the patient's voice is very important. If the guideline is to be endorsed by other societies, the guideline panel includes members from those societies so they are represented on the panel.

Another important component of guideline development is the conflict-of-interest policy, or COI policy. It is applied at each step, from panel selection—where, for example, the guideline chairs are completely free of COI—all the way to guideline publication. This ensures transparency and integrity and ultimately results in a trustworthy, high-quality guideline.

Here you can see an overview of all the steps in the GRADE process, starting with evidence synthesis and leading to recommendation development. I will take you through each of these steps.

### Background and foreground questions

The first step in evidence synthesis is the formulation of clinical questions by the panel and the selection of outcomes. There are two types of questions. On the left, you can see what background questions look like. Those are broad, general questions about a health condition. They are good for informing people and guiding future decision-making, but sometimes they are not that practical. An example would be: What is the accuracy of MRI for diagnosing knee osteoarthritis?

On the other hand, we have foreground questions, which are narrower, specific questions about a particular decision or population. These questions can be pertinent to decision-making between patients and clinicians. They can compare either management or diagnostic options, and ultimately they lead to actionable recommendations. Imagine having a patient come to your office with a question. You can practically answer that question and give a recommendation if the guidelines include foreground questions. An example is: Should I use drug X instead of drug Y for patients with disease Y?

### PICO questions and prioritization

The questions have the PICO format. P stands for population, where we describe the population of interest in as much detail as necessary. This could include, for example, age, gender, or any particular comorbidities that we want to address. For the evidence review, one should define how narrow or broad the included population can be.

I stands for intervention. We describe the intervention, again, in as much detail as necessary: for example, which medication, which program, or which test we are particularly interested in. Then we have the comparator. What is the alternative? We need to describe that and be very specific. Are we looking at no active treatment, or just a different treatment? These components are very important for the systematic review and, ultimately, the meta-analysis later.

Finally, there are the outcomes. We need to describe patient-important outcomes that will inform decisions about the intervention. What outcomes are we interested in? Examples could be mortality, morbidity, and quality of life.

The guideline panel starts by brainstorming all the different questions that they think might be important. Usually there are many, but ultimately they have to narrow the list down to about 10. This is a predetermined number of questions based on the resources available. After they have brainstormed and put all the questions into a list, the questions are scored. Ultimately, the 10 most highly rated, if you will, are selected for the guideline panel to address.

### Selecting patient-important outcomes

Then we move to the outcomes. Typically, you do not want to have more than seven outcomes because it becomes very difficult to manage. Guidelines usually have about three to five outcomes that we examine. You want to include a mix of desirable and undesirable outcomes, and we want outcomes that are important to patients.

Many times, when we ask panels what outcomes to measure, they will say, “Oh, you know, let's not look into this outcome because we're not going to find any evidence.” We actually do not decide outcomes based on the availability of evidence. At the end of the day, if there is no evidence for an outcome, that might mean that we need to draw attention to it as a research question—something that we need to examine in future research—or we can choose a surrogate outcome to measure. We often say that not everything that is important is measured, and not everything that is measured is important for decision-making.

Let me give you an example. Again, there is brainstorming by the committee, and then each outcome placed on the list is scored and prioritized. Ultimately, we stratify outcomes as critical for decision-making, important but not critical for decision-making, and of low importance for decision-making.

As an example, we can have mortality, fractures, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and adverse events. These are all critical for decision-making for this panel. Very often, we ask the panel to consider whether, if we do not find evidence for a specific outcome, they want to suggest a surrogate. In this case, the panel determined that instead of fractures, if we did not find anything, we could look at bone mineral density. It is important to note that the use of surrogate outcomes can decrease the certainty of the evidence.

### Evidence synthesis and certainty of evidence

The next step is the systematic review. As you heard, there is a team from Mayo Clinic. They create evidence profiles and rate the quality of evidence for each outcome. Here you can see an example of a forest plot at the evidence-synthesis stage, where all the studies answering this specific PICO question are pooled together and a single effect estimate is produced. We will discuss this more in the next slides.

Judgments about the certainty of the evidence are based on several factors. We have risk of bias: Were the research studies well done? We have inconsistency: Were the results consistent across studies? We have indirectness: How directly do the results relate to our question? Then we have imprecision: Is the effect estimate precise, or are the confidence intervals very wide? Finally, we have publication bias: Are these all the studies conducted in the field that we obtained through the systematic review?

Certainty of evidence is stratified from high, where we are very confident that the true effect lies close to the estimated effect, all the way down to very low, where we have very little confidence in the effect estimate and the true effect is likely to be substantially different from the estimated effect. You can also see that represented by the small symbols next to recommendations in guidelines. Typically, at least in endocrinology, we end up with low or very low certainty of evidence, so that is something guideline panels need to work with.

Here is another example where the evidence-synthesis team provides the systematic review and effect estimate. Separately, on the right, you can see the certainty of evidence for each outcome. Again, we will talk about this in a little more detail later.

### From evidence to recommendations

Then we proceed to recommendation development, where the panel addresses and makes judgments about all the important Evidence-to-Decision considerations. Finally, they develop a recommendation that includes its direction and strength.

Here you can see the Evidence-to-Decision table, which includes the summary of judgments. GRADEpro is software that we use to support guideline development, and Dr. Brito will go through all these different factors in much greater detail later. Here, I want to talk a little about the balance of effects.

When we move from evidence to a recommendation, one of the judgments we make concerns the balance of effects. On one hand we have desirable consequences, and on the other we have undesirable consequences. We need to determine where the balance lies.

In this example, the desirable effects clearly outweigh the undesirable effects. When the balance for or against an intervention is clear, we are able to provide a strong recommendation. At the population level, the implication of a strong recommendation is that most people in this situation would want the recommended course of action, and very few would not. For health care workers, it means that all or almost all people should receive the recommended course of action in all or almost all circumstances, so detailed discussions with patients are generally not necessary. For policymakers, it means that the recommendation can be adopted as policy in most situations.

On the other hand, sometimes the balance is not very clear. In the situation shown here, undesirable effects probably outweigh desirable effects. When the balance for or against the intervention is not clear, we issue a conditional recommendation. Instead of the language “we recommend,” we use “we suggest.”

At the population level, the implication of a conditional recommendation is that a majority of people in this situation would want the recommended course of action, but a substantial proportion would not. For health care workers, it means that we need to consider key factors, understand the evidence, and help patients make a decision consistent with their own values. That is where we implement shared decision-making. For policymakers, there is a need for substantial debate and stakeholder involvement.

Based on the balance of the EtD factors, these are the options when the panel votes on the strength and direction of a recommendation. At one end, we have a strong recommendation against the intervention, where undesirable effects clearly outweigh desirable effects. At the other end, we have a strong recommendation for the intervention, where desirable effects clearly outweigh undesirable effects. Then we have options for a conditional recommendation, either against or for the intervention. Sometimes we end up in the middle, with a conditional recommendation for either the intervention or the comparison. That is the situation where the trade-offs are equally balanced or uncertain.
