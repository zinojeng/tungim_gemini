The learning objectives are to understand the role of molecular testing in the evaluation of indeterminate thyroid nodules, to be aware of the available molecular tests and how they work, and to recognize advances as well as limitations in using these tests.

### Indeterminate cytology and baseline risk

Indeterminate cytology accounts for about 18%–22% of cytology results. It includes Bethesda III, atypia of undetermined significance (AUS). Bethesda III is divided into nuclear atypia and other atypia, which includes architectural changes, oncocytic cells, and lymphoid tissue. Bethesda IV includes follicular neoplasm and oncocytic follicular neoplasm. Bethesda V is suspicious for malignancy.

The Bethesda system provides baseline risks of malignancy. For Bethesda III, the risk is 13%–30%. When the subtypes are considered, nuclear atypia carries a risk around 40%, while other atypia has a risk closer to 20%. For Bethesda IV, the risk is 23%–34%; for Bethesda V, it is 67%–83%.

Because the Bethesda V risk is so high, surgery is generally recommended. Molecular testing may help determine the extent of surgery if high-risk mutations are found, but it has a smaller role. I will therefore focus on Bethesda III and IV nodules.

### Management options for Bethesda III and IV nodules

The draft guidelines present several equally valid options for a Bethesda III nodule. One is repeat fine-needle aspiration (FNA). Repeating the FNA changes the diagnosis about 40%–60% of the time. About 30%–50% shift to a benign result, about 15% may be malignant, and about 10%–30% are again atypical.

If the initial result is Bethesda III and the repeat FNA is benign, the risk of malignancy is low—around 4%, similar to a benign FNA—and monitoring is appropriate. If both the initial and repeat results are Bethesda III, the risk rises to about 40%; surgery or molecular testing may then be considered.

Other reasonable Bethesda III options include molecular testing, surgery when the ultrasound is highly suspicious or the patient has compressive symptoms or prefers surgery, surveillance when sonographic suspicion is low, and second-opinion review by an experienced thyroid cytopathologist at a tertiary center.

There are fewer options for Bethesda IV. Molecular testing, surgery—particularly for a large nodule or according to patient preference—and second-opinion cytology are reasonable. Ultrasound is still important but is less helpful for follicular neoplasms, because follicular thyroid cancers and follicular-variant papillary thyroid cancers tend to have fewer specific sonographic features.

Molecular testing stratifies cytologically indeterminate nodules into clinically meaningful risk categories. The goals are to avoid unnecessary surgery for nodules likely to be benign and potentially distinguish high-risk cancers for which total thyroidectomy is best from low-risk cancers for which lobectomy is reasonable.

These decisions are not made from the molecular result alone. The pretest probability of malignancy influences both positive and negative predictive value. The same result may therefore carry different meaning in a practice with a high prevalence of malignancy than in one with a lower prevalence. The patient's age, comorbidity, nodule size, ultrasound appearance, cytologic subtype, surgical risk, access to follow-up, and preference must remain part of the interpretation.

### Commercial molecular tests

There are six commercially available molecular tests discussed in the guideline table: three in the United States and three outside the United States. The amount of validation and real-world evidence varies. Afirma GSC and ThyroSeq v3 have extensive testing; platforms such as ThyroSPEC have fewer studies.

Afirma GSC uses next-generation RNA sequencing, whole-transcriptome analysis, and machine-learning algorithms. The slide specifies 10,916 genes, including 1,115 core classifier genes. Additional classifiers identify parathyroid tissue, medullary thyroid carcinoma, and high-risk findings such as `BRAF V600E` and `RET/PTC` fusions. Follicular-content and oncocytic-cell indices were added to improve performance in oncocytic neoplasms. Results are reported as benign or suspicious. For suspicious results, the Xpression Atlas evaluates 593 genes, 905 variants, and 235 fusion pairs.

The first part of the Afirma report is intended to answer whether the sample behaves as benign or suspicious. The additional classifiers and Xpression Atlas can then provide information that may affect the extent of surgery or counseling, but a suspicious result is not itself a histologic diagnosis.

ThyGeNEXT/ThyraMIR v2 is a two-step test. ThyGeNEXT uses next-generation sequencing to detect DNA mutations and RNA fusions. If no clearly actionable alteration is found, ThyraMIR applies an algorithmic pairwise comparison of six growth-promoting and five growth-suppressing microRNAs. The reported malignancy risks are about 99% for high-risk, 1% for low-risk, and 38% for moderate-risk results.

ThyroSeq v3 is a next-generation sequencing platform that examines DNA and RNA across 112 genes for mutations, fusions, copy-number alterations, and gene-expression changes. It provides a genomic classifier score: 0 is negative, 1 is currently negative, and 2 or higher is positive. The report identifies the molecular alterations and assigns a corresponding risk of malignancy.

These platforms use somewhat different technology and reporting language, but all are trying to separate nodules that can reasonably be observed from those for which diagnostic surgery remains appropriate. Their validation populations and local performance should be understood before the reported probabilities are applied to an individual patient.

Outside the United States, Mir-THYpe is a microRNA-based classifier developed in Brazil and available in parts of Latin America and Africa. ThyroidPrint is a 10-gene quantitative PCR classifier developed in Chile; it measures markers of the tumor microenvironment and epithelial cells and uses a neural-network algorithm. ThyroSPEC is a Canadian MassARRAY test that assesses 139 prevalent mutations and gene fusions and reports no mutation, benign, intermediate-risk, malignant, or high-risk malignant categories.

### Comparison of Afirma GSC and ThyroSeq v3

A UCLA randomized clinical trial evaluated roughly 350 Bethesda III and IV nodules. Samples were assigned by alternating month to Afirma GSC or ThyroSeq v3. The final analysis included 189 nodules tested with Afirma and 149 with ThyroSeq. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were similar, with no significant difference in test performance.

A 2025 meta-analysis by Dowell and colleagues reviewed real-world experience from 13 ThyroSeq v3 studies and 16 Afirma GSC studies. Both tests were effective rule-out tools that reduced unnecessary diagnostic surgery. Specificity was lower when analysis was restricted to surgically confirmed cases, because most molecularly negative nodules do not undergo surgery. When negative molecular results were treated as benign in the full cohorts, estimated specificity improved. Overall, the two platforms appeared comparable.

That distinction is important. A surgical series preferentially includes positive or clinically worrisome nodules and excludes many negative nodules that remain under observation. It can therefore make a rule-out test appear less specific than it is in routine practice. Conversely, treating every unoperated molecularly negative nodule as truly benign can overestimate performance. Both approaches carry verification bias, which is why longer follow-up is needed.

### Oncocytic lesions

Oncocytes are large oxyphilic cells derived from follicular epithelial cells. They occur in benign conditions such as Hashimoto thyroiditis, autonomous nodules, and hyperplastic multinodular goiter, as well as in oncocytic thyroid carcinoma.

Earlier molecular tests had low benign call rates for oncocytic nodules, frequently classifying them as suspicious or positive and leading to surgery for lesions that proved benign. Newer versions incorporate knowledge of oncocytic tumor biology, including chromosomal loss of heterozygosity and prominent mitochondrial DNA mutations.

In the Afirma GSC validation set, 26 oncocytic neoplasms included nine cancers and 17 benign lesions. The updated classifier reached about 89% sensitivity and nearly 60% specificity. The ThyroSeq v3 validation set included 52 oncocytic lesions; all 10 cancers were classified as suspicious, with a 53% benign call rate, 100% sensitivity, and 67% specificity.

The benign call rate is the proportion of tested nodules called benign or negative. In the older generations, oncocytic cytology often drove a suspicious result, so the test failed to spare many patients from diagnostic surgery. The newer classifiers substantially improve that practical outcome while maintaining sensitivity.

The UCLA trial's oncocytic subgroup showed 100% sensitivity for both tests, specificity of 88% for Afirma and 68% for ThyroSeq, and benign call rates of 84% and 56%, respectively. These values were substantially better than those of earlier test generations.

### RAS mutations and active surveillance

Thyroid cancers are often discussed as BRAF-like or RAS-like tumors. `HRAS`, `NRAS`, and `KRAS` alterations are common in indeterminate nodules and can occur in both benign and malignant lesions. This makes a clinical recommendation based on an isolated RAS alteration difficult. Surgery is still commonly recommended, but recent retrospective studies have examined active surveillance.

A three-center New York study identified 209 RAS-mutant indeterminate nodules that underwent surgery; 33% were malignant. Sixty-three nodules in 62 patients were monitored for a median of 24 months. Ninety-four percent remained stable; six later underwent surgery, and one was malignant.

A Canadian single-center study using ThyroSPEC identified 126 surgical cases, with 34% malignant. Seventy-seven nodules were monitored for a median of 23 months; 81% remained stable, seven underwent delayed surgery, and two were malignant. The cancers in both cohorts were generally low-risk, often follicular-variant papillary thyroid carcinoma. These studies suggest that immediate surgery may not always be necessary for an isolated RAS-mutant nodule, particularly when comorbidity makes surgery difficult, although surveillance evidence remains limited.

A 2026 study by Wong and colleagues evaluated 354 RAS-mutant nodules; 302 underwent surgery, and 52.3% were malignant. Sixty percent of the cancers were invasive encapsulated follicular-variant papillary thyroid carcinoma. Among RAS-only nodules, 47% were benign or low-risk neoplasms. Malignancy occurred in 54.8% of RAS-positive nodules with one additional alteration and reached 100% when more alterations were present. Comprehensive molecular profiling therefore matters: the number and type of accompanying alterations modify the risk conveyed by RAS alone.

These data are still evolving. They do not establish that every isolated RAS-mutant nodule should undergo active surveillance, nor do they prove that surgery is always required. They show why “RAS-positive” is not a complete risk statement. An isolated alteration, the presence of additional mutations, the ultrasound pattern, growth, patient age, and operative risk all influence management.

### Cost-effectiveness

Cost-effectiveness analyses comparing molecular testing with diagnostic thyroidectomy have been mixed because surgical experience, local costs, and molecular-test performance vary by institution.

A prospective Canadian study evaluated 500 consecutive Bethesda III and IV nodules, 1–4 cm and predominantly TI-RADS 3 or 4, at five academic centers using ThyroSeq v3. The benign call rate was 78.4% for AUS and 65.1% for follicular neoplasm/oncocytic follicular neoplasm. Using the molecular result for risk stratification and reserving surgery largely for positive cases was projected to save US$6.1 million over 10 years.

The projected savings arise mainly from avoiding the immediate and downstream costs of diagnostic thyroidectomy. They are sensitive to the price of the molecular test, local operative costs, complication rates, the likelihood of lifelong thyroid-hormone replacement, and the proportion of negative results. A result is not cost-saving if it does not change what the patient and clinician choose to do.

Molecular testing is more cost-effective when the benign call rate is high. It therefore has its greatest value in indeterminate nodules with low or intermediate sonographic suspicion, where a negative result is more likely. A sonographically suspicious nodule is more likely to produce a positive molecular result and proceed to surgery anyway. Patient preference also matters: testing adds little value if the patient would choose surgery regardless of the result.

### Follow-up after a negative molecular result

Long-term follow-up data are limited. A 2023 report followed the UCLA randomized cohort. Among 369 randomized nodules, 176 had negative molecular results. Fourteen patients chose immediate surgery, and all 14 nodules were benign. Of 162 monitored nodules, 12 later underwent surgery; one cancer was found. The reported false-negative rate was 0.6% over a median follow-up of about three years.

A 2024 study by Ahmadi and colleagues evaluated 834 indeterminate nodules tested with Afirma GSC. Of 528 molecularly benign nodules, 37 underwent immediate surgery, revealing five cancers. Thirty later underwent delayed surgery, revealing three cancers. The reported false-negative rate was approximately 2%.

The reasons for delayed surgery included growth, changing sonographic features, symptoms, or patient preference. Importantly, surveillance selects which negative nodules eventually obtain histologic verification. The apparent false-negative rate therefore depends on both follow-up duration and how unoperated nodules are classified.

These studies are reassuring but do not imply that surveillance can stop. A benign or negative molecular result should return the patient to ultrasound monitoring similar to that used for a cytologically benign nodule. Growth or new suspicious features should prompt reevaluation.

### Conclusions

Molecular testing is one component of the evaluation of indeterminate thyroid nodules. It must be interpreted in the context of cytologic subtype, sonographic pattern, clinical history, local cancer prevalence, available expertise, and patient preferences.

Current platforms are effective rule-out tools and have improved substantially for oncocytic lesions. Specific alterations can refine risk, but an isolated RAS mutation is not synonymous with malignancy. Comprehensive profiling, rather than a single mutation label, is important. Negative results still require surveillance, and the evidence base continues to evolve.

The key clinical question is whether the test will change management. Before ordering it, the clinician should discuss what a benign, suspicious, or mutation-specific result would mean for that patient. Molecular testing is most useful when a negative result would support observation and a positive result would meaningfully inform surgery. It is less useful when the patient has already chosen surgery or when clinical and ultrasound findings make surgery appropriate regardless of the molecular result.
