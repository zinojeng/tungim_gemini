## Opening and overview

**Christopher McCartney:** Thank you all for being here. I was the GRADE methodologist for this wonderful, wonderful panel. I'm an adult endocrinologist, but the panel made me want to be a pediatric endocrinologist.

I'm going to give you a very high-level overview of the GRADE methodology for producing clinical practice recommendations. GRADE stands for **Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation**.

## Formulating clinical questions

To start—and this was previewed just a minute ago—the panel of experts will brainstorm the important clinical questions that we need to answer in a guideline. Of course, there are so many clinical questions you could ask, so it involves brainstorming what you could ask and then prioritizing, because there is going to be a limit to the number you can put in a guideline. Then you develop those clinical questions and select the outcomes.

The way we frame the clinical questions is in a specific format that we call the PICO format. That is an acronym for four elements:

- **P — Population:** the population that you are interested in.
- **I — Intervention:** the intervention that you want to assess.
- **C — Comparator:** the comparator against which you assess that intervention.
- **O — Outcome(s):** the outcomes of interest.

This is an example from the guideline: one of the PICO questions related to gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist use. I have color-coded these. You can see that the population is patients with central precocious puberty (CPP), the intervention is GnRH agonist treatment, and the comparator is no GnRH agonist treatment.

## Selecting patient-important outcomes

When it comes to the outcomes that you could select, again, the panel would brainstorm the different outcomes that might be important. Some outcomes might not be important for decision-making when you are trying to develop a recommendation. Some will be important, but some will be critical for decision-making. In other words, if you do not have information about the health effect of the intervention on this outcome, this outcome, and this outcome, we really cannot make a decision about what we will recommend. Those are the outcomes that are critical for decision-making.

What you see here is for the same clinical question I mentioned about GnRH agonist use versus no treatment. These are the outcomes selected by the panel of experts. What I want to emphasize is that, as much as possible, we like to focus on what we call patient-important outcomes—or, in this case, outcomes important to patients and their caretakers.

One outcome that you will all be very familiar with is adult height. That is an important outcome for treatment. Psychological well-being is another, and so on.

Just a quick comment about surrogate outcomes: sometimes we do not have data on those patient-important outcomes, so we use surrogates. The reason I bring this up is that whenever you use a surrogate instead of the actual outcome of interest, it introduces some uncertainty. For example, adult height is what patients and caretakers really care about. You could use predicted adult height as a surrogate, but you are going to introduce some uncertainty when you do that.

## Systematic review and evidence synthesis

After the clinical questions are assembled and the outcomes are selected, we turn those clinical questions over to our partners at Mayo Clinic, who conduct the systematic reviews for us. This is led by Dr. M. Hassan Murad.

In essence, they are trying to find all the relevant evidence through a systematic review, and then they synthesize that evidence for us. In other words, based on all the available evidence, what is our best estimate of the effect of an intervention on the outcome of interest?

One last thing: they help us understand the certainty of that evidence. They give us the best estimate, but they also help us understand our confidence—or what confidence we should have—in those estimates.

If you have many randomized clinical trials, that often means you will have very high confidence in those estimates. But I think it is very common in pediatric endocrinology, where you are often relying largely on observational studies, that confidence will be lower because those studies are subject to bias and confounding, for example.

## Certainty-of-evidence designations

When we talk about these different levels of confidence, one way to think about high certainty is that your confidence is high that the estimated effect is very close to the true effect of that intervention. I am going to go through these fairly quickly.

As we move from **high** to **moderate**, **low**, and **very low** certainty, our confidence decreases. Again, in pediatric settings, it is not uncommon for the certainty of the evidence to be low or very low.

When confidence is low or very low, it means that the estimated effect may be substantially different from the true effect, but you do not know in which direction. It could be different in a way that supports or strengthens what you see, or it could be something that mitigates your estimate.

## Moving from evidence to a recommendation

After the systematic reviews are completed, we go through a process called **Evidence to Decision (EtD)**. Basically, you have the evidence, and we want the panel to work through that evidence, along with other important factors, to reach a recommendation about whether you should use the intervention.

The panel determines the direction—whether to recommend for the intervention or against it—and then the strength: whether it will be a strong recommendation or a conditional recommendation.

To give you a sense of what the panel is doing in the Evidence-to-Decision process, it is trying to weigh all the desirable effects of an intervention against all the undesirable effects. These include not only health effects, which are what we are often thinking about, such as adult height and psychological well-being. There may be desirable effects on the different outcomes, and there may be undesirable effects.

We also have to incorporate our certainty in the evidence. If we are highly confident that our estimates are very likely to be accurate, that is one thing. But if you have low or very low confidence in the accuracy of your estimate, that will play a role in how you judge those health effects. Then, of course, there is the balance of effects: does the balance of health effects favor the intervention or not?

Importantly, there are several other factors that are critical for decision-making when trying to choose between an intervention and a comparator. These include costs, resources required, cost-effectiveness, the potential impact on health equity, the acceptability of an intervention versus a comparator or alternative, and feasibility. Is it something that is easy to implement and feasible, or something that is difficult and perhaps less feasible? All of these factors are weighed.

## Strong versus conditional recommendations

I am offering a couple of examples of how this might look. In a case where the desirable effects of an intervention very clearly outweigh the undesirable effects, you might issue a **strong recommendation for the intervention**. Typically, a strong recommendation is supported by evidence in which you have high confidence, and it seems that everything—or almost everything—aligns in favor of the intervention.

However, you may have a situation where the undesirable consequences probably outweigh the desirable consequences, but it is not entirely clear. Sometimes this may relate to the fact that you do not have high or moderate certainty of evidence; perhaps you have low or very low certainty. Or it could be that you have some desirable effects and some undesirable effects, and different patients and caregivers may weigh those effects differently.

In this particular example, where the undesirable effects probably outweigh the desirable effects, we would typically make a **conditional recommendation for the alternative**.

## How to interpret a strong recommendation

These last two slides relate to how you interpret a strong recommendation versus a conditional recommendation.

A strong recommendation is the case where nearly everything aligns toward either the intervention or the comparator. The language we use is **“we recommend.”** That phrasing implies a strong recommendation.

For individuals and their caretakers, it means that if they knew everything the panel knew and had that expertise, most patients and caregivers would want the recommended course of action.

For clinicians, it means that most patients and caretakers, if they knew everything that the panel knew and all the available evidence, would favor the recommended course of action. Not everyone necessarily would, but the vast majority would. This means that you do not necessarily have to spend a great deal of time on counseling and shared decision-making, because the panel has largely done that work for you.

I am not going to comment much on policymakers, except to say that strong recommendations can often be used as quality criteria. In other words, you could assess the quality of care against how well someone follows a strong recommendation.

## How to interpret a conditional recommendation

In contrast, for a conditional recommendation—and to let the cat out of the bag, all the recommendations in this guideline are conditional—the recommendation begins with **“we suggest.”**

Individual patients and their caretakers can understand this to mean that most patients and caretakers would want the suggested course of action, but an important minority would not. It depends on their values, their particular situation, and perhaps their clinical characteristics.

The same applies to clinicians: different choices will be appropriate for different individuals. This is a situation where shared decision-making is very, very important, including talking with patients about the anticipated benefits and potential risks.

For policymakers, a conditional recommendation should not be used as a quality criterion, because the appropriate course of action is going to depend on many different factors.
