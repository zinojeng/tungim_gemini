## Structure-based drug discovery and the androgen receptor

**Steven James C. Salvador:** In biology, we often hear the principle that form fits function. Structure is closely intertwined with biological activity, just as a lock fits a key or a bird's wings are suited for flight. At the molecular level, proteins such as enzymes and transcription factors assume diverse shapes and sizes that dictate their function, localization, and substrate specificity.

This structure-function relationship has revolutionized how drugs are identified and designed. Structure-based drug screening increasingly uses experimentally derived protein structures and computational methods to identify potential drugs. The approach becomes much more challenging when the available protein structures are limited.

That is the case for the androgen receptor (AR), a nuclear hormone receptor dysregulated in prostate cancer. Prostate cancer remains one of the most common cancers affecting men, with an estimated 1.5 million cases annually. Treatments such as enzalutamide and bicalutamide have significantly improved outcomes, but resistance ultimately develops, highlighting the need for new AR inhibitors.

## Homology-modeled antagonist conformation

One challenge is that most available AR structures are agonist-bound. In this conformation, helix 12 closes over the hormone-binding pocket, creating a relatively restricted binding site and potentially limiting the chemical diversity of ligands that can be screened. No experimental structure of wild-type AR ligand-binding domain (LBD) bound to an antagonist is currently available.

To address this limitation, we used the antagonist-bound LBD structure of the related progesterone receptor to construct a homology model of an antagonist-bound AR LBD conformation. We then screened 64,833 compounds against this model and identified 16 candidate AR inhibitors that otherwise produced unfavorable interactions when docked to agonist-bound conformations.

These results show that an antagonist-state receptor model can reveal potential inhibitors that may be missed when only conventional agonist-bound structures are used. This may provide a framework for identifying next-generation inhibitors for prostate cancer therapy.

## Audience Q&A

**Audience member:** For a broad scientific audience, what exactly is an agonist-bound structure, and why could having only those structures prevent scientists from finding new inhibitors or other molecules that target the receptor?

**Steven James C. Salvador:** Inhibitors have already been identified using agonist-bound conformations. What is unique in our study is the possibility that other inhibitors are missed by those conventional structures.

The antagonist-bound conformation in our model is open. This type of structure is difficult to crystallize because the proteins tend to aggregate, so it is difficult to characterize experimentally. We therefore used molecular simulation and homology modeling. Specifically, we used the crystallized ligand-binding domain of the related progesterone receptor to design an antagonist-bound AR conformation.

**Audience member:** Does the antagonist-bound state also involve allostery? Could the apparent sites be unavailable in the unliganded state, meaning that the antagonist creates a state that would not otherwise be observed?

**Steven James C. Salvador:** In the antagonist-bound state, helix 12 is oriented away from the main body of the protein, whereas the agonist-bound state has helix 12 in a closed conformation. The conformation changes the receptor surface, including the AF-2 region involved in coactivator recruitment and androgen-responsive transcription.

**Audience member:** I was wondering whether an unliganded structure is needed to establish that the antagonist will actually bind.

**Steven James C. Salvador:** Yes. Thank you.
