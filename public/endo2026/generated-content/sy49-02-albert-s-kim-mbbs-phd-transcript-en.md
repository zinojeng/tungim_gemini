# Understanding the Pathophysiology of the Post-Denosumab Overshoot: Mechanistic Insights

**Speaker:** Albert S. Kim, MBBS, PhD  
**Conference:** ENDO 2026  
**Session:** Bouncing Back from Denosumab Discontinuation  
**Reviewed source interval:** 26:30–48:45

**Albert S. Kim:** Thank you very much for the wonderful introduction, and thank you to the Endocrine Society for the invitation to speak. Thank you to the organizers and to all the delegates for making this such a successful and enjoyable meeting. I am an adult endocrinologist, but I also have preclinical experience exploring this phenomenon.

We have heard a great deal about what happens. Denosumab is a fantastic medication: patients gain bone while receiving it, but lose bone when it is stopped. Bone turnover is suppressed during treatment, followed by a rise and overshoot after the treatment effect wears off. We know from observational studies, our own experience, and excellent prospective studies such as the ZOLARMAB study from Dr. Langdahl's group that this is a difficult problem to solve. Despite different timings and triggers for intervention with sequential bisphosphonate therapy, BMD loss occurs, and multiple doses of zoledronate are often required.

We therefore know that denosumab discontinuation causes rebound loss of bone mineral density and an overshoot in bone turnover, and we know who is at risk. My talk will focus on why this happens and what preclinical studies tell us about the underlying mechanisms. What causes the phenomenon? Why do current treatment strategies not work consistently? What does it teach us about bone biology more broadly?

## RANKL, OPG, and challenges in human studies

First, a 30-second orientation to bone biology. RANK ligand, or RANKL, is made by cells of the osteoblast lineage. It promotes osteoclast differentiation and formation, so high RANKL produces increased bone resorption. Osteoprotegerin, or OPG, is the brake. It is a decoy receptor secreted by osteoblast-lineage cells and newly embedded osteocytes. By binding RANKL, it stops osteoclast formation, with a net result of increased bone formation. The discovery of the RANKL–OPG pathway and the development of denosumab form a lovely story. Denosumab mimics the action of OPG by applying a brake to osteoclast formation, thereby promoting bone gain and preventing fractures.

Mechanistic studies of this phenomenon in humans are difficult. An ideal study would keep people on denosumab, collect blood regularly, biopsy their bones, and then allow them to continue losing bone and sustain fractures after treatment stopped. That would not be ethical. We therefore rely heavily on observational data. This has led to empirical approaches to sequential therapy in which we wait to detect changes on DXA after bone has already been lost. CTX is a product of collagen degradation and is released after bone has already been resorbed. Strategies based on BMD loss and conventional bone-turnover markers are therefore reactive. Perhaps we should look at what happens before bone loss occurs, just as an endocrinologist treating hyperglycaemia looks at the events that preceded it.

## Four preclinical models

There are inherent problems in modelling this human condition in mice because denosumab does not interact with murine RANKL. It is like having the wrong key for the lock. Different laboratories have therefore either changed the key or changed the lock. The key can be changed by using OPG:Fc or a murine-specific anti-RANKL antibody. Alternatively, the murine RANKL target can be changed so that denosumab binds it.

I will describe four confirmatory models now published in the literature:

1. Our OPG:Fc model uses OPG:Fc to bind RANKL in wild-type mice.
2. A Japanese group uses OYC1, a murine anti-RANKL antibody, in wild-type mice.
3. The humanized RANKL, or hRANKL, model from Dr. Charles O'Brien's laboratory in Arkansas uses CRISPR to replace the denosumab-binding epitope so that denosumab can bind murine RANKL.
4. The transgenic RANKL, or TgRANKL, model from Dr. Eleni Douni's laboratory in Greece uses denosumab in mice expressing human RANKL.

All four models consistently show that bone mass increases during treatment and is lost when the treatment effect wanes. The OYC1 model shows marked mineralization and a gain in bone mass that is lost after discontinuation. The hRANKL and TgRANKL models show the same pattern in BMD.

The turnover findings also reproduce what we see in humans. Bone-turnover markers are suppressed during treatment and rise above baseline after treatment stops. In the OPG:Fc model, TRAP-5b, an enzymatic marker of osteoclast number, is suppressed and then overshoots. TRAP overshoots in the OYC1 model, CTX overshoots in hRANKL mice, and turnover likewise overshoots in TgRANKL mice.

The remainder of the talk considers what these models reveal beyond BMD and bone-turnover markers. I have divided the process into four phases: active treatment; the inflection point after treatment discontinuation, when bone loss is imminent; rapid bone resorption with a turnover overshoot; and the period after BMD and turnover have returned to baseline.

## Osteoclast precursors and osteomorphs during treatment

Work with OPG:Fc by my mentor, Dr. Michelle McDonald, showed that osteoclasts have an alternative cell fate: they can undergo fission into osteomorphs. RANKL inhibition, as occurs with denosumab, prevents these osteomorphs from fusing back into osteoclasts, potentially causing osteomorphs to accumulate during treatment. At the same time, osteoclast differentiation is arrested, causing osteoclast precursors to accumulate. When treatment is discontinued and RANKL surges, these primed pools of precursors and osteomorphs are free to form osteoclasts that can readily resorb bone, producing rebound bone loss and an overshoot in bone turnover.

The OYC1 model supports this concept. Two weeks after the final dose of the murine anti-RANKL antibody, marrow from treated mice formed far more osteoclasts than marrow from control mice. This occurred well before bone loss and turnover overshoot, while TRAP remained suppressed and bone mass was still increasing.

Flow cytometry was used to identify the contributing populations. CD11b-negative, c-Fms-positive, c-Kit-positive cells and CD11b-negative, c-Fms-positive, Ly6C-high cells—populations associated with increased osteoclastogenic potential—were expanded at the end of treatment, again well before bone loss and turnover overshoot. The precursor pool was therefore present and ready to differentiate as soon as the brake was released.

The hRANKL model did not show the same finding, perhaps because of differences in the models and their physiology. Two weeks after the last dose of denosumab, marrow from those mice formed fewer osteoclasts, and expression of *Acp5*, which encodes TRAP, was unchanged.

Human precursor data are still more complicated, and they concern circulating blood rather than marrow. Dr. Marian Schini and colleagues found higher numbers of CD14-positive, CD11b-positive cells classified as osteoclast precursors in postmenopausal women receiving denosumab. Kaneko and colleagues, however, found fewer CD14-positive, RANK-high cells. These variable results probably reflect different collection times, the comparison of mouse marrow with human peripheral blood, and different flow-cytometric definitions of an osteoclast precursor.

This leaves three open questions. What exactly is an osteoclast precursor? Can the precursor accumulation seen in mice be replicated in humans? How do precursor populations change over time when denosumab is started and after it is discontinued?

## The RANKL-to-OPG signalling shift

The second part of the active-treatment phase asks what drives osteoclast formation after RANKL inhibition stops. In our OPG:Fc model, we collaborated with Dr. Thomas Andersen in Denmark and used spatial transcriptomics. At the end of OPG:Fc treatment, RANKL expression was increased on trabecular bone surfaces, while OPG expression was reduced in trabecular and cortical bone-surface cells.

At a later time point, just before bone loss, qPCR of marrow-depleted femora showed increased *Rankl* expression without a significant change in *Opg*. The net result was a large shift in the RANKL-to-OPG ratio toward osteoclastogenesis. Serum RANKL was also significantly higher both at the end of treatment and when the rise in TRAP and subsequent bone loss were imminent. Thus, the RANKL signal changes before BMD loss and the turnover overshoot.

Extracellular vesicles, or EVs, have recently attracted increasing attention in health and disease. In the OYC1 model, RANKL was present in apoptotic bodies, microvesicles, and exosomes, and these RANKL-bearing vesicles accumulated during treatment. EVs may therefore contribute to the origin of increased RANKL during RANKL inhibition and the post-denosumab period.

On the OPG side, the hRANKL model showed reduced OPG expression over bone surfaces, a fourfold reduction in the OPG signal in cancellous bone, and approximately 50% fewer OPG-expressing osteocytes after denosumab. Putting these findings together, RANKL inhibition is accompanied by increased RANKL expression on bone surfaces, accumulation of RANKL-bearing EVs, reduced OPG expression on bone surfaces, and suppressed OPG expression in osteocytes. The result is a massive pro-osteoclastogenic signal capable of producing an overshoot rather than a simple return to baseline.

## TRAP rises before BMD loss

What happens at the inflection point, and what might predict impending bone loss? In the OPG:Fc model, we tracked BMD longitudinally by DXA. Because mice have a small circulating blood volume, we selected cross-sectional time points at which to obtain enough blood for contemporaneous measurements of TRAP, CTX, and P1NP.

The mice gained bone during treatment and began losing it from week 10 onward as the treatment effect wore off. Importantly, TRAP began to rise between weeks 8 and 10, before BMD loss. We then compared TRAP with the clinically familiar markers CTX and P1NP. At week 2, at the end of OPG:Fc treatment, and at week 8, while its effect remained active, all three markers were suppressed relative to vehicle-treated mice. By week 11, when mice were actively losing bone, TRAP had overshot, whereas CTX and P1NP had only returned to vehicle levels. It was not until week 13, when BMD loss was complete, that TRAP, CTX, and P1NP all showed an overshoot. TRAP therefore rose before CTX and P1NP and before measurable BMD loss.

## Prolonged osteoblast suppression and sclerostin

Is the later phase simply a return to the pretreatment baseline? The OYC1 model suggests that it is not. Long after bone loss and turnover overshoot had ended, marrow from discontinuation mice still had a reduced ability to form osteoblasts. There were fewer CFU-ALP colonies and fewer Alizarin Red-positive, mineralized CFU-Ob colonies. Their femora had reduced mineralization and were more susceptible to fracture under the same load.

Sclerostin is also part of the story. After OYC1 treatment, *Sost* expression was significantly higher, and a greater proportion of osteocytes expressed sclerostin. The same was demonstrated after denosumab in the hRANKL model, with higher *Sost* expression in bone and more sclerostin-positive osteocytes. Persistently high sclerostin may prevent anabolic recovery after denosumab discontinuation.

The preclinical models therefore show delayed resumption of osteoblast-mediated bone formation, with sclerostin continuing to act as a brake. We still need to establish what happens in humans, because P1NP, a marker of bone formation, also overshoots, so substantial bone formation is clearly occurring. What are human osteoblasts doing during the overshoot? Could osteoblast-directed therapy prevent post-denosumab bone loss? Could we target persistent *Sost* overexpression?

## A fire as the conceptual framework

As a clinician who recently moved to the bench, I find that simple analogies help me understand complex physiology. During denosumab treatment, osteoclast differentiation stops, but kindling accumulates. Osteoclast precursors build up and osteoclasts undergo fission into osteomorphs, adding fuel. RANKL expression begins to increase at a time when CTX and P1NP remain suppressed and BMD is rising.

As treatment continues, more precursors and osteomorphs accumulate. This fits the clinical observation that longer exposure is associated with a greater risk of bone loss and fractures after discontinuation. RANKL-bearing EVs and increased RANKL expression add further fuel, while OPG expression falls. Nevertheless, CTX and P1NP remain suppressed and BMD continues to rise, preventing fractures while denosumab is active.

When denosumab is stopped, the fire is lit. Osteoclasts begin to re-form in a RANKL-rich environment. The RANKL-to-OPG ratio remains elevated, TRAP starts to rise, BMD has peaked, and bone loss is imminent. Newly formed osteoclasts then resorb bone rapidly. TRAP, CTX, and P1NP overshoot, and BMD is lost quickly.

After the fire, BMD and turnover normalize, but elevated sclerostin continues to suppress bone formation. TRAP, CTX, P1NP, and BMD may all have returned to pretreatment levels, yet the underlying biology is not necessarily normal. Are we waiting to see smoke and ashes before trying to put out the fire?

## Earlier sequential therapy

Current approaches often administer sequential therapy routinely six months after the final denosumab dose, following its six-month treatment cycle, or wait until CTX and P1NP rise and BMD has already been lost. A raging fire may require repeated treatment. Could a smaller fire be easier to extinguish by intervening while TRAP is rising and osteoclasts are forming, before bone loss occurs?

We modelled this in four groups of OPG:Fc-treated mice. The first zoledronate dose was given while turnover was markedly suppressed and BMD was still rising. One group received a second zoledronate dose at the time when rebound bone loss was anticipated. We measured TRAP as an indicator of osteoclast number and followed BMD by DXA.

BMD peaked and was lost from week 10 onward in mice that received OPG:Fc alone. Early zoledronate attenuated BMD loss, leaving BMD significantly higher at the end of the study, and a single early dose prevented the TRAP overshoot. A second dose consolidated the BMD gain, and TRAP never rose above vehicle levels. Thus, early and repeated zoledronate prevented the TRAP overshoot and mitigated rebound BMD loss in this model.

Could sclerostin also be targeted? Dr. O'Brien's laboratory used the hRANKL model to administer romosozumab after denosumab but before BMD loss and the TRAP overshoot. Romosozumab produced further BMD gains while TRAP remained suppressed. One intriguing feature of this study was the early timing of romosozumab after denosumab; perhaps the effect relates partly to the timing of sequential therapy, although this requires further study.

## Conclusions

The post-denosumab phenomenon is probably more than rebound BMD loss and an overshoot in turnover. The mouse models identify several multicellular drivers: accumulation of osteoclast precursors and osteomorphs, a shift in the RANKL-to-OPG ratio, and prolonged abnormalities of osteoblasts and sclerostin. Together, these changes contribute to bone loss and fractures.

This is a rapidly developing field. Additional proposed mechanisms, including the roles of bone-marrow adipose tissue and macrophage polarization, have emerged in recent months but could not be covered today. The central message is that preclinical findings provide a framework for approaching this complex and urgent clinical problem. Mice are not humans, but clinical studies of denosumab discontinuation and sequential therapy should incorporate these mechanistic findings and determine whether they also occur in people.

Mechanisms matter. Fundamental discoveries still drive clinical progress.
