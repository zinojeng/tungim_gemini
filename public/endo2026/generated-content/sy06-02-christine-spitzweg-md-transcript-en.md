### RET fusions in radiation-associated and pediatric thyroid cancer

It was soon recognized, particularly through the study of papillary thyroid cancers occurring after the Chernobyl nuclear accident, that RET fusions are highly prevalent in radiation-associated papillary thyroid cancer. This was first reported by Yuri Nikiforov and then confirmed by others. An analysis from Jim Fagin's group of 26 PTC samples from Ukrainian children who developed PTC after the Chernobyl accident showed a very high prevalence of fusions: 22 of 26 samples harbored fusions, mostly RET fusions, with a few other fusions. In contrast, a control group of sporadic papillary thyroid cancers had a low prevalence of RET fusions and a much higher prevalence of BRAF V600E.

And the molecular mechanism of this, how irradiation increases the prevalence of RET fusions, is thought to be a mistake in the repair of DNA double-strand breaks that are caused by irradiation and that are repaired by non-homologous misjoining of genes, causing thereby the high prevalence of RET fusions in these tumors.

And RET fusions are not only very prevalent in pediatric papillary thyroid cancer caused by irradiation, but also generally much higher in pediatric papillary thyroid cancer. That is nicely shown here in this broad genomic analysis of 348 pediatric papillary thyroid cancers. 83% of them show genetic alterations and 58% fusions, and mostly RET fusions.

In pediatric papillary thyroid cancer, RET fusions are associated with a more aggressive disease course, including higher risks of invasive behavior and regional and distant metastases. This was also shown in a retrospective analysis by the Child and Adolescent Thyroid Consortium (CATC) of 715 children diagnosed with differentiated thyroid cancer, 94% of whom had papillary thyroid cancer. Oncogenic drivers were identified in 306 tumors, and 23% of the overall cohort had oncogenic fusions. Within the molecularly characterized PTC subgroup, more than 50% harbored oncogenic fusions, mostly RET fusions.

And especially the younger the children, the higher the prevalence of the RET fusions, with 100% in children younger than nine years.

And the RET fusions are strongly associated with TNM stage, with extrathyroidal extension, and again high frequency in post-radiation papillary thyroid cancer.

So this association with more aggressive disease is more controversial in adult papillary thyroid cancer. Also, as we know from this comprehensive genomic analysis of 402 papillary thyroid cancers within the Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network, we know that BRAF V600E mutations are the most prevalent genetic alterations in these tumors. Followed by RAS mutations, and also RET fusions can be found in about 15% of patients, primarily RET fusions, but also other fusions like TRK fusions and ALK fusions.

But the association, or the association with a more aggressive tumor course of disease, is not that clear in adult RET fusion-positive differentiated thyroid cancer.

RET fusions are key oncogenic drivers in the genomic landscape of differentiated and poorly differentiated thyroid cancer. They are generally mutually exclusive with other oncogenic drivers, including BRAF and RAS mutations, and contribute to radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer through increased MAPK output and reduced expression of thyroid-differentiation genes.

### Multikinase and selective RET inhibitors

This is why, as Mimi already pointed out, multikinase-inhibitor trials in both medullary thyroid cancer and radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer selected TKIs with anti-RET activity: sorafenib, lenvatinib, vandetanib in a phase II trial, and cabozantinib. The therapeutic efficacy of these agents led to approval of sorafenib and lenvatinib, and of cabozantinib in the second-line setting, for radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, although with dose-limiting toxicities. The subgroup of RET fusion-positive differentiated thyroid cancers was not analyzed separately in those trials, so we do not know how effective multikinase inhibitors are specifically in that group. In contrast, selective RET-inhibitor trials with pralsetinib and selpercatinib revolutionized treatment not only for advanced medullary thyroid cancer but also for advanced RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer. RET fusion-positive thyroid tumors were included in the phase I/II LIBRETTO-001 trial of selpercatinib and the ARROW trial of pralsetinib.

And here, in the first analysis of the LIBRETTO trial, including 19 patients with RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer, all pretreated patients at this point showed a very good objective response of 79%, with complete responses, partial responses, and durable responses across all histologic subtypes: papillary thyroid cancer, poorly differentiated, even one anaplastic thyroid cancer, and also an oncocytic thyroid cancer, and irrespective of the type of RET fusion.

This led to FDA and EMA authorization of selpercatinib for RET-altered thyroid cancers, including RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer.

In the follow-up analysis of the phase I/II LIBRETTO-001 trial, there were two groups with RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer: treatment-naive patients and, as in the initial analysis, patients previously treated with other systemic therapies. Both groups responded dramatically to selpercatinib, with a superior effect in the treatment-naive group: an objective response rate of almost 100%—95.8%—versus 85.4% in previously treated patients, with durable responses and a very manageable safety profile, as we already heard from Mimi.

Similar results were reported in the ARROW trial of pralsetinib, which included a cohort with RET fusion-positive tumors, including RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer. In the initial analysis, nine patients with RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer had all received prior systemic therapy; the objective response rate with pralsetinib was 89%. Efficacy was observed irrespective of the RET fusion partner, with durable responses, leading to FDA authorization of pralsetinib for RET-altered thyroid cancer.

This drug has actually never been approved in Europe because of what Mimi has already pointed out.

In a follow-up analysis of the phase I/II ARROW trial, the RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer subgroup had an objective response rate of 90.9%, again with durable responses.

Very recently at ASCO, just a few weeks ago, the final results of the phase I/II ARROW trial were presented, including the cohort with RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer. Among 24 previously treated patients, the objective response rate was 91.7%, comparable to what we see with selpercatinib.

Also presented at ASCO were results of the phase II TAPISTRY basket trial in oncogene-driven cancers treated with selective inhibitors. The subgroup with RET fusion-positive thyroid cancer treated with pralsetinib had an objective response rate of 81%, with durable responses.

### Redifferentiation and restoration of radioiodine uptake

Selective RET inhibitors are therefore a valuable addition to targeted therapies for oncogene-driven thyroid cancer, including in the context of redifferentiation therapy. Redifferentiation aims to reinduce NIS expression and radioiodine uptake in tumors that have lost that activity. The oncogenic drivers I have mentioned, including RET fusions, stimulate the central pathway involved in downregulating NIS—the BRAF–MEK–ERK pathway—resulting in loss of NIS expression and loss of response to radioiodine. Radioiodine, the first theranostic agent, was first used in 1941, 85 years ago, at Massachusetts General Hospital by Saul Hertz.

In transgenic RET/PTC1 mouse models, tumors expressing RET/PTC1 lose NIS expression. This significant downregulation was confirmed by Jim Fagin's group in thyroid cells transduced with RET/PTC3, which lost NIS expression and showed decreased expression of other thyroid-differentiation genes, including thyroglobulin and PAX8.

This results from substantial stimulation of MAPK signaling by fusions, particularly RET fusions. The MAPK output is lower than that seen with BRAF V600E but generally higher than that seen with RAS mutations.

For all of these oncogenic drivers, targeting strategies are available and have been evaluated for their ability to reinduce NIS expression and radioiodine accumulation in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer.

With the availability of selective RET inhibitors, specifically targeting RET fusion-induced MAPK signaling is a very attractive therapeutic strategy.

RET/PTC-positive tumors were included in the first clinical redifferentiation trial at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center using selumetinib, a MEK inhibitor. Of three patients with RET/PTC fusions, two showed reinduction of radioiodine uptake; one reached the dosimetric threshold for therapeutic radioiodine and had a dramatic response.

And since availability of selective RET inhibitors, several case reports have been published and case series in patients with RET fusion-positive papillary thyroid carcinoma.

The first published case involved a patient with an NCOA4::RET fusion-positive papillary thyroid cancer with disseminated metastases.

### Conclusions

RET fusions are key actionable oncogenic drivers in radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer. This highlights the need for early, broad molecular screening to identify patients eligible for first-line selective RET inhibitors, which offer superior therapeutic efficacy and a more manageable safety profile than approved multikinase inhibitors. Selective RET inhibitors can also be considered for redifferentiation, ideally within clinical trials.

Earlier use of selective RET inhibitors during the disease course—in the setting of adjuvant radioiodine treatment in radioiodine-naive patients or in new neoadjuvant approaches—must be discussed individually by an expert multidisciplinary team and defined further in clinical trials. Because this disease is so rare, that work requires international, multicenter collaboration, which ITOG helps catalyze.
