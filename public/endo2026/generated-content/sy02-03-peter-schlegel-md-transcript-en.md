## Introduction and nonobstructive azoospermia

**Peter Schlegel:** Thank you for the opportunity to speak about some of our experience with the management of Klinefelter syndrome. Here are my disclosures. I will discuss some off-label uses, but none of the companies with which I have worked has products related to this presentation.

We have had a longstanding focus on the treatment of nonobstructive azoospermia, or NOA—sperm production so impaired that no sperm appear in the ejaculate. Phenotypically, these men typically have small testicular volume and markedly elevated follicle-stimulating hormone, or FSH. In young, healthy men, FSH should be less than 8 IU/L; the mean FSH in the patients whom we treat is about 25 IU/L.

We have viewed patients with Klinefelter syndrome as a common paradigm for NOA. Klinefelter syndrome is present in approximately 1 in 660 live births. The typical karyotype is nonmosaic 47,XXY, although mosaicism is also possible. Nearly all—but not all—men with nonmosaic Klinefelter syndrome are azoospermic. Many are never diagnosed during their lifetime, and the phenotypic presentation can be somewhat variable.

We know that Klinefelter syndrome has metabolic, hormonal, and neoplastic effects, as well as neurodevelopmental issues that may be particularly important during adolescence. Our focus today is fertility and fertility preservation.

## Why Klinefelter syndrome is an informative model

We focused on Klinefelter syndrome because it represents a distinct subset of men with NOA and because the testicular phenotype is relatively consistent. Circulating testosterone levels are usually abnormal. The mean total testosterone in the cohort we see is 150 ng/dL, despite suggestions that intratesticular testosterone may be normal or even relatively high.

We thought this could serve as a model for the treatment of other men with NOA. Klinefelter syndrome is a recognized cause of severe testicular dysfunction, and descriptive information is available. Characteristic sclerotic changes in testicular tissue are commonly described on histopathology.

There is relative Leydig-cell hyperplasia, but this largely reflects having a normal number of Leydig cells in a much smaller testis, which creates the appearance of relative hyperplasia or clustering of Leydig cells. We think this is part of the pathology.

Germ cells have been described as being lost early in puberty. We have also shown that sperm retrieval followed by intracytoplasmic sperm injection, or ICSI, can provide fertility for adult patients. Unfortunately, sperm-retrieval outcomes vary greatly among centers in the United States and around the world.

The major change occurs around the beginning of puberty, when sclerosis of the seminiferous tubules increases. The typical adult pattern includes Leydig-cell hyperplasia and sclerotic seminiferous tubules.

## Testosterone treatment and fertility concerns

Testosterone is often used in patients with Klinefelter syndrome to enhance neurobehavioral features and induce puberty. We were concerned that exogenous testosterone could suppress sperm production. This occurs through gonadotropin suppression, particularly with injectable testosterone.

Early in our experience, we also noticed a substantially lower sperm-retrieval rate among men with a history of prior testosterone therapy. This suggested that intratesticular testosterone was important for fertility and led us to consider how endogenous intratesticular testosterone production might be increased.

Low circulating testosterone can result from low production or excessive metabolism, particularly conversion to estradiol through aromatization. We began quantifying aromatization by examining testosterone-to-estradiol, or T/E₂, ratios.

Abnormal T/E₂ ratios occur with aging and testicular failure and are also seen in men with Klinefelter syndrome. In a preliminary study comparing men with normal fertility and sperm production with men who had NOA or testicular failure, we saw a dramatic difference in T/E₂ ratios. A cutoff of approximately 10 was useful when testosterone was measured in ng/dL and estradiol in pg/mL. Patients with Klinefelter syndrome typically had a T/E₂ ratio of approximately 4.

## Aromatase inhibition and empiric hormonal therapy

We sought ways to treat these patients. Initially, testolactone was the only available aromatase inhibitor, although it is no longer available. We subsequently moved to anastrozole and found it effective. With anastrozole monotherapy, however, we commonly observed tachyphylaxis, with a decrease in the testosterone response within two to three months, as reported in a number of publications.

We also considered clomiphene, although this made limited conceptual sense because luteinizing hormone, or LH, and FSH are already high in most patients with Klinefelter syndrome.

Empirically, we found that the combination of human chorionic gonadotropin, or hCG, and anastrozole is quite effective in patients with Klinefelter syndrome, including those who are severely hypogonadal—for example, those with serum testosterone below 100 ng/dL.

We focused on Leydig cells because of their structural arrangement within the Klinefelter testis and because they are a primary testicular source of aromatase. We were concerned about increased aromatase activity with hCG administration. The physical arrangement of Leydig cells resembles what we see in Leydig-cell tumors, in which a testosterone-producing cell aromatizes testosterone to estradiol and may produce feminizing effects in men.

The physiological role of estrogen is important. Estrogen participates in fluid reabsorption as fluid leaves the testis and enters the epididymis. Eliminating all estrogen is therefore not beneficial. Estrogen deficiency can also severely affect libido, and its adverse effects are particularly pronounced in rodents because of the large volume of fluid leaving the rodent testis.

Excess estrogen also has adverse effects. Fibrosis is seen in the testes of patients with prostate cancer treated with estrogens. Clinically, however, we can see a response to aromatase inhibitors.

What hormonal treatment should we use? These patients have low testosterone and abnormal conversion of testosterone to estradiol. We asked whether medical therapy could improve sperm production, particularly before surgical sperm retrieval.

We initially treated a series of oligospermic patients with low testosterone and abnormal T/E₂ ratios. Testolactone treatment was associated with an increase in total motile sperm count. Similarly, anastrozole treatment improved semen parameters, including sperm concentration and, particularly, total motile sperm count per ejaculate.

Unfortunately, anastrozole monotherapy is not routinely effective in patients with Klinefelter syndrome. Many have an almost paradoxical response, with a drop in testosterone during anastrozole monotherapy.

Our usual empiric approach in men with NOA and low serum testosterone is first to rule out hyperprolactinemia or significant pituitary abnormalities. For men with serum testosterone below 300 ng/dL, we typically use anastrozole monotherapy if the T/E₂ ratio is below 10. We use clomiphene or tamoxifen if the T/E₂ ratio is above 10. Patients with Klinefelter syndrome often respond best to hCG plus anastrozole.

As previous speakers noted, testosterone mainly acts on the later stages of spermatogenesis, so a full three-month course may not be necessary. Two months of treatment may be sufficient. We follow the T/E₂ ratio and serum testosterone during treatment, and we always repeat semen analysis in azoospermic patients on the day before a planned sperm-retrieval procedure.

These observational data outline the response. Baseline testosterone did not differ between men in whom sperm were found and those in whom no sperm were found. Baseline serum testosterone therefore did not predict sperm retrieval in patients with Klinefelter syndrome.

Among patients receiving medical therapy, those who had a hormonal response were more likely to have sperm found than those who did not respond as well. It remains unclear whether this simply selects patients with somewhat better testicular function, who therefore respond to medical therapy, or whether medical therapy actually induces sperm production.

## Development of microdissection testicular sperm extraction

How do we treat patients with NOA? A normal testis is usually approximately 4.5 cm long. A typical patient with NOA may have a testis only 1.5 cm long.

Initially, we performed a series of biopsies and sampled tissue from different sites. It was very difficult to predict when or whether we would find sperm, and we sometimes performed many biopsies. That is particularly problematic because the testicular blood supply runs circumferentially around the testis. Multiple biopsies therefore risk devascularizing the testis.

We began using an operating microscope to avoid those vessels. We observed that functioning seminiferous tubules could be distinguished from the background tubules, which usually had a Sertoli-cell-only pattern. The operating microscope therefore allowed us to identify potential sites of sperm production within the testis.

As has been described for centuries, the seminiferous tubules run parallel to the testicular blood supply. We can therefore dissect and examine many areas without substantially damaging testicular tissue or removing large volumes of tissue.

This procedure is called microdissection testicular sperm extraction, or micro-TESE. Comparative studies show that it is superior to conventional TESE, which uses multiple biopsies, and to testicular sperm aspiration, or TESA, in men with NOA.

Schematically, the procedure begins by entering through a relatively avascular plane to open the testis. We then examine and dissect the testicular tissue under approximately 10× to 15× magnification. Some manipulation is necessary to examine deeper areas rather than only the superficial surface.

There are hundreds of seminiferous tubules, each of which may function autonomously, and heterogeneous sperm production is common. Areas with better sperm production are identified by tubules that are larger and more opaque. Not surprisingly, those tubules contain more cells and are more likely to contain sperm. Small pieces of those tubules can be removed.

This is a real-time view of the procedure. Although the testis is quite vascular, it can be opened through a relatively avascular plane. We then dissect through the tissue, following planes in which the tubules run parallel to the vessels.

The greatest risk of any testicular procedure is leaving bleeding behind. Blind biopsies can create far more scar tissue than this more extensive but microscopically controlled dissection. When bipolar cautery is used to limit thermal damage, we can examine large areas safely.

Here, one very small segment of a single tubule contained sperm. This tiny site had thousands of sperm that could be used, and it was the only productive site in that testis. Because sperm are located within the tissue, special processing is required to identify the rare sperm that are present.

## Coordinating sperm retrieval with IVF

We prefer to coordinate sperm retrieval with in vitro fertilization, or IVF. I repeat the semen analysis on the day before surgery because up to 10% of patients whom we thought were azoospermic will have rare sperm in the ejaculate, eliminating the need for surgery.

Intraoperative examination of tissue directs the retrieval. When we have enough sperm, we stop. Microdissection sperm retrieval is complex and may take hours, even though the testes of patients with Klinefelter syndrome may have a volume of less than 2 mL. The productive foci may be only 1–2 mm in size and often consist of very small segments of otherwise sclerotic tubules.

We usually perform sperm retrieval one to three days before oocyte retrieval. We coordinate the treatments because testicular sperm do not always survive freezing and thawing.

The usual measures of testicular function, such as FSH, do not predict the likelihood of sperm retrieval. A patient needs only a single functioning focus, which may not change the serum FSH level. The mechanical search is important: tissue deep within the testis can contain sperm even when tissue at the surface does not.

Sperm from men with NOA differ from ejaculated sperm. They have abnormal morphology, and their fertilization rate with ICSI is only approximately 45%, compared with the approximately 75% rate we usually see. The appearance of the sperm does not predict ICSI success, but the lower fertilization rate can be limiting. A couple may have very few oocytes fertilize and very few embryos available. Repeat sperm retrieval is possible, but frozen sperm do not always survive freeze–thaw.

## Chromosome findings and offspring

Historically, it was thought that only 46,XY spermatogonia were functional and that all men with Klinefelter syndrome who had spermatogenesis must have some form of mosaicism. Direct evidence, however, indicates that functional 47,XXY spermatogonia exist.

The increased presence of hyperhaploid sperm within the testes of patients with Klinefelter syndrome, together with the absence of hypohaploid sperm, strongly suggests that 47,XXY spermatogonia produce sperm. Men with Klinefelter syndrome may produce 24,XY; 24,XX; 23,X; or 23,Y sperm. Preimplantation genetic testing may therefore be considered.

By random chance, we would predict that 50% of sperm produced by 47,XXY spermatogonia would be hyperhaploid. Hyperhaploidy is seen in approximately 10% of sperm within the testis. There appears to be natural selection favoring euploid 23,X or 23,Y sperm during fertilization: only approximately 5% of embryos have an extra X chromosome, and fewer than 1% of ongoing pregnancies are 47,XXY or 47,XXX. There is therefore only a limited rationale for preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy, or PGT-A, although embryo testing is possible.

For patients with Klinefelter syndrome, sperm are a limited resource. Up to 70% of men have sperm in the testis despite having none in the ejaculate. The low fertilization rate, even with ICSI, may result in few embryos for transfer. We often transfer embryos on day 3 at the cleavage stage rather than waiting for the day-5 blastocyst stage commonly used in IVF.

At our center, sperm were found during the first micro-TESE procedure in 68% of men with Klinefelter syndrome. The clinical-pregnancy rate on the first IVF attempt was 43%, and additional embryos may have been available from that treatment.

## Fertility preservation in adolescents

How do we translate this adult information into the management of adolescents? Biopsy studies suggest that sclerosis develops within the testis and limits sperm production, which raises the possibility of early fertility preservation or optimization.

We ask adolescents to provide a semen sample for analysis and cryopreservation. Our detection rate for sperm in the ejaculate appears to be higher than in adults.

What about early surgical sperm retrieval and freezing? Could we retrieve sperm before germ cells are lost and the testes become sclerotic? This might appear to offer an opportunity to preserve future fertility. Important limitations are that testicular sperm do not survive freeze–thaw reliably and pregnancy rates with frozen–thawed testicular sperm tend to be low.

One group reported sperm-retrieval rates of up to 70% in adolescents after treatment with testosterone gel and an aromatase inhibitor. However, our retrieval rate in adults was already 68%.

We therefore retrospectively examined whether sperm retrieval during adolescence differed from retrieval at older ages. These are retrospective data rather than an interventional comparison. Men younger than 20 actually had a lower sperm-retrieval rate than men aged 20–29, and even than men in their 30s. The rate appeared to decline again among older men.

These data suggest that intervening during adolescence does not necessarily provide a better sperm-retrieval rate than waiting until the patient is in his 20s or possibly his 30s.

This has led us to manage adolescents by optimizing endogenous testosterone production and attempting cryopreservation of ejaculated sperm when possible. For adolescents who require testosterone therapy, we consider unilateral sperm retrieval and cryopreservation before exogenous testosterone—particularly injectable testosterone—while recognizing the limitations I have described.

Our current impression and recommendation is that routine early surgical sperm retrieval is not required in Klinefelter syndrome.

## Summary

Klinefelter syndrome accounts for approximately 10% of the NOA cases that we treat. Sperm retrieval and fertility in adults are common despite azoospermia. Medical therapy is associated with better sperm-retrieval outcomes, although causality is unclear. Surgical sperm retrieval and IVF are usually required. Coordinating IVF and sperm retrieval is complex, and outcomes vary.

For adolescents with Klinefelter syndrome, assess semen early and avoid exogenous testosterone unless there is a clear indication. If testosterone therapy is planned, consider upfront testicular sperm retrieval and cryopreservation. For most patients with Klinefelter syndrome, however, we believe fertility is best managed during adulthood.

Thank you.

**Peter Schlegel:** Especially if you are using injectable testosterone. There are several formulations of testosterone that may not have the same adverse effects on gonadotropins. With testosterone gel, for example, some of those effects can be mitigated with anastrozole co-therapy.

If injectable testosterone is used, we recommend sperm retrieval from at least one side before treatment because of our poor rates of subsequent fertility treatment in those individuals after testosterone exposure.

We know that a surprisingly large proportion of younger men—perhaps 10%–15%—have sperm in the ejaculate. They are severely oligospermic, but sperm are still present.

One structural issue that we observe in testes from patients with Klinefelter syndrome is a pocket of spermatogenesis with sclerotic tissue on both sides. There is essentially no path for those sperm to exit. Could some younger men have productive pockets close enough to the outflow tract that sperm appear early, after which sclerosis develops and blocks the path? That is one possible theory.
