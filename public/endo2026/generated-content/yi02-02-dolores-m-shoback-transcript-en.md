## FNIH-ASBMR SABRE project

The FNIH-ASBMR SABRE project was a public-private partnership. SABRE stands for the Study to Advance Bone Mineral Density as a Regulatory Endpoint.

There are major gaps in osteoporosis care. Only about 20% to 30% of fracture patients who should be treated receive treatment. Among patients who start therapy, fewer than 50% continue it, creating an adherence gap. Perhaps the most important problem is the innovation gap: few new drugs are being developed because the trials required for approval are long, costly, and powered for fracture outcomes. SABRE therefore sought to validate new trial endpoints and to qualify BMD as an endpoint for osteoporosis drug development.

The 21st Century Cures Act created a pathway to qualify surrogate endpoints as drug-development tools. A surrogate endpoint is a trial endpoint, usually a biomarker, that substitutes for direct measurement of the clinical endpoint of interest. Total hip BMD measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is an excellent candidate. The measurement is standardized and precise, correlates strongly with bone strength in biomechanical testing, and predicts fracture risk. Treatment-related effects on total hip BMD are also strongly associated with the clinical outcome that matters: fractures.

The initial SABRE analysis used individual patient-level data from 52 randomized trials involving more than 150,000 participants and 15 drugs. It showed that treatment-related differences in percentage change in total hip BMD could predict treatment effects on three fracture endpoints initially and later on five endpoints.

The surrogate threshold effect (STE) is the minimum between-treatment difference—active drug versus placebo—in mean percentage change in total hip BMD that predicts a statistically significant reduction in fracture risk in a trial. The 24-month active-minus-placebo difference in total hip BMD was associated with the odds ratio or hazard ratio for vertebral, hip, and nonvertebral fractures. The greater the BMD difference, the larger the fracture-risk reduction. The estimated STE was 1.42% for vertebral fracture, 3.18% for hip fracture, and 2.13% for nonvertebral fracture.

New work during the past year analyzed patient-level data from 22 trials and further validated total hip BMD and the threshold effects across different trial designs and drug mechanisms, including antiresorptive therapy alone and anabolic therapy followed by antiresorptive therapy. The validated STE was 1.43% for vertebral fracture and 2.04% for all clinical fractures. This generalizability is important.

The investigators also estimated the sample size needed to use the surrogate endpoint successfully. They performed Monte Carlo resampling of the data set 1,000 times and examined potential trials with different sample sizes. A trial of approximately 500 participants—250 per group—using the surrogate successfully reproduced the fracture conclusions after 24 months of treatment.

Perhaps the most important accomplishment was the U.S. Food and Drug Administration announcement on December 19, 2025, qualifying the 24-month percentage change from baseline in total hip BMD by DXA as a validated surrogate endpoint for trials of investigational osteoporosis therapies in postmenopausal women at risk for fracture. This allows smaller trials of defined duration, involving many fewer patients and substantially lower cost. It was a major accomplishment of the SABRE project.

## Infrequent zoledronate for primary fracture prevention

I will next review new findings with zoledronic acid. One important study was a 10-year primary-fracture-prevention trial led by Mark Bolland's group in New Zealand. It evaluated the efficacy and safety of infrequent zoledronate infusions in women 50 to 60 years old, examined efficacy across subgroups, and assessed what monitoring might be needed. I will then discuss two acute-treatment studies in hospitalized patients with hip fractures.

The 10-year placebo-controlled trial enrolled approximately 1,000 women aged 50 to 60 years with T-scores below 0 but above -2.5; therefore, many had normal BMD. There were three treatment groups: zoledronate 5 mg at baseline and again at year 5; zoledronate 5 mg at baseline followed by placebo at year 5; and placebo at both time points. The primary outcome was a new morphometric vertebral fracture at 10 years. Secondary outcomes included fragility fracture, any fracture, and major osteoporotic fracture.

Both zoledronate groups had an approximately 40% to 45% reduction in new morphometric vertebral fractures at 10 years. For fragility fractures, the group receiving two doses had about a 30% reduction. For any fracture and major osteoporotic fracture, reductions ranged from roughly 25% to 40%, and both active-treatment regimens were effective for those outcomes. The number needed to treat to prevent one vertebral fracture was 21 with two infusions and 22 with one infusion.

BMD remained stable or increased modestly over 10 years in the treatment groups, whereas it declined gradually with placebo. There were no new safety signals, and adverse events were distributed across the three groups. A post hoc analysis found that fracture reduction was consistent over the decade, especially during years 5 through 10; all nine examined subgroups benefited without a clearly favored subgroup; and serial BMD monitoring did not improve prediction of benefit. This is important because the population differs from the patients we commonly consider for osteoporosis treatment.

## Zoledronic acid during hospitalization for hip fracture

The first inpatient study evaluated the safety and efficacy of administering intravenous zoledronic acid during the initial hospitalization for hip fracture, with the goal of reducing the secondary-prevention treatment gap. This cohort study emulated a randomized trial using real-world data. It compared 652 hospitalized hip-fracture patients who received acute inpatient zoledronic acid with 1,926 propensity-matched untreated controls. Outcomes were all-cause mortality and new fractures over 24 months.

Overall survival was better in patients given inpatient zoledronic acid than in untreated patients. Among women, survival was 44% better with treatment; among men, the estimated improvement was 27% and was not statistically significant. The study also reported a 60% reduction in clinical vertebral fractures, with no difference in nonvertebral fractures or second hip fractures. There was no difference in nonunions, and no atypical femur fractures or osteonecrosis of the jaw was observed. This approach could help narrow the treatment gap in an extremely high-risk population.

The second inpatient study addressed patients with acute hip fracture and advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD), a population with high morbidity and mortality that is rarely treated because of concern about acute kidney injury, hypocalcemia, vitamin D deficiency, and other complications.

The investigators analyzed real-world data from hip-fracture patients with CKD stage 4 or greater who received reduced-dose intravenous zoledronic acid. The cohort included 46 inpatients, 78% of whom were women, with a mean age of 89.9 years and a mean creatinine clearance of approximately 31 mL/min. They were compared with 98 matched untreated hip-fracture controls with CKD.

All treated patients received calcium 650 to 1,000 mg/day and vitamin D 1,000 to 2,500 IU/day. Baseline 25-hydroxyvitamin D exceeded 20 ng/mL, and acetaminophen was given for 48 hours. Zoledronic acid doses were adjusted for kidney function: 3 mg for creatinine clearance of 30 to 34 mL/min, 2 mg for 20 to 29 mL/min, and 1 mg for 10 to 19 mL/min. The mean dose was 2.7 mg, generally administered on hospital day 3 over 45 minutes. Laboratory values and vital signs were monitored for 5 days.

One treated patient developed fever, compared with two untreated controls. Serum creatinine remained stable, and no patient developed acute kidney injury. Serum calcium declined from approximately 8.8 mg/dL before treatment to 8.2 mg/dL on day 5, but no value fell below 7.5 mg/dL and no patient required intravenous calcium rescue. Length of stay and readmission rates did not differ between treated and untreated patients. This was a small study without long-term outcomes, but the simple regimen could affect a population that is almost never treated during acute hospitalization.

## Three months of romosozumab followed by denosumab

I will now turn to a novel sequential regimen with romosozumab. Romosozumab is a highly effective, dual-action osteoporosis drug. It is osteoanabolic during the first few months and antiresorptive thereafter. Limitations include cost, monthly administration at a health-care facility, lingering concern about cardiovascular safety, and adherence below 50%.

This was a 12-month prospective, open-label, randomized noninferiority trial in postmenopausal women at high fracture risk. It compared 3 months of romosozumab followed by 9 months of denosumab with the standard 12-month course of romosozumab. The hypothesis was that a shorter course could retain anabolic efficacy while addressing practical limitations.

The primary endpoint was the 12-month change in total hip BMD. Secondary endpoints included lumbar-spine, femoral-neck, and distal-radius BMD. The prespecified noninferiority margin at the hip was 2%.

At 12 months, mean total hip BMD increased 5.7% in the 3-month romosozumab/denosumab group and 6.0% in the 12-month romosozumab group, meeting the noninferiority criterion. The curves were broadly superimposable. Femoral-neck BMD increased 5.0% versus 6.3%, lumbar-spine BMD increased 10.6% versus 12.5%, and distal-radius BMD changed by -0.3% versus -1.5%, respectively. A shorter but similarly effective romosozumab course could lower costs, improve adherence, and perhaps reduce concern about cardiovascular risk.

## Teriparatide followed by zoledronic acid in osteogenesis imperfecta

In the final part, I will discuss a sequential-therapy trial of teriparatide followed by zoledronic acid in osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), published in *JAMA*. OI is a rare bone disease, but endocrinologists often serve as the primary physicians caring for affected patients. A prior trial from approximately a decade ago suggested that teriparatide could improve BMD in OI, although it was not powered for fractures.

This multicenter, open-label, randomized trial enrolled 350 adults with a clinical diagnosis of OI. One group received sequential therapy with teriparatide 20 mcg/day for 24 months followed by intravenous zoledronic acid 5 mg. The comparison group received standard or usual care; some patients received bisphosphonates or vitamin D, but anabolic therapy was not allowed. The primary outcome was imaging-confirmed and adjudicated incident fracture. Secondary outcomes included BMD, bone-turnover markers, and quality of life.

The trial did not meet its primary endpoint. There was no difference between sequential therapy and usual care in the number of participants with incident fractures or in the total number of incident or vertebral fractures. Four of five patient-reported measures—including pain, quality of life, overall health, and sleep—favored sequential therapy, but the open-label design tempers enthusiasm for those outcomes.

Teriparatide clearly had biological activity: lumbar-spine and total-hip BMD increased, and bone-turnover markers moved in the expected direction. Nevertheless, despite following a sequence that is becoming standard in osteoporosis care—an anabolic drug followed by a potent antiresorptive—and despite the BMD and marker responses, fracture rates did not differ from usual care. OI is fundamentally different from postmenopausal osteoporosis. Bone quality and matrix composition substantially alter the relationship between BMD and fracture in this population.

## Conclusions

In December 2025, the FDA made what the presentation described as the first-ever qualification of a surrogate endpoint in any disease: percentage change in total hip BMD for osteoporosis drug development through the SABRE program. We reviewed evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of intravenous zoledronic acid in younger postmenopausal women and in hospitalized patients with acute hip fracture. We reviewed the noninferiority of 3 months of romosozumab followed by 9 months of denosumab compared with 12 months of romosozumab for total hip BMD. Finally, we reviewed the absence of fracture reduction with teriparatide followed by zoledronic acid in OI despite improvements in BMD and bone-turnover markers, emphasizing the importance of bone quality in fracture risk.
