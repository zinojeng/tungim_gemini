### AR signaling, therapy resistance, and condensates

To start, I don't need to introduce AR signaling for this group, but it is the main driver of prostate cancer progression and the main therapeutic target for prostate cancer treatment. For framing in this session, AR is usually cytoplasmic and bound to chaperone proteins, but upon androgen binding it undergoes a conformational change, translocates to the nucleus, and can regulate its target genes.

Treatment for prostate cancer aims to block this signaling pathway either by reducing androgen levels or by blocking androgen binding to the androgen receptor. However, treatment resistance occurs and leads to an advanced form of the disease known as castration-resistant prostate cancer. There are many mechanisms underlying this resistance, such as AR overexpression, coactivator overexpression, AR mutations in the ligand-binding domain where androgen binds—which can transform antagonists into agonists—as well as expression of splice variants that completely lack the ligand-binding domain and make the receptor constitutively active.

That's regarding AR and prostate cancer. How about condensates? Condensates are liquid-like droplets that form in the cell to perform specific functions. They form when the cell needs them or upon specific stimuli, and then they dissolve when they are no longer needed. They have been shown to be involved in many cell functions, such as stress response, DNA repair, translation, and transcription, which is the focus of my lab. There are beautiful reviews in the field about their role, function, and sometimes composition.

What I want to introduce today is a concept introduced by the Tanja Mittag lab. They think about transcription factors working on a continuum, going from soluble complexes all the way to condensates. Maybe I should add to this slide, after today's talk by Dr. McDonnell, the monomer side of AR.

The traditional view of transcription factor function is that the transcription factor recognizes specific DNA and recruits cofactors and RNA polymerase II, which leads to transcription. On the condensate side, we have multiple copies of that transcription factor for various reasons. It could be because we have multiple binding sites at specific genomic loci or because local overexpression increases the local concentration of the transcription factor. That could lead to enhanced recruitment of cofactors and RNA polymerase II, and we think that could lead to enhanced expression of target genes regulated by condensates.

### Full-length AR condensates

When I started my lab back in 2020, I was already working on the androgen receptor, but more from a drug-development point of view during my postdoc. Coming from a biophysics and structural biology background, I was always trying to find the right conditions to solve the structure of AR. I was thinking that if I found the right cofactors that bind to AR, I would be able to stabilize the disordered NTD that you see in green and solve the structure by cryo-EM. It didn't work.

Then the condensate field started publishing papers about transcription factors that have large disordered regions similar to the N-terminal domain of AR. You are seeing it as spaghetti here. I then decided to start looking at whether AR forms condensates in prostate cancer.

Indeed, we were able to show that AR forms condensates in LNCaP cells. Here you are looking at LNCaP cells transfected with AR tagged with a non-dimerizing GFP. Upon androgen stimulation, AR translocates to the nucleus and forms this kind of speckle in the nucleus.

We published a paper a couple of years ago in which we characterized those condensates. We showed their dynamics. They form when we add androgen. If we remove androgen, the number of foci goes down. If we then add androgen again, they form again. We also showed that they correlate with AR transcription and bind to specific DNA sequences. We were also able to create them outside the cell using recombinant full-length protein, which you can see here. They are liquid-like because, if you follow some of them, you see them merging and forming larger condensates.

### AR-V7 condensates in CRPC models

Today I'm not going to talk about full-length AR because this work has been published. We were curious whether truncated forms of AR that share the same disordered N-terminal domain can also form condensates in prostate cancer models. We used AR-V7 as our template.

When we tried truncating full-length AR to identify which domains of the protein are important for condensate formation, removing any domain led to loss of condensates, including when we tried AR-V7. However, those experiments were done in LNCaP cells, which do not usually express splice variants.

A very talented postdoc in my lab, Shabnam Massah, joined and started looking at whether endogenous AR-V7 forms condensates. Endogenous AR-V7 can form condensates in CRPC models that express splice variants. Indeed, in CRPC models, she was able to see that AR-V7 formed condensates in the absence or presence of androgen. However, full-length AR in those models still needs androgen to form a significant number of condensates.

We validated this finding using exogenous AR-V7 in different CRPC models. Again, in LNCaP, the non-CRPC model, AR-V7 formed a very small number of condensates. However, in all the CRPC models we tested, it was able to form condensates regardless of androgen, which is expected because it is an androgen-independent variant. Full-length AR still required androgen to form those condensates.

We wondered whether AR-V7 and full-length AR need each other to form those condensates because there are some models in which AR-V7 needs to dimerize with AR-FL to help AR-FL move to the nucleus. We used a PROTAC molecule to degrade full-length AR because it recognizes the ligand-binding domain without affecting AR-V7, as you can see here on the Western blot. We lost the foci formed by full-length AR. However, degrading full-length AR with the PROTAC molecule had no effect on the number of foci formed by AR-V7. We therefore know that AR-V7 is able to form condensates regardless of androgen and regardless of the presence of full-length protein.

### Biophysical properties and DNA recognition

Then we wanted to know whether they have the same biophysical properties. Do they undergo phase separation at the same saturation concentration? To measure that, we used an AR-negative prostate cancer model and transfected it with increasing amounts of full-length AR-GFP or AR-V7-GFP. We quantified the number of foci as a function of protein concentration and found that AR-V7 required at least a twofold higher concentration to reach the same foci density as full-length AR.

We also validated these data with recombinant protein. We expressed and purified both full-length AR and AR-V7 and again saw a two- to threefold higher concentration requirement for AR-V7 to form condensates.

These data are in line with some of the binding assays we are doing. These are very preliminary data, but we have a C-Trap optical-tweezers system. We have a DNA sequence with an AR-binding site—an androgen response element, or ARE—in the middle, tagged with a red fluorophore, and we trap the DNA between the two beads you see here. We can then follow fluorescence as a function of time.

What you are looking at here is the region containing our ARE, so that is a signal from our DNA. We then move those beads into a channel containing either full-length AR or AR-V7. As you can see, full-length AR binds at the exact spot where we have our ARE. However, to obtain the same binding with AR-V7, we had to use a fivefold higher concentration, showing that AR-V7 has a different affinity for DNA than full-length AR. We also found that, if we remove the beads from the channel, full-length AR stays bound to the DNA, whereas AR-V7 dissociates very quickly.

Those single-molecule experiments were not done in the context of condensates, so we wondered whether AR-V7 is able to recognize specific DNA sequences within condensates. To test that, we designed DNA containing an AR-binding site in the middle and labeled it with a red fluorescent dye. We formed AR-V7 condensates in vitro. If the DNA is enriched within those condensates, we obtain red dots. If the DNA is diffuse and is not concentrated anywhere, we do not obtain a signal in the red channel.

With the scrambled sequence, we did not obtain any signal. With a specific ARE, we were able to see red droplets. When we used exactly the same sequence unlabeled, we were able to compete that DNA out of the AR-V7 droplets. Adding DNA helped AR-V7 form even more condensates, showing that DNA can lower the threshold concentration for droplet formation.

We used this methodology to test different ARE sequences. We tried an AR half-site, a palindromic ARE that is the canonical sequence, and a chimeric FOXA1-ARE that we see more often in CRPC patients. Surprisingly, for both AR-V7 and full-length AR, the chimeric FOXA1-ARE was the most enriched DNA within the droplets and lowered the concentration at which AR-V7 could form condensates.

To validate these data, we formed AR-V7 droplets with ROX-labeled chimeric DNA or ROX-labeled palindromic DNA and added increasing amounts of either half-site, palindromic, or chimeric DNA. For both AR-V7 and full-length AR, the chimeric DNA containing FOXA1 and ARE half-sites had the greatest ability to displace all the other DNA from the droplets.

### A condensate-disrupting AR-V7 mutant

AR-V7 is able to form condensates and recognizes specific AR DNA sequences. We wondered whether it can activate a specific program in CRPC models. To probe that, we designed a mutant that would disrupt those condensates.

We were inspired by work from the Salvatella lab in Spain reporting that tyrosine residues in the N-terminal domain of AR can disrupt condensate formation if they are mutated to serine. They tested variants with increasing numbers of tyrosine-to-serine mutations. However, some mutants affected nuclear translocation, which is a problem if we want to study the transcriptional program.

We therefore decided to find the minimum number of tyrosines we needed to mutate to affect condensate formation without affecting nuclear translocation. We found that mutating five tyrosines to serines—the 5Y2S mutant—significantly affected condensate formation without affecting the expression level, nuclear level, or DNA binding of AR-V7, as you can see from the ChIP-PCR here.

We then used these two constructs and transfected them into AR-negative PC3 cells. Sorry—AR-negative PC3 cells. We performed bulk RNA sequencing to identify a condensate-dependent transcriptome. Among the enriched pathways, we found KRAS signaling, hypoxia, and Notch signaling, which are all involved in cancer progression.

We validated some of the condensate-dependent and condensate-independent genes we identified. As you can see, with GFP alone we do not have expression of those genes. Their expression increases with AR-V7 and is reduced by the mutant. However, the condensate-independent genes do not seem to be affected by the mutant.

Because most of the data came from PC3 cells, we also wondered whether they are relevant in models that express AR-V7. We validated all our findings in 22Rv1 and LNCaP95. I'm showing 22Rv1 here, but we couldn't study the mutant there because there are endogenous proteins. Instead, we used 1,6-hexanediol, a chemical that dissolves condensates, and found conditions in which we did not see toxicity, because it is a very toxic chemical. We did not see toxicity in the cells, but condensate formation was reduced. As you can see here, we were able to reduce condensate-dependent gene expression without affecting condensate-independent genes.

### Condensate-dependent transcription and cellular phenotypes

To return to our model: do these condensates help enhance expression of condensate-dependent genes? To probe that, we transfected cells with increasing amounts of AR-V7 or the mutant. As you can see, condensate-dependent genes have exponential expression as a function of AR-V7 concentration, and that increase was disrupted by the mutant. However, if we take known AR-V7 target genes that were identified as condensate-independent in our assay, we see linear expression, and it was not significantly affected by the mutant.

We asked what would make certain genes condensate-dependent and others condensate-independent, and we collaborated with the Nathan Lack lab at the Vancouver Prostate Centre. One finding was that condensate-dependent genes tend to have a higher number of AR-binding sites in the promoter than condensate-independent genes. Sorry for the typo on the slide.

We created DNA sequences to test this idea in vitro. We created DNA with increasing numbers of AR-binding sites and, using the same method I showed before, looked for enrichment within AR-V7 and full-length AR condensates. For both proteins, DNA with a higher number of AR-binding sites tended to be more enriched within the condensates.

We also wanted to see whether those condensates have a functional outcome, especially because KRAS was among the top enriched gene pathways. We checked proliferation and migration and found that, when we disrupted the condensates with the mutant, proliferation and migration were lower.

### Patient relevance and conclusions

How is that relevant to patients? Are these condensates even present in patients, and what is their role? We started looking at some tissue microarrays that we have at our center. Our preliminary data show that cancer regions tend to have more cells with this type of speckling than adjacent normal benign tissue.

We decided to look at whether our signature of condensate-dependent genes is relevant in patient cohorts. This is very complicated because there is no agreement on AR-V7 target genes. There is no single list of AR target genes, and if we take different studies, we find only a few genes in common.

We decided to use three available studies that compared androgen-dependent and androgen-independent models. We narrowed this down to what are hopefully AR-V7 target genes and then took the overlap between those genes and our condensate list across the three studies. When we looked at this refined signature, we found that it was higher in patients with CRPC than in patients with adenocarcinoma. We also found that patients with high expression of our signature tended to have higher proliferation and metastasis scores.

The data are very preliminary. We are still trying to validate them in different cohorts and to narrow the signature to a level at which we can validate it using multiplex IHC, for example.

In conclusion, I showed you that AR-V7 is able to form condensates, but only in CRPC models. We were able to design a mutant that disrupts those condensates, giving us a tool to probe condensate function. We also found that AR-V7 is able to drive a condensate-dependent program and a condensate-independent program, most probably through the soluble complexes that I showed at the beginning. The condensate-dependent program seems to be associated more with growth and proliferation.

What we are trying to do now is identify the composition of these condensates using proteomics and determine whether we can use them for drug screening, because they can affect treatment responses by excluding some chemicals from reaching their targets. We may be treating prostate cancer, but if our cancer cells are forming these droplets, our chemical may not enter the droplet and reach the target, and that could contribute to resistance. Those are the two directions in which we are taking this work.

At the end, I want to thank my team. The work was mainly led by Shabnam Massah, and I would like to thank all our collaborators on this project and the funding agencies. I'm glad to answer all your questions. If you have more questions after this session, I have a poster on Monday. Thank you.
