Dr. Kahn is the Vice Chair for Research Strategy at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston and is internationally recognized for her groundbreaking discoveries that have helped shape the fields of diabetes and endocrinology research for over three decades. Her pioneering discoveries have illuminated the molecular mechanisms underlying obesity and type 2 diabetes, including the regulation of insulin sensitivity through intertissue communication and adipocyte biology.

Her lab has made several novel discoveries in this area of research, and she has trained numerous highly successful leaders in endocrinology and metabolism around the world. She has served as an editor for *Endocrine Reviews* and on the editorial board of *Endocrinology*, both premier Endocrine Society journals. Please welcome to the stage Dr. Barbara Kahn.

**Barbara B. Kahn:** Good morning. I'd like to start by thanking Carol Lange and the organizers for the opportunity to be here today. I'm really excited about telling you about these novel lipids that we discovered with Dr. Alan Saghatelian.

This drawing indicates that these lipids are in the human body. They promote insulin secretion when glucose rises, promote glucose transport into adipocytes via the GLUT4 glucose transporter, and are anti-inflammatory.

What I'm going to tell you today is, briefly, about the discovery of the lipids, their biological actions, their beneficial effects in type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and the mechanisms regulating them.

## Adipose GLUT4 and the discovery of FAHFAs

Our story starts with the GLUT4 glucose transporter, which, as you know, is the major insulin-regulated glucose transporter. Years ago, we and others observed that, in humans and rodents with obesity and type 2 diabetes, GLUT4 is downregulated in adipose tissue but not in muscle. This was perplexing because not very much glucose goes into adipose tissue, and we were interested in whether this could contribute to the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

We became further interested because we collaborated with Ulf Smith in Gothenburg, Sweden. He performed euglycemic-clamp studies in people who were at risk for type 2 diabetes because of a strong family history, and he found a very tight correlation between insulin sensitivity and GLUT4 protein in adipose tissue. Furthermore, he showed that decreased GLUT4 protein levels in adipose tissue are an early predictor of type 2 diabetes in people.

I was therefore really happy to see a paper a couple of years ago entitled “Genome-Wide Association Study and Functional Characterization Identifies Candidate Genes for Insulin-Stimulated Glucose Transport.” This was a GWAS in 55,000 participants from three ancestry groups. The really important aspect was that the study was performed in the postprandial state. Most GWAS analyses are performed in the fasting state, but in the postprandial state one is able to see genes involved in insulin action.

The authors identified nine candidate genes for type 2 diabetes in humans that regulate the expression or trafficking of the GLUT4 glucose transporter. They validated these genes with functional studies, and mutations in some of these genes have subsequently been shown to cause diabetes in people. These genetic studies tell us that GLUT4 is important for insulin sensitivity and type 2 diabetes risk in people.

But does GLUT4 in adipose tissue, as compared with muscle, regulate systemic insulin sensitivity? We made adipose-specific GLUT4-knockout mice and adipose-specific GLUT4-overexpressing mice. At the time, it was surprising that knocking out GLUT4 only in fat made the animals insulin resistant, with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes, whereas overexpressing GLUT4 only in fat enhanced glucose tolerance and lowered fasting glycemia. When we studied the tissues in vivo and ex vivo, we found evidence for systemic effects on muscle and liver.

We performed a series of studies, including DNA-array analyses. What we found was that driving glucose into adipocytes via GLUT4 confers increased insulin sensitivity. This depends on driving that glucose into fatty acids, and it is regulated by the transcription factor carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein, or ChREBP.

We wanted to know: What are these fatty acids with beneficial metabolic effects? We collaborated with Alan Saghatelian and his student Edwin Homan. Untargeted lipidomic analysis revealed a new class of lipids that we called branched fatty acid esters of hydroxy fatty acids, or FAHFAs.

Let me show you what a FAHFA consists of. There is a fatty acid group—here it is palmitic acid—and a hydroxy fatty acid group. I am showing hydroxystearic acid bound by an ester bond. We call this 9-palmitic acid ester of hydroxystearic acid, or 9-PAHSA. The ester bond can be at a number of different carbons, so there are multiple PAHSA regioisomers with branching at different carbons.

When we first discovered these lipids, we found several families with different fatty acids and hydroxy fatty acids. Through the work of multiple groups, we now know that there are numerous families with multiple isomers in mammals. In fact, we call it a “FAHFAome.” This scheme was made by Ondřej Kuda in Prague, and he organized it around the hydroxy fatty acid. There are more than 300 of these lipids in human adipose tissue. There are also more families in plants, and FAHFAs are evolutionarily conserved and present in yeast.

We have mainly studied palmitic acid esters of hydroxystearic acids, or PAHSAs. What is the tissue distribution of the PAHSA subfamily of the FAHFA class of lipids? They are present in every tissue that we studied and are highest in white and brown adipose tissue. Their tissue concentrations are similar to those of other signaling lipids, such as prostaglandins and prostacyclins.

## Human PAHSA levels and biological actions

Are PAHSAs regulated in insulin-resistant people? For that question, we collaborated with Ulf Smith in Gothenburg, Sweden. Ulf sent us serum from insulin-sensitive and insulin-resistant people. These are different PAHSA isomers, and you can see that their levels are lower in insulin-resistant people. Both groups had euglycemic-clamp data, so we could show that serum PAHSA levels correlate very tightly with glucose infusion rate, with an *r* value of 0.9 for some of these isomers.

Very recently, we have been able to perform a study with Ulf examining changes in PAHSA concentrations over time and showing that these changes predict the development of impaired glucose tolerance in people. This was a relatively small study. All participants had a family history of type 2 diabetes. They were young, with a mean age of 40 years, and relatively lean, with a BMI of 25. All participants had normal glucose tolerance at baseline. Half maintained normal glucose tolerance, whereas the other half developed impaired glucose tolerance.

When we looked at PAHSA levels, as serum PAHSA levels decreased, oral glucose tolerance test glucose increased. Thus, a decrease in PAHSA levels over time predicts impaired glucose tolerance, and PAHSAs may serve as biomarkers of type 2 diabetes risk.

What are the biological effects of PAHSAs? As I mentioned, their serum and adipose-tissue levels are strongly associated with insulin sensitivity and with increased adipose lipogenesis. PAHSAs increase glucose transport and GLUT4 translocation in adipocytes. They increase GLP-1 secretion from gut enteroendocrine cells. They increase insulin secretion in insulin-resistant aged mice and in human pancreatic islets. I will show you that they also have direct protective effects on islets by decreasing senescence.

They also decrease glucose production from the liver, so they are insulin sensitizers, and they are anti-inflammatory. We have shown that they decrease adipose-tissue inflammation in aged mice and high-fat-diet-fed mice, as well as inflammation in mouse models of colitis and type 1 diabetes. All of this improves glucose homeostasis.

You might ask: How can they have so many different effects? It is because they act through G protein-coupled receptors. We identified GPR120 as the receptor mediating glucose transport and GPR40 as the receptor mediating insulin secretion. We are working on another receptor that mediates the effects on hepatic glucose production.

This is an example of the insulin-sensitizing effect. Mice on a high-fat diet were treated with PAHSAs for 13 weeks, and this is the glucose infusion rate. You can see that the vehicle-treated mice were quite insulin resistant, whereas PAHSA treatment markedly improved insulin sensitivity.

If we look at hepatic suppression of glucose production, it is not very effective in vehicle-treated mice, but there is a substantial improvement in insulin's ability to suppress hepatic glucose production after PAHSA treatment. Thus, PAHSAs enhance hepatic and systemic insulin sensitivity.

## Anti-inflammatory and islet-protective effects

I next want to move to the anti-inflammatory effects. We first saw these in a model of ulcerative colitis, and I want to show you this because we worked out the immune-cell effects in this model. PAHSAs delay the onset and reduce the severity of colitis, and they markedly reduce gut inflammation.

In this disease, dendritic cells present antigens to T cells. That causes the release of interleukin-12, which causes T-cell activation and clonal expansion, resulting in cytokine production and gut inflammation. We showed that PAHSAs reduce costimulatory-molecule expression and dendritic-cell activation. There is less clonal expansion of T cells, lower cytokine production, and less gut inflammation.

Could these anti-inflammatory effects be beneficial in other immune-mediated diseases? What about type 1 diabetes? Ismail Syed led these studies in the NOD mouse, a model of autoimmune type 1 diabetes. In our colony, 83% of untreated female mice develop type 1 diabetes. Ismail showed that daily oral PAHSA treatment delayed the onset and markedly reduced the incidence. It also improved survival.

We went on to show that there were two types of mechanisms: immune-cell modulation and direct protective effects on pancreatic islets. First, look at this islet. It is a relatively normal-looking islet, with insulin staining in magenta and glucagon staining around the periphery in green. This islet is not being assaulted by immune cells, whereas another islet in the same mouse is undergoing immune attack.

But look at this vehicle-treated islet. It has lost its normal architecture and some insulin staining. The glucagon cells—the alpha cells—are intermingled with the beta cells, and there is massive immune infiltration. Maria Rubin de Celis went on to examine beta-cell proliferation in vivo in PAHSA- and vehicle-treated mice and showed that PAHSAs increase beta-cell proliferation.

We wanted to know whether these effects were secondary to the anti-inflammatory effects or whether there were direct effects on the islets. Ismail Syed studied MIN6 clonal beta cells in culture. He treated them with cytokines, which increased apoptosis, and showed that this was largely prevented by PAHSAs. Cytokines also increased necrosis, and that was markedly reduced by PAHSAs. Cytokines decreased beta-cell proliferation, and that was partially reversed by PAHSA treatment. We therefore think there are direct protective effects, even independent of the immunomodulatory effects.

How do PAHSAs protect pancreatic islets under metabolic stress? As you know, with the development of type 1 diabetes, all of these stresses are active. We decided to perform bulk RNA sequencing of islets from NOD mice treated with vehicle or PAHSAs. We designed the study so that we would treat for five weeks and then end the study before the mice became hyperglycemic, so hyperglycemia would not affect gene expression. We also removed immune cells from the islets so that we were looking mainly at endocrine cells.

Here is the expression of hundreds of genes in five different vehicle-treated mice. Each row is a different mouse. Many genes have low expression and even more have high expression. We know that 83% of these vehicle-treated mice are going to develop type 1 diabetes, so four out of five of these mice will develop type 1 diabetes.

What do PAHSAs do even before the development of diabetes? There is a massive shift in the expression of hundreds of genes. What are these genes? First, we looked at islet genes and hormones. You can see that PAHSAs increase the expression of insulin and urocortin 3, a marker of beta cells, but also glucagon, so they also affect alpha cells. Then there are genes called “disallowed genes.” These genes are usually not expressed in islets, and PAHSAs decrease their expression—for example, *LDHA*.

Maria went on to perform pathway analysis and found that PAHSAs decrease senescence genes. Senescence is associated with the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, a complex mixture of secreted factors that includes pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases. These factors were also decreased in PAHSA-treated mice.

Senescence has been shown to be involved in beta-cell failure and destruction in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and senolytic agents are in clinical trials for several chronic diseases. We therefore really wanted to understand this better and asked: How do PAHSAs protect against senescence?

Here are a number of senescence triggers. Reactive metabolites are particularly pertinent to diabetes. These triggers activate a p16 pathway and a p53–p21 pathway, which lead to senescence. DNA damage is both upstream and downstream of senescence. We wanted to know which of these pathways PAHSAs affect to reduce senescence.

We first looked at DNA repair and showed that PAHSAs regulate DNA-repair genes. We looked at glutathione metabolism, which regulates oxidative stress, and found that PAHSA treatment favored an antioxidant state. We knocked down key regulators of DNA repair and showed that this pathway is necessary for the effects of PAHSAs on senescence.

Is this relevant to human islets under metabolic stress? To answer that question, Maria obtained islets from six normal donors. When she looked at senescence markers and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, she saw very little expression. When she treated the islets with cytokines, all of the donors had an increase in p21, a marker of senescence. If she administered PAHSAs after the cytokines, she could reduce the expression of that senescence marker. We saw the same effect on the secretory phenotype. Thus, PAHSA treatment actually reverses cytokine-induced senescence in human islets.

In mice and humans, metabolic stress leads to beta-cell senescence. PAHSA treatment increases DNA repair, increases the antioxidant state through glutathione, decreases senescence, and decreases the secretory phenotype. Thus, PAHSAs reduce beta-cell senescence and preserve beta-cell phenotype by decreasing DNA damage. This preserves beta-cell mass. Recall the increased insulin staining that I showed in NOD islets from PAHSA-treated animals.

## FAHFA storage and biosynthesis

Given these beneficial effects, we became very interested in the mechanisms regulating the tissue levels of these lipids. Again, a FAHFA consists of a fatty acid plus a hydroxy fatty acid. There could be regulation at the level of synthesis or degradation.

One of the fellows in our collaborator Alan Saghatelian's lab was very interested in whether FAHFAs could be incorporated into other lipid classes that would serve as storage depots. Dan Tan showed that FAHFAs can be incorporated into triacylglycerols, where they can serve as an acyl chain. What is the physiological relevance? Free FAHFAs are then released from FAHFA-containing triacylglycerols during lipolysis.

Next, we studied synthesis and showed that FAHFAs are synthesized in mammalian tissues in vivo. That sent us on the search for the “Holy Grail”: the enzyme responsible for FAHFA synthesis. This enzyme would be an as-yet-unidentified FAHFA synthase. Based on the chemistry, it would be an acyltransferase or transacylase responsible for making the ester bond between the fatty acid and the hydroxy fatty acid.

We found 60 human enzymes identified in RefSeq as acyltransferases, and Rucha Patel in the lab overexpressed 18 of these with minimal effects on FAHFA biosynthesis. She went back to the adipose-specific GLUT4-overexpressing mice that I introduced at the beginning. These are the mice with enhanced glucose tolerance, increased adipose de novo lipogenesis, and increased plasma fatty acids, and they are the mice in which our global adipose-tissue lipidomics resulted in the discovery of FAHFAs. Here is the massive increase in PAHSAs in the adipose tissue from these mice.

Rucha took the approach of generating stromal vascular fraction-derived adipocytes from these mice. She then fed the cells a precursor—a hydroxy fatty acid—and used LC–MS to detect a fully formed FAHFA. She obtained a really important result: FAHFA biosynthesis is increased in adipose GLUT4-overexpressing adipocytes in a cell-autonomous manner. That meant that we could use these cells to look for regulators of FAHFA biosynthesis.

Rucha then inadvertently found an inhibitor of FAHFA biosynthesis: a fluorophosphonate. Dionicio “Dio” Siegel, a chemist with whom we work, modified the end of the inhibitor so that we could pull out proteins with it. Rucha treated the adipocytes with the inhibitor, made cell lysates in which proteins were bound to the inhibitor, and performed click chemistry to affix a label to the proteins. We then sent this to Alan Saghatelian, who performed streptavidin enrichment and proteomic identification of candidate enzymes.

We strategized to identify proteins that were more abundant in the GLUT4-overexpressing mice than in normal mice. What emerged was adipose triglyceride lipase, or ATGL.

What is ATGL? It is a member of the patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein family. That is a mouthful, but you are probably familiar with some of the other family members. They were initially identified in potato tubers—hence “patatin”—and they are important regulators of glycerolipid metabolism. Mutations in these genes in humans cause various diseases associated with increased neutral-lipid storage.

Mutations in *PNPLA3* cause metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, so there is a great deal of interest in this from the standpoint of MASH. We are talking about *PNPLA2*, which encodes ATGL and catalyzes the first step in lipolysis.

Our next question was: Is ATGL increased in adipose tissue from adipose-specific GLUT4-overexpressing mice? Yes—there is a very clear increase in ATGL when we overexpress GLUT4 in fat.

Can ATGL synthesize FAHFAs in vitro from a triacylglycerol as the acyl donor and a hydroxy fatty acid as the acyl acceptor? This is the only test-tube study I will show you. We have immunopurified ATGL and its coactivator, CGI-58, in a test tube with triolein, a triacylglycerol that we think is the acyl donor, and 9-hydroxystearic acid, which we think is the acyl acceptor.

You can see that there is no FAHFA when the enzyme is absent. When the enzyme is present, there is marked FAHFA synthesis. When we use the specific ATGL inhibitor, synthesis decreases. Thus, ATGL is capable of synthesizing FAHFAs.

## Physiological relevance of ATGL-mediated FAHFA synthesis

What is the physiological relevance of this? Are FAHFAs reduced in adipose-specific ATGL-knockout mice? We made adipose-specific ATGL-knockout mice, and you can see that FAHFA levels are quite low in subcutaneous fat, perigonadal fat, and brown fat. We do not see that reduction in the liver. Interestingly, some FAHFAs are low in serum, which makes us think that adipose tissue is an important source of circulating FAHFAs.

Adipose-specific ATGL-knockout mice also had very low PAHSA biosynthesis in adipose tissue in vivo. When we administered a labeled tracer, we saw very little active biosynthesis. Low PAHSA levels and low PAHSA biosynthesis in the adipose tissue of adipose-specific ATGL-knockout mice indicate that ATGL is an important regulator of PAHSA levels in vivo.

You are familiar with ATGL through its lipase activity, hydrolyzing triacylglycerols to diacylglycerols. This is a completely different activity of ATGL: in the presence of hydroxy fatty acids, it exerts transacylase activity to form a FAHFA. Let us look at it this way. One of the fatty acids is the acyl donor. ATGL then catalyzes the formation of an ester bond between that fatty acid and a hydroxy fatty acid to form a FAHFA.

In the last minute, I want to bring this back to physiology and human adipose tissue. We performed this experiment with a tracer in control mice and showed that fasting markedly increases FAHFAs in adipose tissue, but we do not see that in ATGL-knockout mice.

Sam Klein was kind enough to send us white-fat biopsies from people with obesity. These individuals had fasted and were then refed a mixed meal, and you can see that FAHFA biosynthesis is higher in the fasted state. When we examine isolated adipocytes and provide the precursor, we can increase FAHFA biosynthesis, but the ATGL inhibitor inhibits FAHFA biosynthesis in these human adipocytes. Thus, ATGL regulates FAHFA biosynthesis during fasting in mice and in human adipose tissue.

## Conclusion and acknowledgments

To summarize, GLUT4 in adipose tissue drives lipogenesis, which is driven by ChREBP. That causes the production of palmitic acid esters of hydroxystearic acids and other FAHFAs, which act through G protein-coupled receptors to increase glucose transport and decrease inflammation—effects that we think may be important for both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. They increase GLP-1 secretion from the gut and increase insulin secretion directly in human islets through GPR40. They also protect these islets through an antisenescence effect.

There are PAHSAs in other tissues that undoubtedly also contribute. Most recently, we have shown that PAHSAs decrease lipolysis, which is part of the mechanism by which they decrease hepatic glucose production. We hope to capitalize on all of these effects to treat or prevent type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

I mentioned the lab members as I went along, and here are our collaborators. I want to thank the islet donors and their families for the human islet cells, and mention Sam Klein again for the gift of human adipose tissue. Thank you.
