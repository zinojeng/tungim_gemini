## Perioperative management

Let us start with a case. This is a 70-year-old patient with pituitary insufficiency after resection of a pituitary macroadenoma. She is scheduled for hip replacement surgery, and the orthopedic team requests guidance on perioperative fluid management and desmopressin treatment. She currently drinks approximately 2–2.5 L/day and takes one 10 µg spray of DDAVP nasal spray at bedtime.

In patients with proven AVP deficiency, we recommend not withholding desmopressin before surgery. If desmopressin is omitted, rapid free-water loss may occur, resulting in significant polyuria, dehydration, and hypernatremia. This is especially dangerous when the patient is not allowed to drink.

### Before surgery

If the patient is conscious and oral fluid intake is allowed, proceed as usual: provide access to fluids and administer desmopressin as prescribed.

If the patient must fast and is nil by mouth, provide desmopressin as needed. It may be necessary to switch to parenteral desmopressin if the usual medication route is not permitted. Provide intravenous fluid replacement and monitor urine output—hourly when a bladder catheter is in place, or with each void otherwise. Monitoring urine output helps determine how much fluid the patient is losing and how much replacement is required. We recommend checking serum sodium every 6–12 hours. The endocrinology and anesthesia teams should be involved early.

### During surgery

During surgery, monitor fluid intake and output. If the operation lasts more than 4 hours, measure sodium using a venous blood gas, usually every 2 hours depending on urine output.

If the patient is normonatremic, continue sodium measurements every 2 hours, depending on the duration of surgery, without changing management.

If the patient is **hyponatremic with low or no urine output**, carefully restrict or pause intravenous fluid, measure sodium every 1–2 hours, and delay desmopressin until urine output increases.

If the patient is **hypernatremic with high urine output**, administer hypotonic intravenous fluid, such as 5% glucose, and measure sodium every 1–2 hours. High urine output—greater than 300 mL/h—indicates a need for additional desmopressin; the slide lists a starting dose of 1 µg intravenously.

### After surgery

After surgery, regularly assess volume and hydration status. Continue monitoring fluid intake and output, and check sodium every 4–6 hours until oral fluid intake and the usual desmopressin route are allowed. The treating team should manage volume and hydration until the patient has regained full cognitive function.

If oral fluid and desmopressin are not yet allowed, continue intravenous-fluid management until the patient can again self-manage the condition. Change to oral fluid replacement as soon as it is clinically safe, and allow self-management of desmopressin once cognition is no longer impaired by anesthetic or analgesic effects.

## Pregnancy

### Scenario 1: Pre-existing AVP-D

Consider a 34-year-old patient with isolated AVP-D due to hypophysitis and a newly confirmed pregnancy. She is worried about possible adverse effects of desmopressin on her unborn baby.

Desmopressin is considered safe during pregnancy and lactation. We suggest reevaluating the desmopressin dose during pregnancy because dose adjustment may be needed. Some patients require a higher dose because placental vasopressinase breaks down circulating endogenous AVP. Dose adjustments aim to prevent nocturia and maintain sodium within the target range. Within days to weeks postpartum, return to the pre-pregnancy dose.

### Scenario 2: Newly recognized AVP-D during pregnancy

Now consider a 32-year-old patient at 30 weeks of gestation who is referred with progressive polyuria and polydipsia of approximately 6 L/day. She underwent pituitary surgery for a prolactinoma 18 months earlier and has had residual mild polydipsia of approximately 4 L/day and intermittent nocturia since surgery.

In a pregnant patient with a history of pituitary disease and polyuria-polydipsia, we recommend considering AVP-D, because pregnancy can unmask mild deficiency.

Start treatment as you normally would. The aim is to prevent repeated nocturia and daytime urinary frequency. Start with the lowest possible dose of desmopressin at bedtime, then titrate according to symptoms and serum sodium. The slide gives examples of 50 µg orally, 10 µg by nasal spray, or 1 µg subcutaneously, intramuscularly, or intravenously at bedtime. For newly recognized pregnancy-related AVP-D, discontinue treatment within days to weeks postpartum if the deficiency resolves.

## Colonoscopy

One of the hardest questions is how to manage a patient who must undergo colonoscopy. Here, a 68-year-old patient has AVP-D following pituitary surgery and radiation therapy for acromegaly. He is scheduled for routine colonoscopy and asks how to manage desmopressin and fluid intake during bowel preparation.

Even patients without AVP-D are at risk of hyponatremia during colonoscopy preparation because they may consume large amounts of fluid while preparation-induced diarrhea is occurring. Patients with AVP-D should use a bowel-cleansing protocol that requires less fluid whenever possible—for example, a low-volume 2 L polyethylene glycol preparation rather than a high-volume 4 L preparation. We also suggest checking serum electrolytes on the morning of the procedure to ensure there is no severe disturbance.

## Adipsic AVP-D in outpatient care

For outpatient management of adipsic AVP-D, establish a goal weight at which the patient is both euvolemic and normonatremic. Prescribe a mandatory fluid intake of 1.5–2 L/day, or an individualized amount according to need. Plan for a 24-hour urine output of 1.5–2 L and determine the corresponding desmopressin dose.

Patients should weigh themselves daily to detect excessive water loss or gain. Daily fluid intake can be calculated as the mandatory intake in liters plus the difference between goal weight and daily weight, using 1 L for each 1 kg difference. Measure plasma sodium regularly—weekly at first and, once the patient is stable, at longer intervals such as monthly.

Educate patients and their families. Advise increased fluid intake during hot weather or periods of increased physical activity.

Intercurrent illness, diarrhea, vomiting, or reduced consciousness is particularly dangerous. These situations can precipitate **severe or fatal hypernatremia** by reducing effective fluid intake or causing additional water loss while desmopressin management continues. Any intercurrent illness warrants urgent sodium measurement, and hospital admission should be considered if oral intake is compromised. Patients with hypernatremia due to adipsic AVP-D should be treated acutely as any other hyperosmolar patient.

If available, point-of-care sodium measurement can make fluid-balance monitoring more practical and patient-friendly.

## Other situations

For time-zone changes during travel or for shift work, keep the same total daily desmopressin dose but adjust dose timing to the time zone or working hours. Avoid prophylactic drinking and drink according to thirst.

The same principle applies to exercise: drink to thirst and avoid excessive fluid consumption, which can cause exercise-associated hyponatremia.

Patients often ask about alcohol. Consuming large volumes of dilute alcoholic beverages, such as beer, can cause hyponatremia in AVP-D and should be avoided.

If a patient develops vomiting or diarrhea, advise increased fluid intake, consider adjustment of desmopressin, and consider switching prolonged oral treatment to a nasal or sublingual melt preparation when appropriate. Check serum electrolytes within 24 hours. Older or frail patients and those with hypodipsia or adipsia may need inpatient admission for stabilization.

### Chemotherapy-associated hyperhydration

**An audience member:** Thank you for the great presentation. We are often consulted when a patient with AVP-D is admitted for hyperhydration during chemotherapy. Do you have recommendations for desmopressin management during hyperhydration?

**John Newell-Price:** Thank you. It is a great question and, of course, it affects adults as well. I will pass that one to Julie.

**Julie Refardt:** Thank you very much. That is indeed a difficult situation. I would closely monitor fluid intake, urine output, and serum sodium. I would try to keep the desmopressin dose stable, but perhaps reduce it if you see the sodium falling.

**An audience member:** Our usual management is to give one-third of the dose, although some attendings hold it completely. We check sodium frequently—at least every 4 hours—and then give desmopressin as needed. Do you have any other thoughts?

**Julie Refardt:** No, that sounds reasonable to me.

**John Newell-Price:** Joe, would you like to comment?

**Joe Verbalis:** It is highly variable and depends on the individual case. You must monitor the fluids carefully. If hyponatremia develops because of excess water intake, you should delay the next desmopressin dose, but that does not mean stopping desmopressin altogether, because the patient may then develop excessive aquaresis.

I do not think we can give a fixed answer of 2, 4, or 6 hours. Give the next dose when the patient begins to have aquaresis with increased urine output and the serum sodium begins to rise.

### SGLT2 inhibitors

**An audience member:** If a patient with AVP-D has comorbid diabetes, is there any special consideration regarding the use of SGLT2 inhibitors?

**John Newell-Price:** I will direct that to Mirjam Christ-Crain.

**Mirjam Christ-Crain:** SGLT2 inhibitors lead to osmotic diuresis, but I would not see that as a major problem. I would monitor serum sodium when starting an SGLT2 inhibitor to see whether it changes; otherwise, I would not expect a major effect on desmopressin treatment.

### Timing of sodium measurements in pediatric AVP-D

**An audience member:** I am a pediatric endocrinologist. In congenital AVP-D, we often use twice-daily desmopressin. Do you have any thoughts about the timing of sodium testing relative to the last dose of desmopressin?

**John Newell-Price:** I will direct that to Mirjam.

**Mirjam Christ-Crain:** We are not going to recommend a specific time point for checking serum sodium during routine follow-up of patients with AVP-D. Water intake is usually relatively stable and is mostly meal-dependent.

If you give a nighttime desmopressin dose—which you almost always will—then checking a morning sodium is generally adequate. You are not trying to fine-tune the sodium; you are trying to prevent hyponatremia while keeping electrolytes in the normal range and allowing a normal lifestyle.

I do not think the time of day matters greatly in routine outpatient management. Sodium can fluctuate hour to hour if AVP-D is uncontrolled, but in a stable outpatient I would recommend a morning sodium. It does not matter whether the patient has already taken the morning dose, because that will not significantly change the serum sodium immediately.

**An audience member:** For infants receiving high fluid volumes, timing may make more of a difference. That was why I asked, but your answer makes sense. Thank you.

### Diagnostic testing during pregnancy

**An audience member:** Should the diagnostic algorithm for suspected AVP-D during pregnancy be the same as in the general population, or should it be modified?

**Julie Refardt:** It is not exactly the same algorithm, because there are several dynamic tests that should not be performed during pregnancy. Baseline testing is the same and can be done during pregnancy.

For overnight fluid deprivation, the decision depends on the pretest probability and the risk of dehydration. If the patient is at risk of dehydration, I would clearly not perform the test in a pregnant patient. If the risk is low, it may be performed very carefully. The available dynamic tests are not indicated during pregnancy, which makes diagnosis more challenging. Fortunately, it is a rare scenario.

### Laboratory turnaround during hypertonic-saline testing

**An audience member:** What about the safety of water deprivation and hypertonic-saline testing if the laboratory takes 30–60 minutes to return a serum sodium result? Should the test proceed when sodium reporting is delayed?

**Panel response:** For the hypertonic-saline test, it is a prerequisite to have rapid sodium measurement by venous blood gas, with results available within 30 minutes. Otherwise, the risk of excessive sodium elevation is too high.

**An audience member:** So a result within 30 minutes is the requirement?

**Panel response:** Absolutely, yes.

### Mechanism of pregnancy-related unmasking of AVP-D

**An audience member:** You mentioned that pregnancy can unmask diabetes insipidus, or AVP-D. What is the mechanism?

**Julie Refardt:** The placenta secretes vasopressinase. Some patients already have partial AVP deficiency, and their remaining endogenous AVP is degraded by vasopressinase during pregnancy, so they become symptomatic.
