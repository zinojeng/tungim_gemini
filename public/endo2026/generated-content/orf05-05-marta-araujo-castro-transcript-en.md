## Introduction and study aims

Hello, everyone. First, I would like to thank the organizing committee for inviting me to participate in this session of oral communications. The title of my presentation is “International Real-World Study on Osilodrostat Efficacy and Safety in Adrenal Cushing Syndrome.”

As you know, the treatment of choice for adrenal Cushing syndrome is surgical removal of the cortisol-secreting adrenal lesion. However, medical therapy has an established role in nonsurgical candidates, in patients who refuse surgery, when surgical resection is not possible, or as presurgical treatment, especially in patients with severe hypercortisolism.

Several steroidogenesis inhibitors are currently available for the treatment of Cushing syndrome, and osilodrostat is one of the most potent and effective oral drugs used as monotherapy. However, most patients included in clinical trials and real-world studies of this drug have had ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome, while data on the efficacy and safety of osilodrostat in patients with adrenal Cushing syndrome are scarce.

Considering this background, the purpose of our study was to assess the efficacy of osilodrostat in ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome and identify predictors of response to osilodrostat in these patients. In addition, we evaluated the impact of treatment on the cardiometabolic profile and the safety of osilodrostat in adrenal Cushing syndrome.

## International cohort and outcome definitions

We performed an international adrenal Cushing syndrome study with a total of 433 patients: 95 patients with adrenocortical carcinoma, 251 with unilateral adrenal disease, and 87 with bilateral disease. For the present study, patients treated with osilodrostat at any time were included in the safety evaluation, while those treated for longer than one month were included in the efficacy evaluation.

Patients were classified as responders if they experienced a reduction in urinary free cortisol greater than 50%. Complete responders had urinary free cortisol levels below the upper limit of normal. Partial responders had a reduction in urinary free cortisol greater than 50% without normalization.

As I previously mentioned, the main efficacy endpoint was the proportion of patients with a partial or complete response after treatment with osilodrostat for longer than one month. As a secondary outcome, we evaluated the safety of osilodrostat in adrenal Cushing syndrome.

## Patient characteristics

Moving to the results, 28 patients with adrenal Cushing syndrome were included. Sixteen patients had adrenocortical carcinoma: 13 had metastatic adrenocortical carcinoma and three had nonmetastatic disease. Twelve patients had benign disease: five had unilateral and seven had bilateral disease.

The median age at the time of Cushing syndrome diagnosis was 59.5 years, and 68% were women. The median urinary free cortisol level at diagnosis was 6.3 times the upper limit of normal. Fifteen patients, or 53.6%, had severe Cushing syndrome, defined as urinary free cortisol above five times the upper limit of normal.

When we compared patients with benign disease and those with adrenocortical carcinoma, we found that patients with adrenocortical carcinoma were younger, had higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure, had a higher prevalence of hypokalemia—81.3% versus 8.3%—and more frequently had severe hypercortisolism and unilateral disease.

For example, the dexamethasone suppression test cortisol value was 26.6 µg/dL in the adrenocortical carcinoma group and 8.1 µg/dL in the benign-disease group. Similarly, urinary free cortisol was 21.1 times the upper limit of normal in adrenocortical carcinoma and 3.7 times the upper limit of normal in benign adrenal disease.

## Osilodrostat treatment patterns

In relation to osilodrostat therapy, the median urinary free cortisol level before starting osilodrostat was 4.2 times the upper limit of normal, with a range from 0.6 to 53.3. This means there was one patient with a normal urinary free cortisol level at the time osilodrostat was initiated. Therapy was started based on the clinical phenotype, hypokalemia, and late-night salivary cortisol greater than five times the upper limit of normal. This patient was not included in the efficacy evaluation because urinary free cortisol was normal when osilodrostat was initiated.

The median duration of therapy with osilodrostat was seven months, with a range from one to 64 months. Osilodrostat was used as monotherapy in 22 patients and in combination with metyrapone in six patients. It was used as first-line therapy in 15 patients, second-line therapy in nine, third-line therapy in three, and fourth-line therapy in one case.

The median starting dose was 4 mg/day, with a range from 1 to 10 mg/day. The median maintenance dose was 9 mg/day, and the median maximum dose was 10 mg/day. When we compared combination therapy with monotherapy, there were no significant differences in the initial or maximum osilodrostat doses between the two groups.

Ten patients were treated with osilodrostat using a titration approach followed by block-and-replace, and one patient receiving combination therapy started block-and-replace from the beginning. The use of block-and-replace was more common in patients receiving combination therapy than in those receiving monotherapy. As expected, the maximum osilodrostat dose was higher in the block-and-replace group than in the titration group: 19.2 versus 10.3 mg/day.

## Main efficacy results

Moving to the main efficacy endpoint, 21 patients were included in the efficacy assessment. These patients had received treatment for longer than four weeks and had urinary free cortisol results available before and after treatment.

The response rate to osilodrostat was 66.7%: six patients had a complete response and eight had a partial response. Among complete responders, the median time to normalization of urinary free cortisol was 10 weeks. When we compared combination therapy with monotherapy, the response rate was 80% in the combination group and 62.5% in the monotherapy group.

I would like to highlight that the response rate increased to 87.5% when we assessed patients treated with osilodrostat for longer than 12 weeks. Complete response was observed in 62.5%, partial response in 25%, and there was only one case of nonresponse.

## Predictors of response

We compared patients who responded to osilodrostat with those who did not. We found that the probability of response was higher when osilodrostat was used as second-, third-, or fourth-line therapy rather than as first-line therapy, with an odds ratio of 15.

It is important to note that first-line use of osilodrostat tended to be more common in patients with adrenocortical carcinoma than in those with benign adrenal disease: 68.8% versus 33.3%. The association between response to osilodrostat and non-first-line use disappeared after adjustment for adrenocortical carcinoma. Response tended to be higher in patients with benign disease than in those with adrenocortical carcinoma: 87.5% versus 53.9%.

We also found that patients with urinary free cortisol below five times the upper limit of normal before starting osilodrostat tended to have a higher response rate, with an odds ratio of 6.25, although statistical significance was not reached. In addition, patients with type 2 diabetes tended to have a higher response rate to osilodrostat than patients without diabetes: 81.8% versus 50%.

The table showed that the only significant predictor of response to osilodrostat was its use as non-first-line therapy, but we must remember that this association disappeared after adjustment for adrenocortical carcinoma.

It is also important to mention that the proportion of nonresponders tended to increase as the baseline urinary free cortisol category increased. The nonresponse rates were 14.3% among patients with urinary free cortisol below two times the upper limit of normal, 20% among patients with values between two and five times the upper limit of normal, and 55.6% among patients with values above five times the upper limit of normal.

## Cardiometabolic impact, safety, and follow-up

In relation to the impact of treatment on the comorbidity profile, most cardiovascular and metabolic parameters remained stable during treatment, but we observed significant decreases in systolic blood pressure and body weight after osilodrostat initiation.

In relation to safety, nine patients, or 32.1%, developed one or more adverse events potentially related to osilodrostat. In five of these patients, or 56% of those with adverse events, osilodrostat was discontinued for this reason. None of the adverse events was classified as severe. The most common adverse event was gastrointestinal intolerance.

The median follow-up time from diagnosis of Cushing syndrome to the last visit was 1.5 years. As expected, follow-up was longer in patients with benign disease than in those with adrenocortical carcinoma: 4.4 years versus 1.4 years.

At the last follow-up visit, 16 patients had died. The cause of death was tumor progression in nine patients, or 56.3%; complications of hypercortisolism in three patients, or 18.8%; and other or unknown causes in four patients, or 25%.

## Conclusions

The conclusion of our study is that osilodrostat effectively controls hypercortisolism in up to 67% of patients with adrenal Cushing syndrome treated for longer than four weeks and in 87.5% of patients treated for longer than 12 weeks, with a positive impact on blood pressure and body weight.

Patients who received osilodrostat after a previous steroidogenesis inhibitor had a higher probability of response to osilodrostat, but this association was probably related to the higher frequency of adrenocortical carcinoma among patients receiving first-line therapy.

That is all. Thank you very much for your attention, and please feel free to ask me anything.

## Question and answer session

### Weight loss in patients with adrenocortical carcinoma

**Moderator:** Thank you so much. We have time for a question. Go ahead.

**Audience member from Ascension in Wisconsin:** Hi, Marta. Brilliant talk. I am a little puzzled about why patients with adrenocortical carcinoma receiving osilodrostat lost more weight. Can you elaborate on the mechanism?

**Marta Araujo-Castro:** It could be due to tumor progression; that could be one reason. However, because we observed improvements in systolic blood pressure and body weight, the weight loss was probably a positive effect of controlling hypercortisolism.

Only 50% of the patients had obesity at the time Cushing syndrome was diagnosed. The patients who lost weight were generally those who had obesity, including patients with benign disease and those with adrenocortical carcinoma, rather than only patients with adrenocortical carcinoma.

**Audience member:** Is it not concerning that patients with adrenocortical carcinoma receiving osilodrostat will lose more weight? I am concerned about cachexia.

**Marta Araujo-Castro:** It is true that we can see weight loss as either a positive or a negative finding. If the weight loss is related to better control of hypercortisolism and a better prognosis, I would see it as positive. If you see weight loss in a patient without obesity, perhaps with malnutrition, that is concerning.

In this case, the loss of body weight was related to better control of Cushing syndrome, so I would see it as positive. We also saw improvement in systolic blood pressure, and most cardiometabolic parameters remained stable. When adrenocortical carcinoma progresses, all these parameters generally worsen. The drug controlled them, or at least prevented deterioration of the cardiometabolic parameters.

### Dose intensity and hypocortisolism

**Moderator:** Thank you. One quick question, and then we will end.

**Antoine Tabarin, Bordeaux, France:** You have a relatively low rate of adrenal insufficiency in your series compared with other series, and the doses used are also not that high. Do you think you could achieve a greater rate of control by increasing the dose of the drug?

**Marta Araujo-Castro:** Totally, yes. I think this is a very interesting question because the median maximum dose in our series was only 10 mg/day. If we compare this with other series, such as your series, higher doses were used. This probably explains both the higher rate of control and the higher rate of hypocortisolism. Our study had a very low rate of hypocortisolism, and I think that was related to the doses used.

Thank you.
