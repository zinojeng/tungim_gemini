### Introduction and biological motivation

**Moderator:** Last but not least, we have Daniel Hochbaum from Beth Israel talking about synchronizing body and brain: the coordination of metabolism and exploration by thyroid hormone. Thank you so much.

**Daniel Hochbaum:** Great. Thank you so much for having me. I'm excited to be here. Today I'll discuss our recent work, in which we find that thyroid hormone coordinates body-wide metabolism and exploratory behavior by remodeling cortical circuitry.

What motivated us to go down this path? First, large seasonal fluctuations in thyroid levels are present in many animal species in the wild, including primates. For example, gray mouse lemurs from Madagascar have a huge surge in thyroid hormone levels during their wet season, when nutritious foods are available. Thyroid hormone is a critical regulator of metabolism, and these animals increase their metabolic rate, with caloric intake increasing approximately four- to tenfold and oxygen consumption approximately doubling in the field observations shown. In addition, these small primates undergo dramatic changes in behavior. Males greatly increase their home-territory size in synchrony with thyroid levels, spending more time during the wet season awake, foraging for food, and searching for mates.

This shift does not come without risk. These exploratory behaviors leave animals more prone to predation, and the mortality rate in males increases dramatically during this season. These studies, and similar observations in other species, suggest that thyroid hormone could be involved not only in changing metabolic state but also in driving exploratory behavior. Many of the observed changes are sex-specific, implicating sexually dimorphic effects of thyroid hormone on behavior.

A second line of evidence is that thyroid hormone may alter cognition and behavior when dysregulated in human disease. Metabolism is driven to extremes, with weight gain common in hypothyroidism and weight loss common in hyperthyroidism. Individuals may also present with psychiatric symptoms ranging from depression to mania, which can be thought of as pathological extremes along an axis of exploratory behavior. With depression, a person may be unwilling to get out of bed and explore the environment. With mania, a person may take risks when it is not appropriate.

These observations led us to the idea that metabolic state and exploratory behavior may be co-regulated. When resources are available, it may be advantageous to increase energy consumption and explore more, sleep less, and take greater risks to gain access to food, mates, and other resources. Conversely, when resources are scarce, it may be advantageous to reduce metabolic expenditure while becoming more withdrawn, exploring less, and sleeping more. This may seem counterintuitive because hunger prompts us to look for food, but the effects under discussion operate over seasonal time scales. It is a biological imperative not to waste energy searching for food that will not be there; winter hibernation is an extreme example.

Our hypothesis was that thyroid hormone directly exerts parallel effects on peripheral organs and the brain to coordinate energy expenditure with appropriate exploratory behavior.

### T3 changes metabolism and exploratory behavior

We instituted a simple mouse model in which we bypassed endogenous thyroid-hormone regulation and treated animals with exogenous triiodothyronine, or T3. Over several days, we examined changes in RNA and protein levels, functional connectivity of neural circuits, and behavior. This let us study a defined change in thyroid state without relying on the endogenous feedback system to generate it.

In collaboration with Alex Banks at Beth Israel, we used indirect calorimetry to characterize energy expenditure. As expected, it increased after approximately two days of treatment. T3-treated animals also increased their food intake, a phenotype thought to be mediated by hypothalamic circuits, and moved around more. They were hyperactive relative to controls.

To obtain finer resolution on how behavior changed with thyroid state, we turned to a simple paradigm that mimics aspects of the field observations. In the light-dark box, half of the arena is sheltered and dark and the other half is exposed to bright light. Mice find the lit, exposed area naturally aversive and spend most of their time in the dark side. We asked whether manipulating thyroid-hormone levels alone could make the animals more exploratory in a perceived risky environment.

After approximately three days of treatment, we tracked movement in the box. T3-treated male mice spent a larger fraction of the session in the lit, exposed side. Because this assay is relatively high-throughput, we used it to test additional perturbations and generate mechanistic hypotheses.

We first asked whether the effect required direct thyroid-hormone activity in the brain. We used sobetirome, a thyroid-hormone analog that retains the ability to bind thyroid-hormone receptors but, at the concentration used here, does not effectively cross the blood-brain barrier. It therefore made peripheral organs hyperthyroid without effectively acting in the brain. Sobetirome reproduced some metabolic effects of elevated thyroid hormone but did not reshape the animal's tendency to explore the light-dark box.

We then treated a separate cohort with propylthiouracil, or PTU, which interferes with thyroid-hormone synthesis and lowers T3 levels. These animals spent less time in the exposed side, demonstrating that the behavior was sensitive to basal thyroid-hormone levels.

The results were independent of overall locomotion. We examined where the animals chose to spend their time rather than simply how much they moved. In this 10- to 20-minute paradigm, T3 did not affect distance traveled. The behavior emerged gradually with increasing T3 concentration, suggesting potential relevance to physiological fluctuations. Female T3-treated mice did not show the same change in this initial assay. That finding is consistent with observations across species implicating thyroid hormone in sexually dimorphic behavior. The rest of this talk focuses on male mice; understanding the sex difference is a future direction.

From these experiments, we concluded that thyroid hormone acting in the male brain can bidirectionally alter spontaneous exploratory-like behavior. They did not yet show where in the brain this action occurred or by what mechanism.

### T3-responsive transcription in frontal cortex

The behavioral changes required several days of elevated thyroid-hormone levels, suggesting long-lasting plasticity mediated by thyroid-hormone receptors. These receptors are ligand-gated transcription factors that regulate gene transcription when bound to T3. In the adult brain, they are expressed not only in the hypothalamus but also throughout the cortex, including areas involved in perception, evidence weighing, decision-making, and risk-taking. Their role in those cortical areas is unclear.

We performed single-cell RNA sequencing in the secondary motor cortex, or M2, a frontal-cortical region implicated in exploration, decision-making, and planning. Comparing control and T3-treated animals revealed tens to hundreds of differentially expressed genes across many cell types. Although I will focus on neurons, non-neuronal cells also showed substantial changes. Oligodendrocytes activated expected genes involved in differentiation and myelination, and astrocytes activated genes involved in forming and maintaining synapses.

Gene-set enrichment analysis of neuronal T3-induced genes showed strong enrichment for axon development and guidance among glutamatergic neurons, while synapse-related genes were enriched more broadly across glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons. Together, the axon-guidance programs in glutamatergic neurons, synaptic regulators in neurons and astrocytes, and promyelination factors in oligodendrocytes suggested widespread remodeling of cortical circuits.

These expression changes could, in principle, have been secondary consequences of thyroid hormone acting elsewhere. For example, thyroid hormone might bind receptors in the hypothalamus and cause secretion of a factor, or a change in activity, that subsequently affects cortical cells. We therefore asked whether the programs resulted from direct T3 binding to receptors in cortical neurons.

We used a thyroid-hormone receptor mutant described in a person with generalized thyroid-hormone resistance. The mutant receptor still binds DNA but cannot bind T3 and activate transcription. It acts as a dominant negative by competing for DNA-binding sites without activating transcription.

We generated adeno-associated viruses, or AAVs, that drove Cre-dependent expression of this dominant-negative thyroid-hormone receptor in neurons and co-injected a very dilute virus that drove Cre expression. The result was mosaic tissue in which neighboring neurons in the same circuit either expressed or lacked the dominant-negative receptor. Because the cells shared the same tissue environment, we could compare receptor-perturbed and unperturbed neurons of the same cell type within a circuit. We then performed single-cell RNA sequencing on the mosaic tissue, using Cre expression to identify cells carrying the dominant-negative receptor.

In cells expressing the dominant-negative receptor, genes normally upregulated by thyroid hormone were reduced, while genes normally downregulated by thyroid hormone were increased. The net effect was suppression of T3-induced transcriptional programs. The perturbation was highly specific: only about 1% of genes not identified as thyroid-hormone regulated were altered by the dominant-negative receptor.

To ensure that the effect arose from the mutant receptor's inability to bind T3 rather than from nonspecific receptor overexpression, we repeated the experiment with the wild-type receptor. Overexpression of the wild-type receptor largely did not perturb thyroid-regulated genes; those genes remained expressed at similar levels in cells with or without the wild-type construct. These data indicated that the cortical programs resulted from local T3 action on receptors in cortical neurons. The genetic tools then allowed us to test the functional role of those programs in selected cell types and brain regions.

### Thyroid-dependent transcription remodels cortical connectivity

Because axon- and synapse-related genes were induced, we asked whether cortical synaptic connectivity also changed. We targeted neurons that project axons from one hemisphere to the other. These neurons induce a T3 program containing many axon-guidance genes and have been implicated in decision-making processes relevant to exploration.

We expressed a channelrhodopsin in these neurons so that their axons could be activated optically. Blue-light stimulation allowed us to elicit and record synaptic currents in postsynaptic cells. Rather than infer connectivity from gene expression alone, this experiment directly measured the functional connection. Across a range of laser-stimulation intensities, T3 increased synaptic strength between cortical neurons.

We then expressed the dominant-negative thyroid-hormone receptor specifically in the presynaptic neurons whose axons were being stimulated. This was sufficient to occlude the effect of T3. We concluded that thyroid-dependent transcription alters cortical connections through presynaptic gene regulation.

### Two-armed bandit task and frontal-cortical necessity

Finally, we connected thyroid-dependent transcription and circuitry to behavior using a cognitive task that requires frontal cortex: the two-armed bandit task. A thirsty mouse chooses between two ports. On any given trial, one port is likely to deliver a water droplet and the other is not. After a variable block of trials, the reward probabilities switch. There is no cue that the environment has changed, so the mouse must explore both ports and flexibly accumulate evidence about the new reward structure.

Once the animals had learned the task, we treated them with T3. Elevated T3 improved performance, increasing reward rate over the treatment days. At the beginning of a new block, mice initially continued choosing the previously rewarding port and therefore had a low success rate. Nothing explicitly told them that the block had changed. Over subsequent trials, they had to infer from outcomes that the probabilities had switched. T3-treated animals explored the alternative port with less evidence and shifted more quickly to the newly rewarding port. This faster behavioral switching, rather than a simple cue response, accounted for the improved performance.

We next expressed the dominant-negative receptor only in frontal cortex, suppressing local T3-dependent transcription while leaving the rest of the body responsive. The perturbation was local: animals still developed the T3-associated increase in heart rate, demonstrating that the body remained in a hyperthyroid state. However, these mice no longer improved their two-armed bandit performance with T3 and did not show the T3-associated change in switching behavior. Thus, direct thyroid-hormone action in frontal cortex, activating transcriptional programs and inducing circuit plasticity, was required for the change in exploratory behavior.

Overall, thyroid hormone not only regulates metabolism throughout the body but also acts directly on cortical cell types. In neurons it activates axon- and synapse-related transcriptional programs, leading to functional changes in cortical circuits and altered exploratory tendencies.

### Conclusion

While thyroid hormone is a critical regulator of metabolism throughout the body, it also directly modifies cortical circuits to shift exploratory tendencies in synchrony with metabolic demands, driving individuals one way or the other along an axis of exploratory behavior.

Many people contributed to this work, including students in my laboratory, members of Bernardo Sabatini's laboratory, and our collaborators. Thank you, and I'm happy to take questions.
